I've seen 5 or so different variegated Sarracenia in my life (including the s. psittacina seedlings documented on this forum   
 edit: the jury is still out there on those seedlings)  , none of which came from my collection, but FINALLY...FIIIINAAAAAAAALYYYYY! (imagine me screaming that out loud).   If I had to make up a number, I've sown and sprouted easily over 100,000 sarracenia seeds in my life, and this is the very first one to finally...FINALLY!!!! produce the variegation mutation! well, that's not accurate: I've had many seedlings that were 100% variegated, but they didn't survive past a few weeks since they can't photosynthesize.  this is the very first seedling to be variegated and survive.  
  
So far, it's been slow growing, but given that it's variegated, that makes sense: there's less surface area to photosynthesize.  Oh, and one thing I learned about these is that they sunburn very easily.    Perhaps the strangest trait about this plant is that it's a chameleon: so far, it appears this plant can produce completely normal looking traps too!  It's very exciting that this mutation occurred on a minor giant, imagine this thing producing gigantic traps, can't wait to see what it looks like once it grows up!  Variegation seems to be about as rare as the anthocyanin free mutation, or perhaps even slightly more rare.  what I'm curious to see is if this trait can be passed down to the next generation.

A note, that a plant that fully lacks chlorophyll is not variegated; variegation is a mixture of normal and abnormal pigmentation, or a chimera of different pigments, so any variegated plant is 100% variegated. A pure white one is simply achlorophyllic.  
Biggest problem as osmosis noted is how rarely it's stable. Gold if it stays, but I wouldn't be shocked if this trait disappears over time.

The variegated trait can be outgrown. It doesn’t just disappear. If the plant goes completely green than normally you prune back the growth point to encourage dormant buds to grow which will hopefully still have achlorophyllic tissue mixed in. Sarracenia produce rhizomes though so pruning to encourage new growth points may be difficult.   
  
I would say the highest risk time that you may “lose” the trait is now till it becomes mature. Once mature it should be big enough to prune the rhizome in case the achlorophyllic tissue gets outgrown

Okay guys, this is some complicated stuff, so put down those 40's, stop hittin the whacky tabacky, and put on your thinkin caps cuz this may cause some sweatin from a hardcore mental exercise!  
  
The variegation in S. minor var. okefenokeensis appears to be stable but dynamic!  The original seedling doesn't appear to produce 100% variegated leaves, but it does consistently produce more variegated leaves....eventually!  This mutation is extremely strange: one leaf showed a lot of variegation, another leaf showed very subtle variegation, and the rest were completely non-variegated....but one side shoot is COMPLETELY variegated, with 100% of the leaves coming out mostly white!  I believe this side shoot might be completely stable: moreso than the original mother plant!    
  
With these new observations, I'm throwing out the pH hypothesis: I don't think pH is as relevant the production of variegated leaves in this clone.  This is what I think is happening: Imagine you have a log, and imagine on one side of that log, you draw a white line on the length of the log.  On that white line, there are nodes and petioles attached to the white line, and only nodes/petioles that happen to be exactly on that line will produce variegated leaves or completely variegated growth points!  For the sake of simplicity, I'll call the suspected length of rhizome that appears mutated "the white line."      
  
  
 I think the original mutation on this minor giant seedling only occurred on the length of one side of the rhizome!  Another hypothesis is that the leaves that are partially on the white line will produce partially variegated leaves, whereas those that are smack in the middle produce fully variegated leaves!  Same can be said with side apical meristems: ones that are smack in the middle of the white line are fully variegated!  
  
  
So why aren't all the new leaves variegated on this S. minor var. okefenokeensis seedling?  We have to think about how the leaves are arranged on the rhizome (phyllotaxy).  I don't know how Sarracenia phyllotaxy is classified, I flunked that part of college, LOL (ie. opposite, alternate, whorled, and spiral).  In the case of the minor giant seedling, they certainly aren't all on or touching the "white line" so they ain't all variegated!  
  
Maybe the original seedling isn't gold, but that one side shoot that produced all variegated leaves is what we're looking for!  Time will tell.....  
  
Are all Sarracenia variegation mutations like this?  No idea, but spoiler alert, I actually found a second variegated Sarracenia this year!  That's for another day though, writing this up was exhausting!
