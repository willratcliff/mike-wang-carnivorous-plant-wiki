I noticed there's another post regarding the AF rosea on this forum, but not positive if these are the same plants or if they have the same origin so here's a new post (I didn't know there was a clone from Liberty Co, FL until reading this post by KE: [sarracenia.proboards.com/thread/1743/rosea-luteola-liberty-flower-update](http://sarracenia.proboards.com/thread/1743/rosea-luteola-liberty-flower-update)  )     
  
So here's the story behind these plants photographed below: The late Bob Hanrahan found an AF rosea in his field, but it was stolen from the property.   Frustrated by the loss, he was determined to find one on his own the hard way.  He grew out 10K seeds from the surrounding area where the original plant was found in hopes of capturing a novel AF rosea, but regretfully, nothing showed up.  He changed his strategy and upped his game and grew approx. 100,000 seeds (to give you perspective, multiply my collection by 12.5 times and that's how many seeds he sprouted...easily a full greenhouse worth of seedlings!).  A few AF seedlings showed up, and the plants I have below are the result of the siblings being crossed with each other. Fortunately, these individuals are vigorous and fast growing:many of the AF roseas are slower growing due to inbreeding.  Fortunately, some turn out well like the one pictured below.  These may be F-2's, and when we get to F-3 and F-4, the probability of inbreeding depression is much higher.  
  
  
So that's the abridged, "remixed" story of S. rosea f. luteola Baldwin Co, AL pictured below.  Photos taken 8/14/15:

`post #9` · `id=23363` · `2015-11-02 19:51:28 UTC`

Thanks KE!    
  
Morpheus: it would actually be pretty easy to improve the AF rosea strain and keep it true to location by outcrossing an the original AF plant with a non-AF plant from the same field (not same county, more on that below) and then either back-cross the offspring with the original AF plant or do a sibling cross to get the recessive genes.  Only thing is this will take a pretty long time because you have to have 2 select, vigorous blooming sized plants, and it'll take 2 generations for the improved line to be realized.  At best, it's a 3-5 year project to produce the first generation, and another 3-5 years to produce the second generation, assuming you have blooming plants to work with this spring.  At worst, you'll acquire both parent plants (AF and non AF rosea from same population) this winter/spring, need 1-2 years to get them to blooming size, and then start the project.  That can take a maximum of 12 years to improve the line, so this is why you don't see so many people jumping on the opportunity....unless of course you're Mike Wang 

![:)](//storage.proboards.com/forum/images/smiley/smiley.png) [not mirrored]

  
  
 Baldwin Co, AL encompasses a lot of square miles (it's one of the bigger counties in AL), and within this county are several populations of rosea, many of which are genetically very different from each other.  Due to their differences, if you want to keep this rosea "true to location" I'd recommend crossing it with a vigorous, regular rosea from the field it came from, and then either do some back crossing or sibling crosses to get the AF genes to show up again.  It's hard to come by pure roseas that are verified from the same exact field, but I might be able to squeeze out some divisions this year 

![:)](//storage.proboards.com/forum/images/smiley/smiley.png) [not mirrored]

  
  
Of course, the easy way of going around this is to self the existing AF roseas, but it'll become more and more difficult to keep that line alive in the long run due to inbreeding depression.
