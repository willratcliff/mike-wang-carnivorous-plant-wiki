After discussing the topic of *Sphagnum* moss in another thread, I figured I'd post a little something about growing this wonderful moss.  This outstanding carnivorous plant companion grows in many carnivorous plant habitats across the planet, and there are various species within the genus *Sphagnum*.  It's also an incredible medium for many carnivorous plants, orchids, epiphytes, etc.     
  
I used to buy bales of dried *Sphagnum* moss every year to repot my Disa orchids (south african terrestrial orchid), but it's becoming more and more difficult to source good quality moss these days.  When I did get these bales, grasses and all sorts of other crap came with it.  What I've learned is this moss is actually a renewable resource, yet it is harvested commercially from the wild.  Anytime you buy this moss, chances are it was from the wild.   
  
 My hope is that this post will spark some of you to consider growing it instead of having it harvested from the wild.  I've heard from many friends that they dream of becoming a sphagnum farmer, and let me tell you this much: if you become one and are successful, you'll go down in the history books as being the first to pioneer it!  No doubt, the methods below can be applied to a commercial operation, and there is no such operation in existence as of today (11/20/15).  
  
Quality bales of *Sphagnum* go for more than $100 and have to be shipped half way across the globe to get to California (where I live), so I figured, what the heck, let me see if I can grow this stuff.  It was shocking to see how big of a yield you can get from growing your own moss.  Within a year, out of seven 10x20 trays, I harvested more than a wheel barrel full (more than a bale) worth of moss!  The crazy thing is, it doesn't even look like that much until you actually harvest it.  
  
How do you grow Sphagnum moss?  As mentioned earlier, I grow them in 10x20 trays.  The trays are 90% filled with old, dead sphagnum moss (or you could fill it with peat moss) and then every fall/early winter, with scissors, I cut the active growth points off my "mother moss plants" and place it in the trays.  It's kept very water-logged, and I fertilize with maxsea 1-2x per month.  Use slightly lower concentrations as you would for filling up pitchers of Sarracenia.  This moss likes as much light as possible, but it also wants to be kept cool.  
  
During the winter, the trays of moss are kept in full sun when it's nice and cold, and once the day temperatures get above 78F, they get morning sun/afternoon shade.  I rarely have to water them during the winter, but during the summer, they get overhead watered with low TDS water daily.  
  
Here's a photo of some Sphagnum moss, there are 7 trays total, and this is after a year's worth of growth:  

![](../../images/0b/0b157c7b8fbc62a3de4aaf5537c9b0f5f7cb7e5479d712319574815d667a3184.jpg)

  
  
Okay, here comes the shocker.  Would you have guessed that those 7 small trays of moss yielded more than a Wheel Barrel full?!  I have more harvested that isn't pictured below.  Imagine if you had an acre of this moss, and you harvested it annually...  Unlike much of the wild stuff, this is free of weeds, sticks, and other crap.  I could barely believe my eyes after harvesting this stuff, there was so much of it:  

![](../../images/c4/c43908bf2ab7b23eacc38b57383687a8f969edb07a7e76cd60d138c3d39ce711.jpg)

  
  
  
The tops were cut off and placed right back into the original trays.  I actually had enough "tops" to plant perhaps 40-50 trays, but space is an issue, so I went for 8 trays this time.  In  a year, this will look just like the first picture in this thread:  

![](../../images/f4/f45189e8ae89be91c8fcc5a423784d02906d844c52aa2bb810d49501ded00a6e.jpg)

  
  
  
I hope everyone goes out and does this so we place less pressure on wild *Sphagnum* bogs.  It takes up relatively little space, and produces an outstanding yield every year.  IF you grow 20-30 trays of this stuff every year, you'll have enough moss to grow a commercial quantity of orchids, epiphytes, carnivorous plants, etc.

Think of Sphagnum as a sponge: it'll absorb and hold whatever you put into it.  If you water with fertilizer water, that sponge will absorb both water and nutrients.   Every time you fertilize, more nutrients go into the sphagnum, so salts will slowly build up.  Even if you flood and drain a tray of sphagnum moss with purified water, you won't necessarily be getting rid of the higher fertilizer concentrations in the moss if you don't flush immediately....it's a little counter-intuitive.        
  
How do you get rid of excess fertilizer that's trapped in the moss?    First you flood the tray with DI water and let it sit for maybe 20-40 minutes.  You're creating a hypotonic solution and water will flow out of the cells of the sphagnum and into the puddle of water in the tray.    Then you drain and repeat...maybe 5-10 times?    
  
 This is why it's recommended to fertilize with caution to begin with.  No leaching needed.  Less work.  Less chance of over fertilizing and killing the whole thing.   It's hard to leach out salts because the sphagnum absorbs and holds onto it like a sponge.  Also, every time you leach, you're squeezing out not only the nutrients but also humid acid and other beneficial organic compounds that help buffer the soil.  For this same exact reason, it's difficult to leach out salty peat moss (well, even more difficult because it has a higher density, so less surface area is exposed to DI water when you try to flood/leach it).   
  
Katya: now to answer your question: you could probably do that, but it's a lot more work, and you still might get too much fertilizer concentrating in there over time.  With 1/6th concentration every week, that's 2/3 tsp fertilizer per month, versus a maximum of 1/2tsp per month with a 1/4 tsp every two weeks.  Again, with 1/6 tsp per week, you probably won't see any toxicity until many months later down the road, it'll take a while to build up.

Dozer: beautiful moss, and I'll give you a dollar to munch that shroom, hehe  
  
Calen: That's actually totally relevant.  80ppm is pretty high to begin with, but it's not the end of the world, especially now that you're in rainy Oregon and you can let nature leach out salts for you.  I don't know the exact answer to your question, but here's a story that might help lead you in the right direction: 2 years ago, I planted probably 30  leucophylla rhizomes in one of those home depot black mixing tubs with 225ppm peat moss.  I almost cried when the TDS meter read such a high number. I flooded the tray with 22ppm water, let it sit, and then  drained the salty water out 6-7 times, and the TDS finally went down to 43ppm.  YAY!?  Unfortunately, no.  Weeks later, I noticed there was salt build up on the soil surface and the plants didn't look that great.   I tested the peat, and it went back up to 170ppm.  WTF?    
  
A quick leach  might get rid of the salt already dissolved in the water/peat solution, but to get that salt out of the peat "cells" requires a lot of time and a lot of leaching!  
  
  
Did another 3 leaches in a row weeks later  and it was back down to about 40 something ppm.  Okay, back out of the red zone?  Still not, it went back up to 90ppm weeks later again!  This is when I gave up and just hoped for the best.  Quite a few plants rotted out, but after such a long struggle, I finally squeezed the salt out.  
  
Okay, 1 year and a full grow season later, I checked the TDS of the peat during the dormancy period.  It was in the 20's!!!!  Turns out, the best way to get rid of the salt is to have the Sarracenias eat it up!   Only catch is, if you start out too salty, they won't live long enough to lower the TDS and tell the story. They'll get diseases which spreads to the otherwise healthy plants.  They'll attract pests.  They'll slowly decline over time.  Repeat this same story with about 5 other community trays of various Sarracenia species to fully understand and appreciate good quality peat that is low in salts to begin with.  There's a reason I'm thinking about growing huge volumes of sphagnum moss

What's up Hunter! Yeah 160 is pushing it for sure! A few years back I used 140 ppm tap water for my water tables and things did okaaaay but the red flavas weren't red and I had some losses from rot. Not sure exactly what you mean by soaking vs rinsing? What you want to do is saturate the peat, let the water pick up the salts in the peat, then get the water out of there taking the salt with it. So just soaking won't help at all if the water and salts just stay in bog. Your bogs are raised planters IIRC so if there are drain holes anywhere you should be able to just run a bunch of water through them - flood and drain, flood and drain, flood and drain. If the drain water tests higher than in the input water then you are removing salts. The purer the water you flush with, the more salt you can remove per unit of water, and the lower you can get the salt levels overall. I've been lucky to have a bunch of 0 ppm rainwater to flush with and it's been super fast, but flushing with your 75 ppm tap water I'd think you could get the bog ppm down to about 75 fairly quickly as well. Good luck and keep us posted!

Pure 100% organic fertilizer will likely work, so long as they are acidic.  The challenge with using poo or other organic material is that it can lead to bacterial and fungal infections.  It's also difficult to source such organic waste, and it's most likely labor intensive to produce.  
  
Maxsea contains micro and macronutrients that may  require a lot of energy to produce (not very good for the environment).  However, the seaweed "waters down" the laboratory-made components, and when you apply it to Sphagnum or other CP's, a single container can last you many years. Overall, I don't think there's an overall net negative environmental impact using this product on CP's and Sphagnum.

After discussing the topic of *Sphagnum* moss in another thread, I figured I'd post a little something about growing this wonderful moss.  This outstanding carnivorous plant companion grows in many carnivorous plant habitats across the planet, and there are various species within the genus *Sphagnum*.  It's also an incredible medium for many carnivorous plants, orchids, epiphytes, etc.     
  
I used to buy bales of dried *Sphagnum* moss every year to repot my Disa orchids (south african terrestrial orchid), but it's becoming more and more difficult to source good quality moss these days.  When I did get these bales, grasses and all sorts of other crap came with it.  What I've learned is this moss is actually a renewable resource, yet it is harvested commercially from the wild.  Anytime you buy this moss, chances are it was from the wild.   
  
 My hope is that this post will spark some of you to consider growing it instead of having it harvested from the wild.  I've heard from many friends that they dream of becoming a sphagnum farmer, and let me tell you this much: if you become one and are successful, you'll go down in the history books as being the first to pioneer it!  No doubt, the methods below can be applied to a commercial operation, and there is no such operation in existence as of today (11/20/15).  
  
Quality bales of *Sphagnum* go for more than $100 and have to be shipped half way across the globe to get to California (where I live), so I figured, what the heck, let me see if I can grow this stuff.  It was shocking to see how big of a yield you can get from growing your own moss.  Within a year, out of seven 10x20 trays, I harvested more than a wheel barrel full (more than a bale) worth of moss!  The crazy thing is, it doesn't even look like that much until you actually harvest it.  
  
How do you grow Sphagnum moss?  As mentioned earlier, I grow them in 10x20 trays.  The trays are 90% filled with old, dead sphagnum moss (or you could fill it with peat moss) and then every fall/early winter, with scissors, I cut the active growth points off my "mother moss plants" and place it in the trays.  It's kept very water-logged, and I fertilize with maxsea 1-2x per month.  Use slightly lower concentrations as you would for filling up pitchers of Sarracenia.  This moss likes as much light as possible, but it also wants to be kept cool.  
  
During the winter, the trays of moss are kept in full sun when it's nice and cold, and once the day temperatures get above 78F, they get morning sun/afternoon shade.  I rarely have to water them during the winter, but during the summer, they get overhead watered with low TDS water daily.  
  
Here's a photo of some Sphagnum moss, there are 7 trays total, and this is after a year's worth of growth:  

![](../../images/0b/0b157c7b8fbc62a3de4aaf5537c9b0f5f7cb7e5479d712319574815d667a3184.jpg)

  
  
Okay, here comes the shocker.  Would you have guessed that those 7 small trays of moss yielded more than a Wheel Barrel full?!  I have more harvested that isn't pictured below.  Imagine if you had an acre of this moss, and you harvested it annually...  Unlike much of the wild stuff, this is free of weeds, sticks, and other crap.  I could barely believe my eyes after harvesting this stuff, there was so much of it:  

![](../../images/c4/c43908bf2ab7b23eacc38b57383687a8f969edb07a7e76cd60d138c3d39ce711.jpg)

![](../../images/f4/f45189e8ae89be91c8fcc5a423784d02906d844c52aa2bb810d49501ded00a6e.jpg)

  
  
  
I hope everyone goes out and does this so we place less pressure on wild *Sphagnum* bogs.  It takes up relatively little space, and produces an outstanding yield every year.  IF you grow 20-30 trays of this stuff every year, you'll have enough moss to grow a commercial quantity of orchids, epiphytes, carnivorous plants, etc.

great input!  Yes,  if you look at Sarracenia as an analogy, when the soil gets too loaded with nutrients, this leads to rot.  Similarly, Sphagnum burns with too high nutrient load and slows down in growth once it reaches a certain level of saltiness.    
  
  
One thing I've learned over the years is the importance of using sufficient "starts" to establish new trays.  These starts need to be free of weeds and other types of moss: if you start with dirty material, it becomes an uphill battle to clean it up later on.  
  
The amount of light that the sphagnum gets during growth affects the quality of the harvest.  full sun creates thicker strands that take longer to break down, whereas shade grown sphagnum produces thinner strands that decompose much quicker.  In my climate, it's too hot to grow sphagnum in full sun all year round, but by the coast, I bet you could produce really great quality moss because you can keep them in full sun all the time.

To recap, here's an overview of how to produce commercial quantities of sphagnum moss within a year.  stop buying that stuff from overseas or even within the US, they rip it out of the wild, and due to increased demand, an unknown amount of habitat is being destroyed as we speak!  
  
1) use a generous amount of starting material, don't skimp on the "starts."  
2) Get your moss established with great growth momentum before growing out large quantities.  
3) literally harvest starts by cutting off the top 1-2" of the moss.  Only select the best looking growth points, and make sure there's a minimum amount of brown moss (from underneath).  
4) let the starts recover for maybe 2 weeks before you think about fertilizing.  Fertilize too early and it can cause everything to crash.  
5) don't lose that growth momentum: poor starts or stressed moss  can set production back a year.  
6) use deionized water or water low in total dissolved solids like you would with carnivorous plants.  
7) Fertilize 1/4 strength maxsea (for acid loving plants), 1X a month, maybe 2X a month, depends on your weather.   These plants aren't very pickly about what kind of fertilizer to use.  Be careful, overfertilizing can cause the plants to take up to a year to recover!  
8) You want to see bright, neon green growth, that means the growth rate is rapid.  Dark green, or yellowish moss means very slow growth and/or unhappy sphagnum.  
9) full sun yields the best quality moss, but you have to balance that out with temperature.  In my climate, I can only grow this in full sun during the fall, winter, and part of spring.  When the air temps. are around 78F, they go into part shade and then when we have our 90F+ temps, the moss only gets morning sun.  
  
I'm still learning about drying sphagnum, and will share my findings probably sometime in the future.  So far, it seems it can take 3-4 weeks to dry in my climate unless you space the moss out very thin on racks.  If you dry the moss outside, you'll want to harvest when it's still warm, sunny, with no rain.

Yes, a few more ideas!  I have a detached garage that isn't insulated, so it gets baking hot during the day, and is super dry with low humidity!  This is probably even better than sun drying!  Keep the layers of moss very thin  if possible when drying, otherwise they will stay hydrated underneath and the fibers will break down quicker.  The top layer exposed to air will dry first, so after a week or so, flip it over so that the dry stuff is on the bottom and the hydrated stuff is on the top.  This helps speed up drying!  Once fully dried, I store it in recycled soil bags, seal them shut, and keep the in a cool, dark place.  Stuff can last decades this way!

I just received an inquiry about growing sphagnum commercially, so here are some updated observations!  
  
Strain selection is a huge consideration: I've grown about 30 different strains ranging from the eastern portion of the US all the way to Papua New Guinea and the only ones that have commercial potential in my climate are the two native strains from California (one from Mendocino County CA and the other from Del Norte Co, CA).  Other strains will grow strong and well for a while, but they will eventually die back drastically and take years to recover.  The California strains, under my climate, are more resilient and recover quicker from abiotic stressors.  The right strain will also produce relatively denser, higher quality "strands" while the wrong strain will only produce less fibrous(?), weaker strands under identical conditions.      
  
temperature-wise, sphagnum, even if it's from very hot areas like  Florida or Georgia, doesn't like both warm temperatures and full sun, it seems to thrive in full sun only when temperatures are near or below 26C or so.  You'll want to experiment with optimal temperatures versus growth rate, it's likely different depending on what strain you're cultivating.  Part shade is ideal when it's very hot, and full sun is ideal when it's on the cooler side.  During the summer, the heads can get "bleached out" from the heat, turning brownish, and this will greatly set the plant back, but despite looking dead, it's typically still alive, so long as they remain hydrated.  Even if they dry out, it's possible that the "heads" are still alive!   I've had dried sphagnum kept in a bag for over a year rehydrated and the heads started growing again!     However, if you're trying to grow this plant commercially, you don't ever want it to slow down, you want it to continuously grow.  When the heads are brown, there isn't much you can do to get growth momentum back until they start to green up again once conditions become favorable.  
  
You could certainly push Sphagnum under LED's or artificial lighting and hypothetically get larger, much quicker yields than growing under natural light, but given the relatively slow growth rate and biomass accumulation, if you are able to reach a net increase, production will likely have to be on a large scale.   The cost of energy is the greatest determining factor as to whether or not the LE the profit margin will be significantly lower compared to using natural light.   Without a doubt, 24/7 photoperiod is recommended for the quickest, largest yields.  
  
Light intensity greatly affects the quality of sphagnum and honestly, I'm now starting to wonder if slower growth rates result in denser, higher quality "strands" or if it's only directly related to light intensity.    under lights, you get massive biomass accumulation quickly, and even with powerful lights kept very close to the grow points, the harvested end product doesn't seem as durable compared to sphagnum grown in full sun.  In other words, if you compare say new zealand sphagnum moss    
  
While I don't know if photoperiod has anything to do with sporulation, what I do know is the plant will only sporulate when it has slowed down growth and run out of space/nutrients.  if you ever see your plants producing spores, this means growth isn't optimal: sphagnum will bypass reproduction when it is growing at maximum "speed" at all times.  
  
I'm still a little bit unsure of what the best substrate is, but in the past, a 6-7cm layer of dead sphagnum at the bottom has produced the best results.  That said, just like Sarracenia,  I think a wide range of substrates  will work well so long as you care for it right!
