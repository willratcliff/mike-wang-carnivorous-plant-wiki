I would **strongly, strongly **caution against any hope whatsoever of involving universities in tissue culture/patent/propagation issues. All of them have strict intellectual property policies (i.e. not only will they say no, but provided they did say yes and help you at all, they'd basically have a strong intellectual property claim on your cultivar/patent).  
  
The lab equipment is prohibitively expensive to set up yourself; an 8-grand surcharge, even though it's definitely ridiculous, is pocket change compared to the cost of tissue culture, not only in the startup costs of the equipment itself, but also the continual expense of chemicals and media.  
  
I have personally approached the founder/owner of one of the largest plant breeding companies in Georgia about TC and distribution of Sarracenia. He flat-out turned me down without even a second thought. Said they'd tried it, didn't work, that the plants weren't reliably growable or shippable, and that the mass market interest and consumer education/knowledge wasn't there.  
  
Now, being the kind of person I happen to be, I'll be spending the rest of my life trying to prove him wrong. I considered those statements somewhat of a slap in the face . My personal experience has been that introducing people to CPs is relatively easy. Obviously they have a higher learning curve than your average houseplant or even landscape plant, but they're just so damn cool!  
  
 Once you bypass the hurdle of fear--fear of not succeeding--people **want **Sarracenia. They care about what's happening to them in situ. They follow the basic guidelines and get their plants past the first year. They learn stuff they never knew before! Half the time, folks didn't even know CPs existed. How can you desire a group of plants you don't even know is out there waiting for you??

I've also heard word, because I am in an orchid society and know quite a few orchid folks, that some of the TC labs out there have some dubious practices as well. Obviously if you contract with a TC lab, you'll want everything in writing so that you have a legal basis for everything.   
  
Sometimes, though, it's done through the old-fashioned "gentlemens' agreement." In cases like that, and keep in mind this is probably rare, the lab can turn around and sell your plants on the side, or distribute them to other wholesale growers for a royalty, and all kinds of backstabbing stuff like that. Not so much if you own a plant patent (good luck with the expense of getting one btw), but if they get ahold of nonpatented clones there is nothing in the world to protect you unless you have a contractual agreement.  
  
So I guess my message at the end of the day is this: as a breeder, you're creating unique genetic material that you need to protect at all costs, regardless of whom you're dealing with.

#### *hcarlton*
`post #28` · `id=21924` · `2015-09-21 00:30:01 UTC`

Endosymbiosis means peeling off the outer surface isn't going to do anything. The fungi (typically micorrhizal fungi) extend through the cells of the plant, often deep into tissue. Most are beneficial, but would run rampant in TC conditions. It's why starting with seed is preferred; no fungi are present until the plant starts growing.
