Here's a very cool and common Azalea that grows throughout Northern California.  Interestingly enough, not many people grow it in their yards, at least not around here, although my friends have wild plants all over their properties.  I really wanted to try growing some of these, so I selected seeds from some gigantic plants that were COVERED in flowers!  
  
They tend to grow high up in the mountains or by the coast.  I've seen them in full sun to part shade, in gravel based/poor quality soil.  Maybe they rely on mycorrizae to get nutrients in the wild?  I have no clue how they do so well.  They're also found growing in sandy soil with a nice organic layer of decomposed leaves on top, kind of what you'd find in a redwood forest.       
  
Here are some pictures of the plants growing in the wild.  These are really fragrant and absolutely jaw dropping to see in person.  This is Mendocino County, CA:

![](../../images/a2/a298b4358008e914cc058b653994970aa9c911987e675adf1a4d9a4f351c0d97.jpg)

  
  
  
I sowed some fresh seeds on 100% peat moss and placed them under grow lights, humidity was kept high.  Temperatures are cold, no heating.  About 2 weeks later, they germinated!  It was shocking how small the plants are when they sprout, they're almost microscopic, but as soon as they produce their first true leaves, they start taking off!  In a week or two, I need to space these out!

The seedlings are growing very strong, while it's tempting to keep them all, I've been slowly roguing out the runts and smaller plants and only keeping the biggest.  If you think about the size of each plant at maturity, how many can you really fit at your home?  Many of my neighbors are excited to give these a try at their home once they're big enough to go into the ground, but there is still quite a ways to go.    
  
These are growing under lights in pure peat moss.  They have a very shallow root system that is fine and fiberous, what I'm learning is that they can only take about 1/2 strength fertilizer (maxsea).  Anything stronger will burn them as I learned the hard way!  That said, the mistake isn't  noticable:

I visited my friend's ranch recently in Mendocino County CA to spend time with friends and enjoy a weekend getaway.  It was a work weekend but the fun type: our job was to help dig up the septic system and put in a new one!  We also were taking down a bunch of shrubs and managing the property to make sure it was in tip top shape for the natives to thrive.   
  
The tiny deer ticks were especially bad: walking 50 feet in the field, I found 7 of them on me!  During that short weekend,  4 bit me and I pulled off over 30 from my cloths!  
  
Thing is, in order to see the full diversity of Rhododendrons on their property, you really have to bush whack and go deep into the thickness.  I know the family well because one of the owners is one of my best friends, and his parents/brother/their family in general are family to me.      I showed them pictures of  all the photos below (they are hardcore plant people/scientists BTW, met my friend in a botany class at UC Davis) and they were blown away!  Some didn't even realize there was this much diversity on their property!  
  
I scoured nearby properties and while there were also Rhododendrons around there, no other property had such a robust, thriving population: all of the other properties were not managed with preservation of the native habitat in mind.  The ranch, on the other hand,  has strictly been managed to preserve the native populations.  One of the family members just recently in the past year or two started their own business managing  land with the goal of protect native flora and fauna for the long haul.  In fact the entire family has this mindset and they have held the property for 100 years now (this is the 100th year the property has been in the family!).  This property is a prime example of what happens when a parcel is managed the right way!  
  
Here is a Lilium maritimum (endangered lily) habitat also peppered with some Rhododendrons.  I spent that weekend cutting out encroaching shrubs to keep the habitat open.  We are also going to experiment small scale with pruning some of the native grasses to see if that helps the lily population expand: anecdotal evidence suggests these habitats need clearing out like Sarracenia habitat.  This spot almost reminds me of the sphagnum site where darlingtonia live, except this site only has seasonal water running through the field.  Look carefully for the orange flower in the field, that's the maritime lily!
