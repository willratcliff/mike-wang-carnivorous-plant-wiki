In a previous thread, we were discussing issues with thrips: how can you tell that a deformed looking pitcher or damaged trap came from thrips and not something else?  Well, it's not always easy.  
  
First off, let's discuss how we get thrips on our plants in the first place.  Where do thrips come from?   Here in California, we have the native Western flower thrips, although I suspect multiple variants of thrips are found pretty much everywhere in the US.  The following picture is a western flower thrips, and credit for the photo goes to: [cues.cfans.umn.edu/old/inter/inmine/Thripm.html](http://cues.cfans.umn.edu/old/inter/inmine/Thripm.html)   
  

![](http://cues.cfans.umn.edu/old/inter/inmine/inimage/wft.jpg) [broken]

  
  
  
  
This particular species is visible to the eye, but BARELY!  Notice the little bastard has wings!  When it's windy, these insects can jump and catch a ride in the wind, which is their usual mode of spreading from one patch to another.  Here in California, thrips aren't a problem outdoors until about mid to late summer, when all the local vegetation has died off, and the only thing green in sight is that fat patch of S. flava rugelii's, or your beloved Sarracenia bog.  I suspect in areas where it's semi-tropical, like the Southeast, thrips become a problem when temps warm up.   
  
 With outdoor plants, no matter how well we spray them, even if we kill every last thrip in the collection, a light breeze will bring new ones in...eventually.  However, if we don't keep them in check, they'll form large colonies and discolor your traps.    
  
Well, there's another species of thrips that hangs out on the indoor plants, and these are yellowish and very tiny.  I don't know what type they are, but they're a SOB! Here's some photos of thrips damage on indoor plants.  Notice the slight brownish discoloration, which can be mistaken for nutrient toxicity.  In fact, the only way I know for 100% that this isn't due to soil toxicity is because I physically can see the little Effers on my plants, but they're sneaky and hide when you go looking for them:

When it comes to organic oils, they all work, but you physically have to smother the insect with the oil, which suffocates them.  You also have to come back with multiple applications to get the eggs that hatch later on.  Oils are a lot less effective than chemicals like spinosad, acephate, or imidacloprid, many of which don't require physical contact with the insect to kill them.  Another downside with oils is that they magnify light, which means you could get leaf burn on very sunny days.  I only use the "hippy oils" indoors (don't want to be in an enclosed area with dangerous pesticides), can't remember exactly the brand but it was intended for fruit trees.

Thrips are indeed quite the little buggers. I fight them every couple weeks at the greenhouse, including suiting up in a tyvek suit complete with respirator and nasty chemicals.  
  
I have also experienced a number of severe thrips-related incidents. Since they live on and eat flowers, and fly, they can carry pollen and make pollinations. Got through flower bags and induced fruit set on one of our research crops. It was surprising, amazing, but totally detrimental to our intentions because all the crosses were thrown off.  
  
Another horror story. Thrips vector plant viruses like INSV and TSWV. Two years ago I detected tomato spotted wilt virus in a $15 grand research project. TNSV is a generalist virus and was also capable of infecting hundreds of other genera of plants. One thrip bite is enough! It took months of coordination with 6 different professors, sprays out the wazoo, and was by far the worst summer I have ever had. Came out on top, though. Throw away infected plants, spray the bugs. Rinse, repeat.  
  
I use yellow and blue sticky traps for detection, and usually prefer systemics for control. Organophosphate and especially neonics are best because they will get deep into flowers and pollen--i.e., the feeding area. Contact insecticides like oils, and even spinosad because it can't penetrate deep into the nooks and crannies of most non-CP flowers, are nowhere near as effective. You wind up repeatedly spraying without achieving control.  
  
Oils are actually the most risky and phytotoxic things I've used. Since they clog stomata, on hot days they'll cause leaf burn more easily than water-soluble pesticides. I can't say they give good control either. I like a once and done approach, as well as the residual protection of a systemic.

+1 It depends on the water solubility of your systemic, and neonics and organophosphates are super-high.  
  
And also, there is no such thing as a systemic miticide. Some will absorb into foliage to get to the feeding zone of mites, but no fully systemic chemicals exist. This is referred to as "translaminar" instead. Since mites feed directly from the phloem and not xylem, they have access to the plant's sugars right as they are manufactured within the chloroplasts. By harvesting plant juices directly from their source, they avoid most systemic pesticides, which don't have time or mobility enough to diffuse to the feeding zone. This is especially true if the chemicals are drench-applied, and uptaken by the roots and transported all the way up through the xylem. So, the translaminar chemicals tend to make a lot more sense.   
  
It's really quite brilliant that the mighty mities have a natural feeding strategy that creates a sort of resistance to chemical control. I kill them all the time, but greatly admire them from an evolutionary standpoint.  
  
A fully systemic miticide is sort of considered the holy grail by a lot of pesticide companies.  
  
And sorry to derail the thread to another infamous pest. Perhaps later we can get something together on all the predominant pest issues of Sarrs.  
  
And also, a disclaimer: I really don't like killing things, and especially hate pesticides. In general, I prefer preventative action (sanitation, good nutrition, supporting predators, etc.) for pest control. I feel like I get enough exposure at work that I won't go near anything chemical at home. Since I'm required to do so for my career, however, I try to become really, really good at wielding pesticides so that each application gets the best possible kill, thereby minimizing how many treatments I have to make. Knowing how each chemical works, developing a rotation, applying at the right point in the lifecycle, tossing or cutting back foliage area, and a lot of other choices will actually be just as or more effective than blatantly spraying. In some cases I've even corrected nutritional issues and induced certain plants to produce secondary metabolites that killed pests without me lifting a finger.  
  
These things can and do cause severe and unintended consequences out in the natural world (i.e. colony collapse). It's quite unfortunate.  
  
Anyway, that's my philosophy in general. Ounce of prevention.
