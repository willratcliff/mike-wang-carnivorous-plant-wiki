Here's a really nice clone of Cephalotus follicularis that originates from Northcliffe in Western Australia.  It is extremely rare in the United States and pretty much impossible to acquire here despite a few growers for sure having it ( I had to get this one imported).    This particular clone originates from wild seed originally distributed by Brad Taylor/John Yates  It's vigorous, showy, produces large traps and is quite colorful.  The plant hasn't yet reached vegetative maturity here so it's too early to say if this is a giant or not, but the trap size is for sure impressive given the relatively small size of the plants.  It has moderate tolerance to powdery mildew (PM): it's not the most susceptible but will get jacked up by the fungus if the air is stagnant and the conditions are ripe for the disease.  That said, it's not a PM magnet like some other clones.   
  
  There are several different clones from Northcliffe (and unfortunately a bunch of selfed seedlings which will likely cause confusion down the road) in cultivation although I think this one is likely different from whatever is floating around in Europe.  Just like at the coalmine beach site, some individuals from NOrthcliffe are superior to others and this seed grown clone so far is the very best that I've ever seen from this site.  If you can find a picture of a different clone  from this location that has better characteristics, I'd love to see it!   
  
Anyways, back to the topic of colorful: there are only 2 clones in the collection right now that exhibit these BLACK TEETH: this clone and Denbarker 3!  All other cephalotus clones grown under identical conditions aren't showing black teeth but my guess is that many can with a serious artificial suntan.  The results in my collection suggest this clone's teeth blacken up relatively easily!  I guess this is one of the few cases where black teeth are attractive LOL  Disclaimer:  I wouldn't recommend anyone not brushing their teeth for long periods of time as a fashion statement.

I divided my mother plant last winter and it wasn't even half the size of the plant you see now!  IT had zero roots and barely any stem tuber, but with a lot of babying and a good amount of adult pitchers to feed from the getgo, I've been able to pump this baby up like Arnold S:  

![](../../images/00/00a3c97752bfcb31541577b7711fd757b8d11690fc7e8c99d66852ed45483dac.jpg)

  
  
The downside to fast growth, at least in my care and conditions, is increased sudden cephalotus death syndrome, so I have since toned down the feeding and am trying to get a moderate but good growth rate going.  slow and steady wins the race with these plants:
