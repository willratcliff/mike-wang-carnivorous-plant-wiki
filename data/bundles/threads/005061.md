With Covid rampant right now, as well as food prices going up significantly this year (at least in Northern California) lots of us have resorted to supplementing our own food supply via growing our own.  Less time in public=safest option.    It's nice to not have to go to the grocery store as often for fresh produce, and it's hard to beat the flavor of organically grown crops that aren't sprayed with crazy chemicals that shrink your you know what!  Your brain of course!  Sheesh.  
  
Growing your own food on a significant scale, especially around here, comes with challenges: squirrels, gophers, voles, moles, raccoons, rabbits, deer, rats, mice,  and in my case pill bugs!  Yes, who would have thought pill bugs would DEVASTATE all of my root crops which are directly sown in the ground?!  In order to succeed, we have to grow starts and transplant in pots before planting them in the ground.  Anything that goes directly in the ground gets eaten before it has a chance to grow!  For this reason, we can't grow root crops here until an organic solution to the  pill bug situation is figured out.  Maybe we'll solarize the soil in the summer (cover with black plastic and cook everything that way)

Your greens look so nice! The cabbage butterflies leave caterpillars that turn our kale and bok choi into swiss cheese and once in a while make it all the way onto your PLATE before you find them. I think I see some dill seedlings in one of your pictures- I love that stuff because the black swallowtail butterflies lay eggs on them. I don't have the heart to hoe it down when it's feeding their caterpillars. If you poke them, their orange antennae pop out to scare you off, lol.   
  
Pill bugs devouring your garden? That is just nuts! I wonder if it's a california thing or if your yard is an isopod perfect storm. I bet diatomaceous earth would nab them, but it would also nab every other thing with an exoskeleton that touched it, so maybe not great.  
This is pretty fiddly and very small scale, but I hear you can use potato halves as traps for pill bugs. You cut the potato in half the long way and carve out a hollow in the flesh side of each piece. Then you nestle your bowl-shaped potatoes peel-side-up in your garden soil. The potato-cave will draw in pill bugs to eat the potato and use the cave as a humid shelter during the day, and each day you can pick it up and shake off the bugs into a plastic dish. Then you can toss them into your neighbor's yard, feed them to the toads under your yardlight, or give them to your kids to keep as pets. I'm not kidding either, I recently caught some to use to keep my tree frog's tank clean and they're an amusing piece of cake to care for. Mine are only brown or bluish, but I bet where you live you might find some orange or yellow-spotted ones!   
  
My family grew a big garden this year even though we had wanted to downsize. A lot of the varieties we love have to be ordered from seed and then started indoors under lights, so I feel really lucky that that meant I had everything ordered by February. We grow too many things to bother listing, but one of my new favorites is the Round Mauve variety of eggplant. Love it, even the unidentified plant virus that popped up in our garden last year couldn't keep this eggplant down! And this year I grew the Blacktail Mountain watermelon for the first time. Along with the Crimson Sweet, it is now one of only two watermelons we can grow with any success in Nebraska. It takes up a weirdly small amount of space and the melons are tiny! But they actually get sweet, even in places with cool temps and a short season. We grew Amish cantaloupes which unexpectedly produced so well that we were struggling to find enough people to give them away to, until something discovered the patch and ate one or two whole melons every night! I think it was a deer based on the teeth marks. We also like Jimmy Nardello frying-peppers and some mildly hot ones like Pasilla Bajio, and Time Bomb and poblanos for stuffing.

My garden takes inspiration from the Native Americans. They would plant corn, beans, and squash together. The corn I grew this year was 'glass gem' popcorn. I took a course in the Native American/ early Spanish history in Florida and Georgia a while back. The professor said you could identify if a Native American site developed corn agriculture based on their tooth decay - so brush you teeth!  

  
  

![ ](../../images/3e/3e80bf903e1f09dabfbc361c7a00e38f93616621855ef5c578dd82d38fa362e8.jpg)

  
  
The squash is a Seminole Pumpkin. It has a thick rind and is one of the only varieties I've found to grow in the humid Southeast. Grows just as well in CA! The Spanish explorer  Pánfilo de Narváez noted that the Apalachee grew these way back in 1528. Mike pointed out that these would grow up in trees so it must have been a sight to see! It's a shame they're not more common because they are so prolific and they're thick rind allows excellent storage. I still have one that's a year old. These five are leftovers from this year's crop after 4 pumpkin pies, a jack-o-lantern, giving a couple away, and a pumpkin thief stealing one!  

  

![ ](../../images/e2/e2556f1b5eb01f4dc58afc548b41afccf4a4250fd34eabacf4696014ea1b7134.jpg)

  
  
Pumpkin flowers are edible and make a treat when pan fried. Male flowers are best.  
  
The beans also did well and are easily dried for later, just not nearly as photogenic! I had no luck with root vegetables for the same pest reason - with the exception of ginger and horseradish. The moles certainly avoid those!  
  
I'm trying a cultivated variety of purslane (*Portulaca oleracea var. sativa*)  and Weywi Cupeli amaranth (*Amaranthus cruentus*) next growing season. I have to be careful with what greens I eat because oxalates cause kidney stones, but Amaranth greens have a safe level. Plus Amaranth grain is very nutritious.  
  
  
Finally, I guess technically a vegetable is sugar cane. It's an old tradition in the southeast to boil the juice from the cane for syrup. The syrup has a distinct taste; kinda like a thinner molasses. It's mainly eaten with biscuits and sausage. My family's made syrup around Thanksgiving since at least my grandfather's grandparents. Here's a photo of the 80 gallon kettle in full boil a couple years ago. I'll miss it this year because of Covid, but some family members are having a social distancing version tomorrow.  My family's cane patch in GA is right next to a swamp with *U. radiata. *Had to bring in carnivorous plants somewhere.  
  

![ ](../../images/31/31cf87a4d6670fb854de97429d4e0957225d089d383d87eafd55e3940c5bd9ff.jpg)

  
  
Even without access to a kettle in CA it is still nice to grow cane varieties that are over a century old. The stems can be eaten or used to sweeten a dish in cooking. Just as a side note, people in the Appalachians have a similar tradition with Sorghum.

Spent most of April planting some Chinese vegetables, but until they've grown out a little, what's in season right now is artichokes! A very easy perennial here in the SF Bay Area, I have three original plants grown from seeds about 4 years ago. These guys sprout up from the roots every year in the middle of our winter rains and don't need any extra irrigation to get decent artichokes once they're established. The flowers attract bees, butterflies, and hummingbirds if you leave a few buds on the plants to bloom. The seed heads are very prickly and have surprisingly few seeds in them, although we started getting volunteer seedlings this year. The plants die back through the hot summer and the dried stems are easy to pull by hand in the fall.  
  
[https://flic.kr/p/2kWFGx3](https://flic.kr/p/2kWFGx3)Our green artichoke. This one looks like the typical green globe artichokes you can get in a supermarket. The plant likes to sucker and has artichokes from mid-April through May.  
  
[https://flic.kr/p/2kWJvuS](https://flic.kr/p/2kWJvuS)  
This purple artichoke is my personal favorite. The plant doesn't sucker much at all and sets buds a few weeks before the green one. The texture after cooking is a little more solid and it tastes earthier than the green, which I really like. Once the artichoke flower buds start emerging, I blast them with water on occasion to keep the earwigs and aphids out. It's a little harder to clean the purple buds because the bracts can't be pulled apart with your bare hands - each bract has a prominent thorn unlike the green one.  
  
[https://flic.kr/p/2kWB5Vd](https://flic.kr/p/2kWB5Vd)  
These are tertiary artichokes, so they're about half the size of the primary and secondary ones, which have already been cut. I've seen artichoke plants get quite a bit taller than ours (more water? actual fertilizer?) but I like that these stay at a convenient height for harvesting and don't take up too much space.  
  
[https://flic.kr/p/2iLhm8s](https://flic.kr/p/2iLhm8s)  
The purple color was muted this year compared to last year's.  
  
[https://flic.kr/p/2kWB5Ye](https://flic.kr/p/2kWB5Ye)  
Artichokes are ready to cut once the bracts are slightly open; they're still edible later, but the size of the heart shrinks and everything's more fibrous. Leave a long stem on them - makes a good handle so you can dunk them underwater to get insects and dust out, and at this stage, the stem is as good as the heart. We don't do anything fancy with them: just snip off the ends of the bracts, steam for around 10 minutes (until a knife goes through the stem easily), and eat with a little mayonnaise or melted butter to cut the bitter notes.  
  
All that said, these guys are super easy to grow around here (they are a thistle, after all!) - zero maintenance other than blasting the buds with water because earwigs will chew some meat off the bracts if you leave them alone, and removing old leaves and stems once they're dry. Some patience is required though since seedlings usually don't produce any buds the first year.

did some trials on various purple broccoli varieties.  Most of the pics in this post are from 3/27/22. Normally, at least in my climate,  you're supposed to plant them end of July thru August, and then if you want to try a spring crop, you can start them indoors in February.    Some purple broccoli require vernalization, or several weeks of very cold weather to produce heads.  The newer varieties can produce normal sized heads and most don't require vernalization. I sowed most of the seeds in   December 2021, which is actually not a good time to plant them, but they still worked!    
  
 What I'm starting to learn is that open pollinated seeds have high variability and low freqencies of high yielding individuals.  There are a few winners in most of the batches though, so you can pheno hunt and improve them.    On the other hand, the F1 hybrids are super productive, mostly uniform, and can produce decent sized heads.  
   
  
Check out the size of the early purple sprouting broccoli plant.  You're not supposed to give them much nitrogen, otherwise they grow large plants with very small heads.   this was grown organically and given low Nitrogen, high P and high K.    This is from open pollinated seeds:  

![](../../images/96/960f643cf6e4adbb06856e7bfc1139f731104c4baef39fc3529825b0a29a8e4d.jpg)

These aren't bred for large heads, but rather a ton of small side shoots.  There was so much variation in this seed batch, and a lot of the plants are less than exceptional.  These seeds were 22 years old, they can last a very long time!

Strawberries are starting to wake up!  I have one bed of june bearing strawberries just for fun, they were started last May and this winter, I cut them all to the ground.  June Bearers need that winter dormancy to produce their one flush of fruit, which usually happens around June (this is different than day neutral strawberries, which flower and produce fruit as long as the weather is favorable.  They stop producing from about late November until about late March/early april for me).    
  
  On the left is Mary's Peak (which we call Mary's Pizzle), a relatively new, patented variety that was released in 2018.  I think I read somewhere it's the "replacement" for hood strawberries.   The row on the  right is the famous Hood Strawberry (released in 1965).  Mary's peak is super vigorous, seems quite disease resistant, and is overall easy to grow whereas hood is notorious for being susceptible to diseases, especially viruses, and is overall more challenging to cultivate.  That said, hood is known for its flavor....I guess we'll see about that!  

![](../../images/aa/aab8802bd39ff21c68dd510e1a41aadddd1baf9f63f2288846e06f47c711e924.jpg)
