All so true!  Here's true confessions of a longtime grower an cp nursery owner; The Venus Flytrap is my least favorite carnivorous plant.  It's why I love a forum devoted to Sarracenia.  There, I said it.  Just like Mike said they can be so amazing, and some do well in particular situations and others don't, but in general they are not hard to grow.  I also 100% agree they are the reason most folks get into carnivorous plant in the first place.  Having said all that, here's what puts them at the bottom of my "favorites" list.  
  
1.  Although not hard to grow, they are just fussy enough that first time growers usually kill them.  Their biggest downfall seems to be that they are not forgiving of common mistakes with cp.  Sarracenia will hang on quite awhile if fertilized or watered with hard water, or kept in low-light.  Not flytraps.  
  
2.  Mass production by big nurseries over the years combined with ultra-sh@#ty growing directions have created a mythos about how to grow them that is so deeply ingrained in the public psyche that even when people hear correct information, they often reject it.  This is slowly changing thanks to people like yourselves, but it's still very alive and well.  
  
3.  When people do finally accept correct cultural information, they are often disappointed, since they want this perfect little plant that can be their pet that sits in the middle of a room, catch flies, and not need any particular care.  After all, it's really a lizard sticking out of a pot, and not really a plant isn't it? 

![:)](//storage.proboards.com/forum/images/smiley/smiley.png) [not mirrored]

  What's all this messing around with sun, peat moss, distilled water and seasons.  It just should need bugs, right?  
  
4.  When I sell them in public I have to keep them behind glass cases or their won't be a single open trap at the end of a day.  Demonstrations don't increase sales, it just makes people gasp and giggle, then move on to buy an Elephant ear.  Even when they do decide to buy one, they don't want the one they just poked, they want one with all the open traps.  Evidently some poor sucker is supposed to by the one they just jabbed.    
  
All of these issues are either much less, or non-existent with the other cp species.  But alas, they comprise about 25-30% of our sales, so "Feed Me Seymore!".

Thanks guys!  
  
Jeff:great comments, and I'm glad you put all that on the table.  I love how marketing gets their word out, which maximizes a company's profits in the short run but completely destroys the product in the long run.  Thankfully, there are a quite a few better educated vendors such as yourself who are starting to reach out to the general public and change the way people look at these plants.  
  
 I have also grown venus fly traps commercially, and it always surprised me how we literally grew half an acre of a really crappy clone that I struggle to keep alive outside a greenhouse.  It wasn't very fast growing, was sensitive to salts in the water and commonly rotted out, and it only grew well under absolutely perfect conditions (and we had perfect conditions).  On the other hand, we grew one other clone that was absolutely amazing: it produced gigantic traps, was super fast growing and they never produced those tall summer petioles, they were always short and squat.  The interior of the trap was the same dark purple color as akai ryu but the rest of the plant was green.  I completely regret not keeping a clone of this plant, and am curious how it would perform outdoors.  On a random side note, there were so many venus fly traps that when you walk into the greenhouse, you could smell the sweet nectar.    
  
  
All that aside, on with the tour de clones! During my college years, one of my best friends, Dr. William Ratcliff aka Will, grew out some seeds that he purchased from Orton's plantation in the Carolinas (I think he bought these around 2002 or 2003). We suspect these were wild seeds collected from private property, as they were only sold in lots of 1000 or more.   
  
Will graduated in 2005 and had to move to Minnesota for his graduate studies, so he graciously gave me all of his plants. At the time, I wasn't into venus fly traps so much, so the pot was placed among the Sarracenia and neglected for....oh, around 5+ years!  
  
They were jammed into a 4" pot and neglected for more than half a decade! No repotting, no TLC, nuthin!  
Out of 50+ seedlings, only a few survived and it was clearly the strongest and tougest of the bunch. This goes to show this clone has great breeding potential since it tolerated such severe neglect.  
  
When I finally got interested in VFT's again, the pot was overgrown with weeds and smothered with dead Sarracenia leaves (it was late winter and time to repot!) In fact, the plastic pot crumbled into pieces when I picked it up since it was so weathered from the sun!  
  
Dionaea "Orton's Red Side" as I'll tentatively call it was repotted and "grown for Jesus." After the first year of recovery, the 3 or so main plants produced huge traps! This clone can turn reddish on the inside of the trap (like most venus fly traps), but what makes this individual stand out from the crowd is its ability to produce red pigments on the outside of the trap while still having a solid green petiole and somewhat green interior. Environmental conditions play a huge role in this phenotype, but it also seems to be genetically based. The petioles very long petioles and can produce decently large traps. Over time, as the "bulb" grows large, it'll split into 2 or 4 plants, and the back of the bulb will form a few tiny plantlets. You know you have a vigorous clone when it constantly divides on its own (reminds me of B52). The fact that it does very well outdoors here in Northern California says a lot as well:it seems only the most vigorous VFT clones do well for us here.

Below is a different commercial clone that I've had since childhood, and it's always been a great performer!  Very typical looking traps, vigorous grower, and can have bright red traps under optimal conditions.  Traps can get big, but not giant.  This clone does require more frequent repotting and does go downhill if you don't change out the soil every 3 years or so.  Grown outdoors here in Northern California, it took a while to recover from transplanting this winter, but now that the plants are fully established, they're pushing out large traps all over the place.  The plant looked a lot less full 2 weeks ago.    
  
This clone seems to produce the best traps around this time of year (July).  The "summer stretched" petioles are medium in length unlike D. "Orton".

Are these destroying a natural habitat, preserving genetics that would have otherwise gone extinct, or both?
