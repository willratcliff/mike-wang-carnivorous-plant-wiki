Aside from what I've seen in the wild, the reddest S. leucophylla I've ever seen in cultivation is S. leucophylla clone B Franklin Co, FL.  I believe that clone originally came from sunbelle.  Anyhow, I got this select clone below from my good homie Rob Co of the pitcher plant project (BTW, he's famous and was recently published in sunset magazine!)  It's a cross between two red clones from the franklin Co, FL population: clone A x clone B.  I've seen other seedlings from this batch, and they don't compare to this plant, which is vigorous, and just an absolute beauty.  
  
Another nice thing about this clone is that it produced a decent crop of spring pitchers.  However, the most impressive, darkest red traps are produced in the fall.  These traps will turn even darker red and perhaps could turn the entire trap solid red during the winter dormancy period, but that is still yet to be seen (I just got this plant last winter).  
  
S. leucophylla clone A x clone B 'select clone'  Franklin Co, FL.  Photos were taken 9/28/13:

The Amur Leopard, *Panthera pardus orientalis*, is a great example. A subspecies that is as about as critically endangered as you can get with only ~26 left in their East Asian habitat, numbers of Amurs in captivity conversely are ~200. Clearly some wild populations of Sarracenia have little chance as the future ticks over. And that last measure requires so much thought and planning as to be no flash in the pan notion.

What is interesting about the Franklin County site is the absence of any other Sarracenia species. The nearest flava that we know of are many miles away, and there are no psittacina. We know introgression plays a part in colors and shapes, but perhaps it is over-rated. We think leucophylla can express red color without introgression, as seen in these Franklin County specimens (BTW, there are more traditional colors present as well)which are isolated. We know others who have visited the site and never found other S. species. The last time our friend Manny visited the site, he found Pinguicula planifolia, which was never seen before there.

Here's an amusing anecdotal story: For the last week or so, with cooler nights (finally below 26 degrees C /80 degrees F), I like to sit outside amid the Sarrs just after sunset, sipping on a brew. Part of the experience includes an interesting dusk visitor: three large moths that visit the leucophyllas, hovering like hummingbirds, lapping up the sugary droplets on the lid and around the lip. They never stop hovering, moving from pitcher to pitcher. One interesting thing I noticed: they never visit other Sarracenia--strictly staying with leucos. I saw one moth buzz around a (leuco x moorei), but no deal. It went straight for the nearby Purple Lip x self! Hybrids are not acceptable, even when it's three fourths leuco. Talk about choosy! The moths have no problem with our red leucos from Franklin County, indicating that there is no hybrid introgression. I trust the moths more than some human experts.

Fascinating, sunbelle. I have often seen butterflies and such sample my leucs and also never get caught. This fall a couple of moths have been caught by pitchers, but not very many. In Northern California where I live moths are most abundant earlier in the summer before the leucs have their big traps. Right now my leucs happen to be brimming with a large, particularly hairy black fly and these small beetles that resemble kiwifruit - their bodies are that exact shade of green and they have black spots like the seeds haha!  
  
 I haven't been able to discern any particular prey partitioning among my plants, except that leucs in general seem to be the most successful insect catchers. What the plants catch seems to have mostly to do with what happens to be flying around in a given season. Different bugs in fall than in spring/early summer, and not much of anything in late August and September as at that time insect populations at a low ebb due to our extended dry summer. For the first two weeks after their fall pitchers opened my leucs caught next to nothing, but right around Oct 1st the rate of prey capture seemed to spike and now they are bulging at the seams, as it were.
