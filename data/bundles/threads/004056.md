I've had a lot of people recently ask me this question, so here's a post that may require you to get your nerd goggles on so you can understand what's being said!    I also figured this will be helpful in the future in case I get Aced out unexpectedly and people are frantically trying to decode my collection.         
  
  
Regarding labeling, I'll start off by mentioning the main purpose of my collection is to house a living library of genetically diverse populations from countless locations (well, I'm not that great at counting).  Many populations in the collection no longer exist in the wild, so these are very significant plants, and in some cases, they are some of the only plants left on this planet from the now extinct populations.     The collection serves as a genetic backbone for breeders and conservationists for now and for the future.    There are 10's of 1000's of different clones from various populations, all carefully labeled and verified true to location.  Any plant with dubious location data is labeled as such.    I don't intend to have any of these original genetics  re-enter the wild as is (some exceptions possible), but I do hope some of these genetics will be reproduced in the future  under sterile or semi-sterile conditions so they can be considered by professionals for reintroduction (kids at home, please be responsible and don't plant stuff back in the wild).  
  
In a lot of cases, individuals in many of the populations have no clonal name, they are merely in large community tubs because I have a family now and no longer have the endless days to geek out on plants  

![:)](//storage.proboards.com/forum/images/smiley/smiley.png) [not mirrored]

    Every community tub where every single individual is genetically unique (ie. they are the original genetics for a population) are labeled with the species name, county, and state.   If a population of plants in a community tray are divisions from multiple different clones from the same population, the community tub have a label indicating they are divisions and not the originals. It is very important from a conservation point of view to distinguish divisions versus original mother plants because the originals will have richer genetic diversity than divisions.  with divisions, you can have multiple copies of a single genotype all in the same tray, so there's less genetic diversity in comparison to a tray of mother plants where every individual is genetically different.  However, if you  take a single division of all the original mother plants, place the divisions  in a community tub, then that community tub can be considered the original clones!  NO, I haven't done this because imagine the confusion of telling the difference between the original mother plants versus copies of the original mother plants....shoot, I can barely follow my own thoughts on this, LOL     
  
 That said, many of my plants have clonal numbers or clonal letters.   The purpose of clonal numbers or letters is to distinguish one clone from another in a population.  For example,  with Hurricane creek white clones A-F (aka HCW clones A-F), every single letter distinguishes one individual from another, and the fact that they all have letters with the same county designation indicate they come from the same original source or a particular location.  HCW clones A-F  all originated from seeds collected directly from the site before it was destroyed.    Does clone A mean it's higher quality or more popular/in higher demand than clone F?  Nope.  The plants were randomly assigned a letter way before they reached maturity or were of known quality.  In contrast, I also have hurricane creek white genetics from John Hummer.  These are from open pollinated seeds from several plants that were dug up from the site before it was destroyed, so they're genetically different than the other seed grown HCW plants, which suspect came from parent plants in the wild that were destroyed when the site was turned into a pine plantation.  At first, I only found one individual from hummer's HCW batch that seemed worthy of giving a clonal designation, so I called it hummer's hurricane creek white Baldwin Co, AL (Hummer's HCW).  A year or two later, another seedling from the batch turned out to be pretty outstanding, so now there's Hummer's Hurricane creek white clone #1 Baldwin Co, AL and HHCW clone #2 Baldwin Co, AL.         
  
With some clones, you'll see numbers instead of letters.  Why?  They may be genetics from the same general county, but from a different population, so the letters and numbers help discern one population from another.  I wouldn't read into the difference between numbers and letters too much though because when I first came up with this system, I used both letters and numbers arbitrarily, whichever seemed to be convenient at the time.  However, the most  important take home message is that every number and ever letter distinguishes one clone from another.  
  
Some outstanding clones with unique characteristics don't have letters or numbers, they'll have short descriptions as part of their name.  For example, S. leucophylla var. alba 'original yellow flower' Washington Co, AL is the first yellow(ish) flowered alba to bloom out of a large population of leucophyllas from Washington Co, AL. keep in mind, some of my plants have been in the collection for 25 years now (and some were in cultivation for decades before I got them), and all of them have that crappy plastic label in their pot/community tray that eventually breaks due to UV exposure.  How do you keep the tag from being lost by the breakdown of the label?    With all of my mother plants, I write the name on both the top and bottom of the label.   In case the top breaks off, there's a portion of the label buried under the soil.  If that tag falls out, you're screwed, so it's a good idea to completely bury a second tag as well.    I have buried fragments of labels like this that are literally 25 years old and still readable!  
  
It seems many collections have their own unique labeling system, and this is one way to know where a plant came from.  For example, if you see  MK somewhere in the label, you know it came from Mike King.   On the other side of the pond, the labels sometimes contain a chain of names that trace it back to the original source.  This method requires nobody in the chain of ownership messes up.  In my opinion, placing only the original source info on the tags makes things less complicated and leaves less room for error.  However, the benefit of maintaining the chain of ownership is if someone Effed up down the line, you have more data and a better chance of finding out what the true ID of the plant is.   
  
What is the best way to label your plants?  That's really up to you.  Whatever you do, my recommendation is to **never** label a plant unless you are 100% certain of that label.  We should also be understanding that humans make errors (except for Mike Wang of course, hehe)  
  
   If you mislabel a plant on accident or on purpose, it can cause a hell of a lot of confusion, and in some cases, it can cause bad blood between people.  I've seen a single case where a distributor has been blamed for being dishonest with their dealings, but after doing my own personal research without any bias towards one side or the other,  I independently found evidence that the distributor genuinely received the plant mislabeled.  The true originator of the clone (not the distributor)  didn't label their plant correctly, and for years, it wasn't a problem until the clone became popular and in high demand!    Of course, there's another side to the story that's valid and I don't really know the truth behind the debate, but I politely ask (with a baseball bat in hand and shanker in my pocket) that we don't open this can of worms in this thread.  Long story short, if you're the originator of a clone, you have a HUGE responsibility that may affect others down the line, and a lot of peace and happiness is maintained with the correct information passed from one hand to the next.  
  
I hope you enjoyed reading this post!  Now that we're on a lighter note,  free to contribute your naming system here if you'd like!

As I am just starting a modest sarracenia collection and wish to preserve all the location and clone information available on plants I obtain, this is excellent guidance!  Thank you Mike!   In labeling Hemerocallis crosses which can get quite complex, I have used the 'write it on the top AND bottom' approach and that works just as Mike describes.    
  
It is quite a challenge to get all the location information on one small tag in a 3 or 6 inch pot.   I notice that on ebay and other places, that many collectors like to use Mike King's numbers (and those of a few other collectors) when they are the original or first large collector source, probably due to a  well-earned faith in Mike King, et al that they have labeled each unique clone separately and accurately.   In a perfect future world, a simple uniform code could be devised and adopted by all especially for wild-originated clones and potentially for later crosses.   It could all be entered in a  master database maintained online/in the cloud and accessible by all, editable by just a few with all the detailed location and clone data that Mike Wang  describes in starting this thread. Sadly the few would be overworked.  But I like to have visions, however delusional they might be.  
  
I saw a past Sarracenia cross with the name of one parent simply being 'Yellow River'.  The owners just copied what was provided by their source.  As a Sarracenia newbie who uses google search as a primary info source,  I surmise that Yellow River refers to a location, and not a specific clone.  Even if it were a recording error and the original was 'Yellow Jacket', I think there is more than one hybrid running around under the name 'Yellow Jacket'  just as there is Daniel Rudd and a slew of Daniel Rudd imposters.  In some respects the errors can be viewed as anachronisms we can smile about.  A part of me likes them, (the part that raised 2 sons and said "because I said so" an infinite number of times and not the part that wears my nerd goggles).

You ever get that feeling you've seen something somewhere but just can't pinpoint it, Mike?😉😂 thank you for writing this up!  
  
To contribute I actually started a somewhat similar system with this year's batch of seedlings. Though it's not to the clonal number stage yet, as most of them only have a pitcher or two. I figure after about six months to a year I'll start the actual clone numbering that way all of the weaker plants should be out of the way.   
  
Since I'm growing old seeds for NASC, and I have my seeds (I tend to be scatterbrained sometimes) I thought I need to come up with a simple way to distinguish between the two and keep track of what's what. I decided that I would for now give the pots a letter-number designation that would at a glance tell me if it's mine or not. The letter designates who's seedlings they are and the number designates which seedlings they are. For example, pot labeled A-1. A designates my seedlings, 1 when I reference my chart designates seed cross S.moorei "MPF" Clone ? x Adrian Slack - PF. (Yes, I'm one of those crazy mfers that blew my wad on high quality seeds) PF being the original source or in this case the breeder.   
  
  
After discussing this topic with Mike I've decided to follow his example when I get to the clonal stage. For example I'll end up with S.moorei "MPF" Clone ? x Adrian Slack - PF/HB clone #1. Do I want to write this out twice on a tag? Nope, I sure don't. That's where the initial pot numbering comes into play. I'll only be labeling this clone as A-1 clone #1 on the tags to save me from getting writers cramp. For sales, and show off purposes I will be using the full cross breeder/grower label. I fully intend on keeping the record in writing as well as digital format, so when I lose one I'll still have a backup. Yes, I have faith in the fact at some point I will lose either the written list or digital list. 😂  
  
Once the plants are grown enough to be potted separately I'll be making a proper label that will be shoved below soil level as well as one with the letter-number designation above soil level.

Excellent points, well said!  Even with a uniform master database of names/descriptions, without the addition of photos depicting specific clones growing under various environmental conditions, it's hard to 100% verify the ID of a plant without DNA testing.    Even that, you'll need a sample of a verified plant true to location to compare the DNA to, and if the original population is already extinct in the wild, getting verified material to compare becomes much more challenging.   This challenge is universal for other cultivated plants, especially food crops where many of the wild-type ancestral populations are extinct.  
  
  
With regards to our attempt to photo-document clones on this very forum,  after seeing what photobucket did,  [petapixel.com/2017/07/01/photobucket-just-broke-billions-photos-embedded-web/](https://petapixel.com/2017/07/01/photobucket-just-broke-billions-photos-embedded-web/)  I'm not aware of any way that guarantees these photo-libraries we're building online will persist forever.  Also, if Proboards decides to change their business model, this forum could disappear in a flash too!  In the orchid world, I saw an entire person's life work cataloguing disa orchid hybrids and cultivars disappear a few months after he passed away.    
  
I have faith in technology nerds who will solve some of our long term issues, as I'm sure the goal of maintaining photos and data in the cloud for generations to come isn't unique to plants.  20 years from now, this discussion will probably be obsolete.  In the meantime, this forum will continue to be updated with more photos and descriptions.
