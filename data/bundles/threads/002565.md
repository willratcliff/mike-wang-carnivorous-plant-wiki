It's no secret that Sarracenia leucophylla produces 2 flushes of pitcher growth: one in the spring/early summer, and then the biggest, baddest, most colorful traps during the fall.  Now that we have grown out many genetically different variants from throughout it's natural range, it seems like some populations or individuals are capable of producing both nice spring and fall traps, whereas other populations produce small/crappy spring pitchers and amazing fall trap.  to make things even more complicated, the same clone that produces crappy pitchers outdoors for us during the early summer can produce amazing early summer traps under greenhouse conditions!    
  
 Some years, the white trumpet pitcher plant produces an incredible fall show for us outdoors here in Northern California, whereas during other years, it merely produces phyllodia all year long and then goes dormant.  Why does it behave this way?  
  
For the longest time, we speculated it must be temperature.  S. leucophylla seems to love warmer temps, and they seem to produce their best pitchers when it's hot.  When it's cooler during the  summer/early fall, perhaps that prevents the plants from producing fall traps.  That hypothesis was thrown out the window when we saw incredible fall pitchers on S. leucophylla Hurricane creek white clone E, grown in Mendocino County, CA.  To give you perspective, the average daily summer temperatures up there are in the mid to high 60's.  My average daily temperature during the summer here south of San Francisco, CA is roughly 78-80F.  To sumarize, S. leucophylla can still produce incredible fall traps with cooler temperatures.  
  
So back to square one.  How do you get S. leucophylla to consistently produce fall traps?  I still can't answer this question in its entirety as there are multiple factors that contribute to fall trap production.  However, I *THINK* I figured out one important factor: spacing.  
  
What do most S. leucophylla with nice fall traps have in common?  Their growth points are getting hit with direct sunlight!  IF you pack S. flava tightly together, and the growth points are shaded by surrounding plants, will they stop producing big old traps?  Nope.  So why is S. leucophylla so much pickier than S. flava?  No idea yet.   
  
Here's some observations that suggest light hitting the rhizomes is one factor that determines fall trap production.  Photos taken 8/24/15. Love it Mike! Dang those HCW are siiick! Yeah I speculate that while light and temperature are both important factors for fall pitcher production, light is probably the more critical of the two. In the wild, overgrown, shaded leucos don't tend to pitcher well at all, despite the ambient temperatures being um, hot as F in the fall in their native range. That said, I'm skeptical about dismissing temperature altogether since there seems to be a lot of genetic variation in how different clones respond to temperature under identical lighting conditions (as when they are grown right next to each other in a collection). HCW seem to consistently produce nice spring pitchers in cooler Cali springs (and maybe more capable in cooler fall conditions too?), whereas Alabama - forget about it!   
Lastly, just to be super annoying 

![;)](//storage.proboards.com/forum/images/smiley/wink.png) [not mirrored]

 it occurs to me that there is a bit of a confounded relationship between the light and temperature variables. After all, the plants in your trays that get more light also experience a warmer microclimate as a result of getting additional direct solar radiation. Ambient air temps will be the same, but the sun directly striking soil and plant tissue could raise the temps at those more exposed plants significantly. Is this making a difference? I don't know, but it could.   
Any way you slice it, I think a leuco getting plenty of light to the whole plant including the rhizome AND experiencing hot temperatures will be a the happiest leuco. Since us growers can't change the weather but we can put our plants in more light, I think you have a point for anyone who's looking to keep their leuco game tight

Those are great observations, thanks calen and rhizomatus!   There's certainly more than just direct sunlight hitting the growth point involved as alluded to earlier in this post.  I think the point is that all else being optimal (ie. longer nights, warm day/nights, and full sun, perfect moisture content, lots of energy stored in the rhizome), if you don't get direct light hitting those growth points for a certain period of time, there's less of a chance you'll get fall pitchers.

The bed below has been "cleaned up" (there's some photos above before traps were pruned out).  To recap, these plants were jam packed in a community tray, so during mid summer (early July?), I transplanted them into individual pots and spaced them out.  Every last plant has direct sun hitting the growth point ever since.   
  
 Now that it's early September, unlike my overgrown hurricane creek whites, where a few pots sent out zero fall traps and are already dormant, EVERY SINGLE plant below is sending out fall traps! I even did an experiment and transplanted a few in late July to see if I could coax them to produce fall traps by exposing the rhizomes to direct sunlight.  Thing is, these had less time to produce roots and acclimate, but interestingly enough, most of the "recently" transplanted individuals are sending out fall traps (although, considering equal sized rhizomes from the same population, the traps produced were smaller than the batch that was transplanted early July).    
  
Long story short, it seems like these plants only need direct light hitting the rhizomes for about a month or so to induce fall traps.  For those of us with space issues, this is good news since you don't need to use up too much space for a very long period of time.  Of course, timing the spacing is everything, if you space them out during September, it's too late.  Perhaps the first week of August is the latest you can wait.

Too early to show off the results, but this looks like the absolute best leuco year I have ever had.  There's so many plants now sending up fall traps, but they're still in the "asparagus tips" stage.  Need another 2 weeks or so to see the show in its fullest glory.    
  
The most ideal situation would be to leave on all the green from the previous year's growth until you see 2-3 spring traps fully develop.  At that time, you'll want to cut off all the old growth.  However, that's assuming this plant isn't being shaded by any neighboring plants and is spaced out quite a bit.  With a lot of plants, this quickly will eat up all of your space.  In the wild, while fields look like they're packed with plants, individual clumps are actually spaced out quite a bit.    
  
If you have hundreds of leucophylla plants and want to pack them as close as possible to maximize space but at the same time, not place them pot to pot so light hits the rhizome, then cutting off all the pitchers/phyllodia to the ground in February (or right before growth resumes) is the way to go under certain environmental conditions (ie. outdoors in California).  However, results may vary....It really depends on your environment:in the wild, burning right before spring growth resumes will consistently give you gigantic traps in the fall because you have so many days in a row that are both sunny and warm.  It's also warm at night in the wild, which seems to favor leuco growth.    
  
Outdoors here in California, it's sunny all summer long, but some weeks, it's only in the high 60's and low 70's during the day, so the cool days really set us back.  We also have a lot of cool nights which seems unfavorable to leucophyllas.   Long story short, if you have a good grow season and lots of leucophylla plants packed as close as you can without shading each other, then cutting off all the traps during the winter dormancy period is a solid strategy.  I took the risk and cut off all the traps to the ground this past winter, and the result, although not complete, have been the best I've ever seen so far:

#### *jdallas*
`post #20` · `id=29578` · `2016-09-27 04:08:56 UTC`

We cut all trumpet pitchers back to rhizomes every winter.  Back in the day I was a conservative trimmer, but I've just seen the result too many times.  Cutting them back almost always results in better looking plants the next season.  It's especially helpful for many of the rubra varieties, and hybrids since they are such weedy growers.  It helps keep the rhizome crown open and less prone to fungus.  
  
We only seem to get good late pitcher development when the summer is fairly warm, and the warmth lasts into late September.  Rainy summers can be dismal.

If you have hundreds of leucophylla plants and want to pack them as close as possible to maximize space but at the same time, not place them pot to pot so light hits the rhizome, then cutting off all the pitchers/phyllodia to the ground in February (or right before growth resumes) is the way to go under certain environmental conditions (ie. outdoors in California).  However, results may vary....It really depends on your environment:in the wild, burning right before spring growth resumes will consistently give you gigantic traps in the fall because you have so many days in a row that are both sunny and warm.  It's also warm at night in the wild, which seems to favor leuco growth.    
  
Outdoors here in California, it's sunny all summer long, but some weeks, it's only in the high 60's and low 70's during the day, so the cool days really set us back. Long story short, if you have a good grow season and lots of leucophylla plants packed as close as you can without shading each other, then cutting off all the traps during the winter dormancy period is a solid strategy. We cut all our upright-growing Sarrs. to 3-4 inch stubs each winter and the leuco's. make great pitchers every year. The periodic cutting may effect trap production from a morphological/physiological standpoint: less photosynthetic area=make more. Or, it may be effected by the total Nitrogen availability. This latter theory makes the most sense, from an evolutionary standpoint.  
  
The plants flower and start producing seed early in the season. The seed, especially, are Nitrogen dense. If the plants have plenty of digesting insects in old pitcher bases, there may be less need for Nitrogen to be "captured". If the total Nitrogen availability is reduced, maybe the fall pitchers are a response. I'd bet the timing is mostly photoperiodic. Since 1982, we've had cool seasons and others hot as blazes; it always seems that the fall traps start appearing when the days shorten considerably, late August to late Sept.  
  
Reducing the summer foliage should create an imbalance with the calories the root/rhizome needs and what the photosynthetic output from the foliage is. This should trigger foliage output.  
  
Many years ago, I was at Longwood Gardens, PA and noticed the Nepenthes had almost no traps during late summer. I asked the person in charge of their care if he had any ideas "I don't know. They're on the injector system, like everything else." Bingo; no need to trap. I suggested they use a different, non-injected tap and was told pitchers started appearing, mostly the following summer.  
  
It would be easy to test, as Nitrogen may have nothing to do with fall pitcher production. The test would be to supply about 100-150 ppm Nitrogen, on a regular basis, starting in, say, June, well before fall trap initiation. If the fertilized plants don't fire up fall pitchers, there's the answer. If they make tremendous fall pitchers, then you know the leuco's are Nitrogen pigs and there is/are other factors, besides Nitrogen, at play.

Continuing to love this thread! Thanks to all the experienced growers who contribute on here - as has been said before it's a unique resource.  
My leuco experiments are ongoing and like Mike said every year the plants will give us more / different data to interpret, some of which will challenge our neat hypotheses. [bogman](/user/852)     I love your ideas too - your scientific training shines through and you think about things in a distinct manner parallel with your knowledge base that brings a new level to the discussion.   
This year I arranged my leucos so they would be better spaced and lit at rhizome level, and the results are unmistakable. Pitchers are earlier by about 2 weeks compared to last year, on average, despite starting 2 weeks late due to the cold spring. Many plants that did not pitcher at all last year are pitchering heavily. Plants that did pitcher last year are pitchering more profusely. As for WHY these methods work, the jury is still out, but my findings so far back up the broad outlines of what others have described:  
  
1) Light to the rhizome is very helpful for fall pitcher growth. Plants will perform best, on average, when spaced or otherwise arranged such that self and mutual shading is minimized. Mike seems to place the pots in a looser grid within his large beds, whereas I arrange them in long narrow rows of 2 pots oriented north-south so that each plant gets several hours of direct light to the crown during the course of the day. There are other methods of illuminating the crowns which I plan to try next season.  
  
2. Seed load REDUCES fall trap production drastically in my experience. Last year I had a heavy pod load on many of my best leucos, and noticed that only the GPs not bearing pods produced large fall traps. This year with fewer to no pods on the clones, traps are emerging from all points. I have noticed this same phenomenon in the wild: the best looking plants aka the ones with lush fall crops will rarely have seed pods on them, whereas the clumps that are basically a hedge of upturned seed pods will have few to no traps. Why I am not sure, but I hypothesisze it has to do with how resource-intensive seedpods are for the plant.  
  
3) Timing of fall traps is influenced by multiple factors that are still being worked out. As others have said, the best-known and probably most important is photoperiod. These plants are CLOCKED baby! Which isn't really surprising at all. Other factors include light to the rhizome (it seems that better lighting makes them pitcher sooner, not just a yes/no pitcher or no pitcher response), and genetics. There is a wide variation in how different clones respond timing wise. I had plants go full-on asparagus mode as early as Aug 1 this year, whereas I have stragglers that just initiated this last week. One clone in particular is a solid 3 weeks behind the average despite perfect health, but seedlings from that clone crossed with another leuco split the diffference! I'll be keeping my eye out to see if this clone is consistently later in future seasons.   
  
These are not "laws of nature" lol. As Mike points out, the plants will defy our conclusions sometimes. I have a tub of Baldwin Co leucos that I was afraid would not pitcher due to being pretty dense and thatchy with spring traps and phyllodia. Nope! Tons of fall traps on every plant. Last year I had a leuco that was totally buried by taller surrounding plants go nuts with robust traps in the shade. But these exceptions do prove the rule, and just because THOSE genetics did that doesn't mean that other genetics wouldn't have had problems, or that they wouldn't have pitchered better / earlier with better spacing.   
  
My leucos look better this year that they have in 2-3 seasons, and there is relevant data there too: when I was rebuilding my collection a few years back, I had relatively few plants and they were not yet large specimens, and they fall pitchered like clockwork. As my grow area became more crowded and the plants grew large, I stopped getting good fall traps except on clones that I babied (which always involved putting them in an extra-sunny spot). I made the crowding mistake as recently as last season (hell, flavas grow great when crowded!) but now looking back in light of these discussions, a pattern emerges.

OK folks, the experiments of 2018 have wrapped up and I'd like to share the results with everyone. The main intervention that I performed this past growing season was to trim all my leucophylla plants at mid-summer. I did this on July 15. At that time the plants had spring traps that were 6-8 weeks old, considerable phyllodia, and some summer traps with more on the way. There were no flower stalks as I did not permit any of my leucos to bloom, except where I planned to breed with the flower. For context, the leuco growing season here goes as follows: the plants break dormancy in late March, bloom in early to mid-May, open spring traps in the second half of May and early June, grow phyllodia second half of June through July, grow summer traps in the second half of July, and initiate fall traps anywhere from the second half of August to the second half of September. By October the temperatures have dropped too low for good growth, although prey capture continues at a very high rate until well into November. The plants are dormant by sometime in the first half of November, it seems.   
  
 Why did I do this and what were the goals of the experiment? As you will have learned reading through this thread, leucos seem to pitcher better when sun is able to penetrate down to the rhizome level, so the trim was intended to increase light levels in that area by removing canopy. I also had heard anecdotal evidence that sudden removal of top growth in summer seemed to stimulate earlier fall trap growth. This would be very handy in my short OR growing season so I would be on the lookout for that effect as well. It also occurred to me that fire adaptation could play a role: with the plants expecting summer fires, it would make sense that sudden canopy removal could be simulating a fire and that perhaps the plants were adapted to wait until after a fire, if possible, to pitcher.  
  
Sterilizing my scissors with bleach between clones, I trimmed everything down to approximately 6-8" of phyllodia and pitcher bases. No distinction was made between traps and phyllodia. This resulted in 2/3 to 3/4 of the material being removed from the plants. I chose July 15 because I knew by this time there would be quite a canopy of phyllodia and traps accumulated, and I reasoned that 6-8 weeks was enough time for the plants to have had a good meal from their spring traps. I also wanted to perform the trim early enough that the plants would have enough time to "notice" the stimulus and pitcher sooner, if they were going to respond that way. Then I waited. Would fall traps arrive significantly sooner? Would trap production be more abundant and complete due to more growth points getting more light?  
  
Did this intervention result in crazy awesome leuco performance??? No, it did not. The experiment was a failure, although as I will discuss below I did learn a lot and feel it was a stepping stone towards success. Results: plants did not pitcher any earlier than normal at all. Some trimmed plants produced pitchers on a larger proportion of their growth points (that is, better pitchers on the north side of clumps where self-shading is the worst). However, most plants were also stunted, producing traps only 2/3 to 3/4 as big as last year's crop. This is all the worse considering that all plants had had an additional season to mature and yet were still smaller. I do not believe this was due to weather. We actually had a record-breaking warm summer that provided excellent conditions for trap growth.   
  
A significant proportion of plants was severely affected, producing no fall traps at all. These individuals seemed to try to pick up where the trim left off - they re-grew what appeared to be summer traps when they should have been producing fall traps. These skinny pitchers seemed to be distinct from the stunted fall traps produced by other plants, which were shorter and smaller than normal fall traps, but still robust in their proportions. The worst-affected individuals produced essentially no growth whatsoever after the trim. This was rare, but it did happen: in the picture below are two large specimens of leucophylla AJ01. This clone is a very vigorous plant and also a reliable fall trap producer - it sends up a strong flush every year assuming growing conditions are halfway decent. The plant on the right was trimmed while the plant on the left was untouched. Both plants have had 2 seasons to recover from division and both have 10-12 growth points apiece. Look at the difference! Granted, most plants did not display such extreme behavior, but this is still indicative of what a disaster this intervention was! Again, the untrimmed plant is on the left and is performing typically of AJ01.  
[https://flic.kr/p/2bufc7G](https://flic.kr/p/2bufc7G)[Untitled](https://flic.kr/p/2bufc7G) by [Calen Hall](https://www.flickr.com/photos/150907390@N02/), on Flickr  
Now that we know how things went, do I have any conclusions about what happened and why the plants responded the way they did? Why were the plants stunted by the trim? I do not believe it was due to removal of photosynthetic tissue (i.e. due to lack of carbohydrates). Many leucophylla clones, especially younger plants and clones from Washington Co. AL produce few to no phyllodia and grow very well. All plants meeting that description did extremely well this year since they were not part of the trim experiment. Rather, I believe that the crucial error was removing the spring and summer traps. I underestimated how critical those are for proper fall trap growth and assuming that the plants had received adequate nutrients from prey after only 6-8 weeks was not correct. On the contrary, at the time of fall pitcher growth a steady flow of nutrients must be occurring from the decrepit spring traps and the freshly filled summer traps. It makes sense that without these crucial resources plants produced at best miniature fall traps, while many simply ceased growth for the year.   
  
Given that many clones that did the mini-trap thing produced well on the entire clump with no pitcherless growth points, I feel these results supports the light-at-the-rhizome hypothesis. One group of plants that did not seem to be negatively affected by the trim was this tub of seed-grown Splinter Hill clones. However, the tub is displaying strong zoning due to shading! The back 1/3 of the tub was partially shaded by overhanging flava pitchers, while the front 2/3 got direct sunlight to the soil level. As you can see, the plants in front pitchered normally while those in back have produced very little (the black plastic is a bit hard to see, but look carefully to see the full depth of the tub - the back row of plants has essentially no traps):  
[https://flic.kr/p/2awHFTd](https://flic.kr/p/2awHFTd)[Untitled](https://flic.kr/p/2awHFTd) by [Calen Hall](https://www.flickr.com/photos/150907390@N02/), on Flickr  
As for the anecdotal evidence of a summer trim stimulating fall trap production, this experiment does not necessarily disprove it entirely. First off, the anecdotal evidence was pretty strong. Second, it comes from southern California, a climate extremely different from that of northern Oregon. It occurs to me that in the extremely long growing season down there, the plants could in fact have enough time to fully digest their spring catch between opening in March and being trimmed in July. S. leucophylla also produces significantly larger spring traps in that environment, meaning the volume of prey capture is far greater that what the plants can attain here. Therefore, it is possible the growers in long-season areas may see benefits from a summer trim that did not materialize for me.   
  
So where do I go from here? I am going to proceed based on the conclusion that light to the rhizome is in fact a critical stimulus for proper fall trap production, but that removal of the plants' nutrient source is very counterproductive. My plan is to perform a trim in 2019 - of phyllodia only. I plan to remove 100% of phyllodia down to the crown, while leaving all spring and summer traps in place. I hypothesize that the plants will be able to photosynthesize adequately in this state while also receiving the full benefit of captured prey and light at the rhizome. This will be a huge pain in the ass - much more labor-intensive than simply mowing off the tops like I did this year - but I'll do it for science! If others would like to join me so as to expand the pool of results, that would be awesome.

OK folks, the experiments of 2018 have wrapped up and I'd like to share the results with everyone. The main intervention that I performed this past growing season was to trim all my leucophylla plants at mid-summer. I did this on July 15. At that time the plants had spring traps that were 6-8 weeks old, considerable phyllodia, and some summer traps with more on the way. There were no flower stalks as I did not permit any of my leucos to bloom, except where I planned to breed with the flower. For context, the leuco growing season here goes as follows: the plants break dormancy in late March, bloom in early to mid-May, open spring traps in the second half of May and early June, grow phyllodia second half of June through July, grow summer traps in the second half of July, and initiate fall traps anywhere from the second half of August to the second half of September. By October the temperatures have dropped too low for good growth, although prey capture continues at a very high rate until well into November. The plants are dormant by sometime in the first half of November, it seems.   
  
 Why did I do this and what were the goals of the experiment? As you will have learned reading through this thread, leucos seem to pitcher better when sun is able to penetrate down to the rhizome level, so the trim was intended to increase light levels in that area by removing canopy. I also had heard anecdotal evidence that sudden removal of top growth in summer seemed to stimulate earlier fall trap growth. This would be very handy in my short OR growing season so I would be on the lookout for that effect as well. It also occurred to me that fire adaptation could play a role: with the plants expecting summer fires, it would make sense that sudden canopy removal could be simulating a fire and that perhaps the plants were adapted to wait until after a fire, if possible, to pitcher.  
  
Sterilizing my scissors with bleach between clones, I trimmed everything down to approximately 6-8" of phyllodia and pitcher bases. No distinction was made between traps and phyllodia. This resulted in 2/3 to 3/4 of the material being removed from the plants. I chose July 15 because I knew by this time there would be quite a canopy of phyllodia and traps accumulated, and I reasoned that 6-8 weeks was enough time for the plants to have had a good meal from their spring traps. I also wanted to perform the trim early enough that the plants would have enough time to "notice" the stimulus and pitcher sooner, if they were going to respond that way. Then I waited. Would fall traps arrive significantly sooner? Would trap production be more abundant and complete due to more growth points getting more light?  
  
Did this intervention result in crazy awesome leuco performance??? No, it did not. The experiment was a failure, although as I will discuss below I did learn a lot and feel it was a stepping stone towards success. Results: plants did not pitcher any earlier than normal at all. Some trimmed plants produced pitchers on a larger proportion of their growth points (that is, better pitchers on the north side of clumps where self-shading is the worst). However, most plants were also stunted, producing traps only 2/3 to 3/4 as big as last year's crop. This is all the worse considering that all plants had had an additional season to mature and yet were still smaller. I do not believe this was due to weather. We actually had a record-breaking warm summer that provided excellent conditions for trap growth.   
  
A significant proportion of plants was severely affected, producing no fall traps at all. These individuals seemed to try to pick up where the trim left off - they re-grew what appeared to be summer traps when they should have been producing fall traps. These skinny pitchers seemed to be distinct from the stunted fall traps produced by other plants, which were shorter and smaller than normal fall traps, but still robust in their proportions. The worst-affected individuals produced essentially no growth whatsoever after the trim. This was rare, but it did happen: in the picture below are two large specimens of leucophylla AJ01. This clone is a very vigorous plant and also a reliable fall trap producer - it sends up a strong flush every year assuming growing conditions are halfway decent. The plant on the right was trimmed while the plant on the left was untouched. Both plants have had 2 seasons to recover from division and both have 10-12 growth points apiece. Look at the difference! Granted, most plants did not display such extreme behavior, but this is still indicative of what a disaster this intervention was! Again, the untrimmed plant is on the left and is performing typically of AJ01.  
[https://flic.kr/p/2bufc7G](https://flic.kr/p/2bufc7G)[Untitled](https://flic.kr/p/2bufc7G) by [Calen Hall](https://www.flickr.com/photos/150907390@N02/), on Flickr  
Now that we know how things went, do I have any conclusions about what happened and why the plants responded the way they did? Why were the plants stunted by the trim? I do not believe it was due to removal of photosynthetic tissue (i.e. due to lack of carbohydrates). Many leucophylla clones, especially younger plants and clones from Washington Co. AL produce few to no phyllodia and grow very well. All plants meeting that description did extremely well this year since they were not part of the trim experiment. Rather, I believe that the crucial error was removing the spring and summer traps. I underestimated how critical those are for proper fall trap growth and assuming that the plants had received adequate nutrients from prey after only 6-8 weeks was not correct. On the contrary, at the time of fall pitcher growth a steady flow of nutrients must be occurring from the decrepit spring traps and the freshly filled summer traps. It makes sense that without these crucial resources plants produced at best miniature fall traps, while many simply ceased growth for the year.   
  
Given that many clones that did the mini-trap thing produced well on the entire clump with no pitcherless growth points, I feel these results supports the light-at-the-rhizome hypothesis. One group of plants that did not seem to be negatively affected by the trim was this tub of seed-grown Splinter Hill clones. However, the tub is displaying strong zoning due to shading! The back 1/3 of the tub was partially shaded by overhanging flava pitchers, while the front 2/3 got direct sunlight to the soil level. As you can see, the plants in front pitchered normally while those in back have produced very little (the black plastic is a bit hard to see, but look carefully to see the full depth of the tub - the back row of plants has essentially no traps):

As for the anecdotal evidence of a summer trim stimulating fall trap production, this experiment does not necessarily disprove it entirely. First off, the anecdotal evidence was pretty strong. Second, it comes from southern California, a climate extremely different from that of northern Oregon. It occurs to me that in the extremely long growing season down there, the plants could in fact have enough time to fully digest their spring catch between opening in March and being trimmed in July. S. leucophylla also produces significantly larger spring traps in that environment, meaning the volume of prey capture is far greater that what the plants can attain here. Therefore, it is possible the growers in long-season areas may see benefits from a summer trim that did not materialize for me.   
  
So where do I go from here? I am going to proceed based on the conclusion that light to the rhizome is in fact a critical stimulus for proper fall trap production, but that removal of the plants' nutrient source is very counterproductive. My plan is to perform a trim in 2019 - of phyllodia only. I plan to remove 100% of phyllodia down to the crown, while leaving all spring and summer traps in place. I hypothesize that the plants will be able to photosynthesize adequately in this state while also receiving the full benefit of captured prey and light at the rhizome. This will be a huge pain in the ass - much more labor-intensive than simply mowing off the tops like I did this year - but I'll do it for science! If others would like to join me so as to expand the pool of results, that would be awesome.

For sure, there are many different valid, proven methods that work.  There are exceptions to the rules, but generally speaking, most leucophylla clones throughout their entire natural range produce nice fall traps if the following criteria are met:  
  
1) growth point(s) of S. leucophylla need to receive direct sunlight. how much?  As much as possible, at least by June or July at the latest in the pacific Northwest.   IF you want to produce a giant pot filled with  tons of fall traps, you have to manipulate the rhizomes during the winter dormancy period such that summer growth doesn't shade out other nearby growth points.  One work around is to Plant several large, established, single growth points from the same clone far away from each other in the same, large container or even bog. Be sure the growth points are all pointing in a similar direction (ie. one isn't facing "North" while another behind i it is facing"south")  and aren't shading each other when they produce summer growth.  If they are facing different directions, or if one growth point is behind another, they will be shaded.  
2) make sure your plants are pest, weed, and disease free.  Any one of these factors may negatively affect fall traps.  
3)Healthy rhizomes:no die back from previous grow seasons.  
4) Healthy roots: it's ideal to not have any root tip die back from the previous 1-2 grow seasons.  strong roots many years in a row prime the plant to produce big fall traps.    
5) Feeding: you'll need every last one of those spring/summer traps to be well fed, and you'll need to have had great growth the previous year as well as warm, sunny spring days to get decent spring traps (note:there are exceptions to this rule).  Plants with more abundant spring traps that are all consistently fed throughout the summer tend to produce the biggest traps.  
6) Weather: you need overall warm, sunny days during late summer and early fall to get the best looking traps.  Warm nights are also a plus.  Poor spring weather isn't always a setback!    
7) During the grow season, If it's brown, chop it down.  If it's green, leave it on....but to reiterate, if those growth points are shaded, you probably won't get consistently nice fall traps.     Those summer traps filled with insects, even if they're brown at the top by late summer, should be left on the plant.  Just chop off the brown portion of the "head" and leave the rest on the plant  Note: There are exceptions to this rule which have already been covered in this thread.  
8) During the winter dormancy period: chop ALL the leaves off to the ground!  There are exceptions to this rule of course, but this helps insure the growth points get light during the next grow season.   
  
Any sort of setback to the plant's growth momentum during the current and/or previous grow season may affect fall trap size, so dial in your watering, feed the traps if they aren't catching insects, and pray for great weather if you're growing outdoors!  
  
  
Just like S. flava, no matter how well you care for the plants and how good the weather is, some clones will produce big fall traps only every other year: the fall traps sometimes tax the rhizome so much that it takes a year to recover.  The following year, it'll produce another set of large traps, provided it's well cared for.

Replantation: I would only remove them to the base of the rhizome if they come off easily: these are ususally older leaves from 1-2 years ago.  Newer leaves produced the previous fall are typically harder to remove and I recommend just chopping them with scissors instead of removing them completely off the rhizome.   In general, if they don't come off easily, just chop them, you want to minimize open wounds on the rhizome whenever possible.     The whole concept is to remove as much leaf material above ground so that minimal amounts of leaf material have a chance to shade the growth points the next grow season.   
  
Regarding when to cut those traps back all the way during the winter dormancy period, I honestly don't know when the absolute best time is to do it, it probably depends on your environment.    Here in Northern California, I like to chop them to the ground as soon as all the traps turn mostly brown, which is probably January.  That way, I know most of the nutrients from those traps have been "sucked out" and stored in the rhizome.

You can cut off the trap's "head", which will prevent the trap from catching more insects, but leave the rest on to feed the fall traps.
