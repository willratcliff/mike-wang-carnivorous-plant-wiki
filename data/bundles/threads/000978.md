There are perhaps only a handful or two of large populations of S. leucophylla left in the wild.  The majority that still remain are either relic patches of a once giant field of plants, or volunteers in modified habitats (ie. man made drainage ditches).  Many of the historically giant populations are now either destroyed, or if they haven't been touched whatsoever, they are now etiolated plants growing in thick, dense forests.    
  
Before people dominated the landscape and plowed or altered every square acre of land, fires would come in and burn up the forest, creating new habitats for Sarracenias to colonize.  Today, those forest are now homes, structures, and farms where fire isn't permitted since it would damage people's property.    
  
It's surprising how short lived many of these sites are, especially during the more recent times.  On the other hand, they're still there, and they've struggled to survive the face of human negligence.   
  
  
This site below only exists because a power company consistently clears the grasses every year or so to protect their electrical equipment.  On the side of the road, I had already seen signs of round-up, and it's only a matter of time when they spray this field.  One application of herbicide can ruin centuries of growth.  If I had to bet, this site will be sprayed in the next few years once they realize the cost savings.  
  
As pessimistic as it may seem, this is the reality for many of these sites.  We visited many historic sites that didn't have a single plant left due to annual herbicide applications.  However, there are some remote sites like the one below that still exist today, so maybe...just maybe a few will stand the test of time.

#### **Mike Wang** (`@meizzwang`)
`post #14` · `id=4406` · `2013-12-04 18:28:13 UTC`

Glad you like them Sunbelle!  It was rather dry at this site, and you could tell the areas where the Sarracenias grew were the lower-lying spots.  No mud, no puddles, and when you dug into the ground, it was only slightly damp.  When we visited, it hadn't rained for a good 2 weeks.  
  
Sonia-what I didn't mention is before entering this site, a gigantic snake darted away from me.  It moved faster than anyone can run at full speed, and thankfully, it was moving away from me, not towards me.  The fire ants/biting insects, extreme heat (you can't stand out there for more than 2 hours without getting heat exhaustion), razor sharp vines, and snakes make it a rather hostile environment.  During the winter, temperatures fluctuate from extreme freezing one day to semi-warm the next day.  Hurricanes are always a threat in the late summer through the fall, and it seems tornadoes can show up any day.  Tropical storms are not uncommon, and get this-it even snows out there!!!  Did I mention the giant hail storms?    
  
Funny how the pictures above paint a completely different environment-it's tough to capture with a camera just how savage it can be out there, especially because we were only visiting for such a short time.

Mike, your answer is sort of what I expected. We've seen leucos growing in dry, compact sand, but know that when there are heavy rains the whole field floods and then slowly drains.  
Did you get an ID on the snake? There are some really nice color forms of rat snakes up around the gulf coastal areas that encompass the Florida panhandle, Alabama, and Mississippi. We've also seen some large Black Racers up there. They are lightning fast and diurnal hunters, so make for a startling discovery when you come across one.  
Sonia, the snake bites to worry about in pitcher plant country are Cottonmouths and rattlesnakes (3 species). Coral snakes are around, but they are shy.

#### **Mike Wang** (`@meizzwang`)
`post #32` · `id=12083` · `2014-11-07 19:26:52 UTC`

Great questions KE!  I too was very curious about the rhizome, but my interest was mainly in the growth points.   
  
As for the previous year's traps, many had toppled over.  The phyllodia from the previous year was still in tact on many plants, but it was completely brown.  As far as growth points are concerned, you don't see 6-7 heads all closely crowded together like you do in cultivated plants.  Instead, you see 6-7 LONG rhizomes "running" in different directions.  Very few of these have any branching "heads."  
  
As far as their position in the ground, it's exactly the same as you'd see in cultivation: the top of the "back" is exposed, but the rest is covered in soil.  Of course, plants that had been blasted out of the soil, covered in soil,etc. from the weather were in abnormal positions, but that was an exception to the rule.  I didn't see many in other positions.    
  
 I was curious about fall pitcher production because I wanted to see if there's a way to predict whether or not you're going to have a good fall flush of pitchers.   Here's what I observed in the wild:  
1) In August, a few plants that already had 2-3 fall pitchers had dormant growth points.    
2) In rhizomes that hadn't yet produced fall pitchers, you could see new ones emerging and still growing.  
3) In very few instances, you could find plants that had 2-3 fall pitchers, but still have 1-2 new ones forming.  That was almost non-existent, the maximum I counted was 4 fall pitchers on one growth point.  If I had to guess, the average was 1-2 fall pitchers per growth point, but many clones had multiple heads, so without seeing the details, it  looks as though each growth point is pushing out a lot of traps.    
  
Not sure what you want to know about the foliage of other surrounding plants?  Unfortunately, I didn't pay very close attention to them, except a few of the outstanding species (Sabatia, Lilium, etc).

Haha KE totally - no two alike and each lovely in its own way.   
  
I was surprised at the unbranched length on many Sarr rhizomes in the wild - much longer than typically seen in cultivation as Mike said. In areas where the soil was bare some plants had 2 feet or so of back rhizome snaking along the ground. The Sandy Creek (Bay Co.) flavas seemed like reluctant clumpers and no doubt we would have found long back rhizomes on them if we had gone digging through the grass. There was a Liberty Co. flava ornata genet I posted a pic of 6-8 feet across but with only 6-8 growth points, indicating little branching. Then on the other hand there were clones that formed incredibly dense masses of growth points, often times growing right next to sparsely branched plants. The "Outlaw" ornata comes to mind - clearly a clumper. So in short I think it's genetics, with some populations having a mix of branchers and non-branchers, and some even having mostly or all non-branchers. In cultivation we don't grow our plants with competing grasses etc. so more light reaches the rhizome and activates dormant eyes. Plus we tend to select for clumping vigor and often toss (and are less likely to receive via division) those plants that are exceptionally slow to multiply!

Thanks for sharing your observations Calen!  the outlaw site is rather unique in the sense that it seems as though the owners are mowing and harvesting hay from the field.  Did the plants seem shorter in that field than in other populations you visited?  If so, that would imply that the hay was harvested during the grow season.  That would also imply the plants had their pitchers chopped off during the grow season, which means you have a larger ratio of roots to shoots.  Under such circumstances, since auxins are mostly produced in the root tips and shoot tips, the plant is no longer balanced hormonally, and you'll have a larger ratio of auxin to cytokinin.  As a result, the plant triggers more growth points to compensate for the hormonal imbalance, which may explain why the outlaw site is more clumpy than other sites.
