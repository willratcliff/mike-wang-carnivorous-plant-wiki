A few threads ago, we were discussing what  leucophylla clones were best suited for the more northern, cooler climates.  Well, here's a clone that should be experimented with in cooler conditions!  Why?  
1) it comes from North Walton Co, FL.  Yeah, it's still deep south, but maybe the geographic location has some selective pressures to withstand slightly cooler temps than other populations  
2) maybe point one is completely wrong and this clone has S. flava mixed in it several generations ago.  Flavas are known to produce decent pitchers even in relatively luke cold weather (ie. 60-70F days).   
3) This is the only leucophylla clone in the collection that has a ridiculous amount of fully mature pitchers this early in the season.  I know it can be environmentally induced, so experimentation is needed to verify these preliminary observations.  Granted, there are a few other potential "cold tolerant" leucophyllas that I'll discuss in another thread.

Great question, I'll take a whiff to see if I can sense anything and will report back!  Flowers do seem to open at the same time the pitchers emerge, which is consistent with many leucophyllas and some mooreis as well.  I'm not sure there's enough evidence at this point to secure the distant flava hybrid hypothesis, but it's something to consider.  The site this plant comes from sure is loaded with hybrids.    
  
  With S. leucophylla 'Alabama' from Southern Baldwin Co, AL,(here's a pic for reference):  

![](../../images/40/402912b496c2167934fb021beb0ecda3cfb9bba4fff51d17d9cdeddefd79fd32.jpg)

  
 no matter how happy you keep that plant, it will rarely produce nice spring pitchers outdoors here in northern California unless it's exceptionally warm for many weeks in a row.  Most of the times, the spring pitchers are thin, pencil-like, insignificant traps.   My friend Rob Co grew the exact same clone in a greenhouse and he had what looked like gigantic fall pitchers in the spring!   His greenhouse was literally almost exactly the same latitude as my collection, and the only difference is that when it was 50F outside, it was near 75F in his greenhouse.    
  
For cooler climate growers, the initial idea was to identify genotypes that produce the best spring pitchers, since the fall might be cut too short in northern climates that experience early freezing.  You can definitely get somewhat consistently warm summers in more northern climates, so timing pitcher production with the natural time of year that's warm seems to increase the chance of producing nice leucophylla traps.   If you miss the fall flush and the plants go dormant before shooting out traps, those "untapped reserves" in the rhizome might be one of the reasons you can get nice spring traps.  I'd like to see how these genotypes that can produce nice spring traps under cool weather perform in more northern latitudes.  
  
There are other individuals from Washington Co, AL and covington Co, AL that seem to produce nice spring traps when all other genotypes sulk from the cooler weather, so I want to investigate these as well.  Interestingly, these individuals also originate from sites where hybrids are very common.
