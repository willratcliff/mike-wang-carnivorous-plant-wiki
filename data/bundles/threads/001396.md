Now here's a really strange looking clone with a unique triangular shaped lid!  Now, this might open a can of worms, but there's a very big suspicion that S. flava var. rubricorpora "evolved" via hybridization with S. x catesbaei.  This plant really sparks my curiosity, and I'm in the middle of trying to recreate a S. flava var. rubricorpora from a S. x catesbaei.  A good friend of mine showed me a photo of a plant that looks to be a naczii x flava rugelii, and it looks shockingly close to the plant photographed below.  There's also another grower on this forum who has made a cross where the offspring resemble rubricorporas. Such results suggests S. x naczii is the historic "father" or "mother" of s. flava var. rubricorpora, but only time will tell.  Sheesh, the more I think about it, doesn't S. montana have a lid that folds over just like this plant?  Hmmmmmm.....the plot thickens.  
  
Was the plant photographed below a freak-chance backcross with S. x naczii, or do all rubricorporas stem from S. x naczii, but they have been so far removed from generations of back-crossing that you can no longer tell?  We will never know with 100% certainty what the truth is, but we'll probably eventually know with 99.9999999% certainty

Well Mike, some of us old farts, who don't even have a cell phone, have this idea that all the other species arose from purpurea. That might be why a few troublemakers still call rosea a purpurea. Theory is that purps once formed a continuum from north to south. Climate, drainage and forest shifts created big holes in the range. But there's that trend of shorter flower stalks and fatter pitchers as you move south. E.G. NJ purps are fatter than far northern types.  
  
During the course of time, mutant forms of purp either had advantages, or died out. Some day, someone will do an intense DNA analysis which may illuminate what happened. It's amazing how seemingly tiny mutations can have big results. Look at humans and chimps, our DNA is shockingly similar. ... ook   ook!  
  
So it is possible the rubric. arose from a ...  

![8-)](//storage.proboards.com/forum/images/smiley/cool.png) [not mirrored]

  purpurea or catesbaei. But where did the catesbaei come from? When you look at how huge the Sarracenia populations USED to be, there was ample population size for just about anything to happen.  
  
Anyway, Larry Mellichamp was out here, years ago, and we had "the purpurea" discussion". Even though Larry had gone the "splitter" route, he was surprisingly supportive of my "lumper" perspective. Both viewpoints are valid and unprovable, for now.

Yes, indeed! Thank you alabamensis and Mike for the views and link. That was a good read. I took great interest in Taxonomy as a college student; worked in the Univ. herbarium and the U.S. National Arboretum herbarium as well. This gave ample time to ask a lot of brilliant people about plant names and naming philosophies. Of course, I prodded them with Sarracenia-scenario questions and the future prospect of gene analysis. Well... that begs the unanswered questions:  
While quantitative similarities may indicate relationships, they do not answer which genes are most important in separating species. One gene/allele can result in a new species if that new species can not breed with others not sharing that gene. The mixing bowl which is Sarracenia reminds me of orchid taxonomy; many theories and philosophies. One could argue all Sarracenia are one species, since they are a (comparatively) recent genus and they interbreed easily, with the morphological difficulties of flava x psittacina aside. (Odd these show"close" relationship genetically when these are amongst the least likely to hybridize!)  
  
Compare the Sarr. to, say, squash (Cucurbita) or Peppers (Capsicum): Cucurbita maxima, winter squash, does not hybridize with Cucurbita pepo, Acorn, Crookneck, Zucchini, etc. Likewise, Habanero peppers, Capsicum chinense, don't hybridize with Sweet peppers or Jalapeños, Capsicum annuum. Yet, all the Sarr. species are capable of interbreeding and frequently hybridize.  
  
So, does anyone REALLY know what's going on? Chances are, there will be different philosophies for many years to come. Nature pushes genetics. Dynamic, changing habitats tend to encourage mutations necessary to survive. What one "sees" in quantitative genetic studies may ultimately reflect evolutionary pressures. This may or may not help decide how to name plants. In the end, we're working with a human-made construct.
