After moving around and having to figure out a new home for the disa collection, they're finally settling in...for now until we have to move again!  These are tricky plants, but in my experience, they're easier to grow than Darlingtonia, Cephalotus, and Heliamphora, so if you have had good luck with those aforementioned plants, Disas should be a walk in the park for you.  
  
What's the secret to growing these?  Aside from cool nights and morning sun/afternoon shade, they really have to be transplanted into new, fresh sphagnum every fall/winter or early spring.  If a plant is in decline, it's probably because the sphagnum is too decomposed.

It's crazy how you can go from having hundreds of plants and then 2 years later there's a few dozen left.  
  
Now that things have settled and I'm getting better at producing my own sphagnum (one of my excuses to not repot a few years back...big mistake!), the Disas are looking happier than ever!  When you see such big plants this time of year, you know they are going to produce some massive flower spikes!  As long as I can keep them from frost damage, next summer is going to have a nice show, so stay tuned!  
  
When I had these growing in an unheated, uncompleted  greenhouse missing a few walls in Sacramento, CA, they would continue to grow throughout the winter, which would lead to massive plants and massive blooms!  That was actually my first time ever growing disas.  Anyways,  Without a coolhouse, they pretty much stop growing leaves in the dead of winter, which limits their flower count but also gives them time to build up good sized  tubers for next year.  
  
In the past, I used to just let them tough it outside during the freezes, and while many survived, a lot died so now I stepped up my game and cover them with greenhouse plastic during cold nights.  You have to be careful because with the cover comes stagnant air, which is really a no-no for disas.  They're also covered during heavy rain because the leaves can get damaged which can cause crazy rot issues.  
  
Speaking of rot, that's the #1 killer of disas.  You'll see small black black spots at first, and they get bigger and bigger until you either chop off the leaf or the weather changes.  I've found if you keep water off of the plants during the rain plus treat the spots with dusting sulfur, you can keep rot in check without resorting to crazy chemicals that will shink your you know what....

Hmmm, if light intensity had something to do with it, why is one pot blooming and the rest, which are in identical lighting, not blooming?  There are other pots of the same exact clone growing side by side with this flowering plant that are on schedule to bloom at the normal time.    
  
I think it has more to do with when new growth started.  In some cases, during the fall, a tuber will produce a very strong plantlet, but during the spring the plant produces its last leaf and is triggered to  die back for some reason.  A new growth will emerge from that same tuber but it'll have a head start from a stolon produced at the same time of the year.  This is probably because the tuber is focusing all of its energy on that new plantlet. the new growth continues to produce leaves at full force even through the summer "dormancy" period and is pretty darn mature by the end of fall.     Come early spring, it'll flower.  Whatever caused that one growth point to  produce its last leaf and then senesce is what will be investigated.  
  
Normally, new growth starts end of July and on for us, so I think if you can get blooming sized tubers to begin new growth in the spring, you can time them to flower late winter/early next spring.  There's probably other pathways to force disas to bloom when you want them to.

`post #21` · `id=38809` · `2018-10-17 23:24:40 UTC`

bogman, totally missed your question!  
  
Yes, these do get fertilizer, but sparingly.  I fertilize them with maxsea, 1/4 strength, 1X per month only during the fall and spring when the plants are in active vegetative growth.  If you fertilize them too often during this time, they'll grow very big and fat, but as soon as it heats up, they'll almost all perish!  Slow and steady is one of the keys to keeping them alive long term, and I suspect this is why I've lost most of my clones over time.  Since these are so rot prone, it's a balancing act to feed them enough to get some nice sized blooms and replacement tubers for the next year, yet not too much such that they become susceptible to rot when the weather changes.   
  
I don't like using hardcore chemical fungicides, which has been my achille's heel with Disas.  I'd probably have much better success spraying the crap out of these all the time.  As a middle ground, I've sprayed them with dusting sulfur (mixed in water) every once in a while, and put sulfur on any spots that look like disease.  It has worked quite well so far.  The disease usually shows up in the winter months when it's rainy and cloudy for weeks on end and during the summer when it gets over 100F around here.  
  
  
Regarding flower shelf life, if kept on the plant and in a cool area, I've had flowers last over 1.5 months, it's crazy!  This is sorta misleading though, I'm talking about the life of the entire spike with multiple blooms, not an individual flower.  Main issue in flower shelf life is thrips, other insects, slugs, snails, and even sunburn if you're risky like me.  If you have them in a greenhouse, you proably won't have the challenges I have.  
  
You're spot on: they are growing with the wasabi, but with a few more hours of filtered sunlight.  Correction: the sunlight is direct when it's cold outside, but filtered when it warms up.

Disas are notorious for being susceptible to fungal diseases. Why try?"  To be honest, I probably have psychological issues, those flowers are just so damn beautiful.

Not many disa growers go into details about their failures, but I believe that killing plants is the only way to really know how to grow one!  Here's some ways to manage rot on disas:  
1) Transplant every fall.  
2) bottom water!  This will help prevent rot on the leaves and stems, but this won't remedy root rot.  
3) Give the plants a lot of time to recover from transplanting before fertilizing with very dilute fertilizer.    
4) If you saw mild symptoms of rot on a plant when transplanting, it probably can't be fertilized until that rot disappears or is no longer on the plant.  Fertilizer turns rot into a wildfire, even when in low concentrations!  
5) plants grown slow and steady are more resistant to disease than plants pumped up like Arnold Schwartznegger.  
6) most clones are just susceptible to rot, but some have higher resistance.  breeding the resistant ones is the way to go!  
7)Grow away from woodchips or other decomposing material that can sporulate.  Cement floors or inorganic surroundings are best.  I learned this the hard way: in my previous grow site, all my disas grew on cement, so rot wasn't as bad.  I grew them this time with woodchips under the trays, and afternoon pure shade+lots of watering+woodchips=disaster.  
8)So far, I've been able to beat rot by moving them into a makeshift shelter that is located in full sun.  The shelter has 75% shade, so the plants get bright, indirect light all day long.  Bright, indirect light, along with great air circulation makes all the difference in the world, but it won't necessarily stop rot.  Along with changing the envionment, I added copious amounts of sulfur to all infected parts of the plant. Pathogens, bah humbug! Yes, spores float or can splash, wash in, or arrive on the butt of a frog 

![:o](//storage.proboards.com/forum/images/smiley/shocked.png) [not mirrored]

 .  
  
The presence of the pathogen is either growing or resting (spores, sclerotia, etc.) or both. The resting stages are the most difficult to control, but easier to mechanically remove by washing, discarding media, disinfectants, et al. The only other alternative is to use fungicides. These can either attack spores/resting stages, as is often the case with Mancozeb, or the germinating spores get attacked, which may be what the sulfur is doing. Sulfur could also kill active fungi if damaged tissues allow osmotic absorption. It could also be lowering the pH outside of the pathogen's tolerance range.  
  
Once you see damage, chances are the fungus is now inside the plant tissue(s). At this stage, suppression is a time bomb. Your choices are: to pitch the plant(s) or infected plant parts (e.g. leaves), change the environment to a point where the pathogen dies off because it cannot tolerate the environment, establish a biological control, or use systemic fungicides.  
  
Eventually, if the pathogen is suppressed long enough, it can die off. I was given some Heliamphora, years ago, with Phytophthora showing up shortly after potting them up. Situation: systemic infection with unknown spore, zoospore status.   
  
Remedy: Isolate infected plants. Treat with Subdue fungicide to halt or kill disease. **After** a week, when pathogen is pretty much static or dead, put on nitrile gloves, move plants outdoors, to a remote area and discard soil, wash plants and pots, remove infected areas, as possible, and toss the pots into a 10% bleach solution. Carefully remove gloves and trash them. This will greatly reduce or eliminate any resting stages, especially if cleaning is thorough. As the fungicide has suppressed or killed the pathogen, new spores aren't being generated. The cycle is disrupted.  
  
The original growing area was sprayed with disinfectants: first Greenshield (quat. salts); after drying, some hours later, 10% bleach. Never mix the aforementioned disinfectants. Follow all label directions.   
  
Freshly repotted plants were returned to the original growing area, vs. risking spreading the problem. Plants were treated regularly with systemic fungicides for about eight months. After that time, I stopped treatment and kept an eye on the plants. It has now been over ten years without the pathogen and fungicides aren't used unless I'm overdue to repot and the plants are very crowded, a precautionary measure.  
  
This was a lot of work, but Heliamphora were very rare and valuable at the time, not easily replaced. The point is it is possible to get rid the pathogen. Without fungicides, the challenge is great. A biological fungicide, or group of these, may be useful. I'd experiment on some spare plants, to determine tolerance to disinfectant quat. salts, which many orchids tolerate. That can help eliminate resting stages, germinating spores and surface infections. Figure disinfectants will kill sphagnum. If the plants can tolerate being bare rooted and soaked in disinfectant, at least you know the plant's surface will likely be clean. After this, or a regular wash and repot, that would be the time to start introducing biologicals.  
  
If stem infections are the principal infection site, I'd start there. Pull moss away from the stem, crown and replace it with something which dries out quickly or does not stay as wet. Do you keep the stem and foliage dry when watering? Many rabbit holes you have. Many questions there are.

The wood chips may be upping the humidity, but it's unlikely it's harboring pathogens, unless they're washed-in and just sitting there. Saprophytes tend to colonize chips and many are antagonistic to pathogens. It's possible slugs or insects in the mulch (e.g.Collembola) can move around spores. But there may be another cultural element to consider: concrete tends to get colder at night and has almost no insulating value. The best high elevation Heliamphora I grew, years ago, were pots sitting on cold concrete.  
  
A fast kill time smacks of Phytophthora, doesn't it? Since at least one, if not two pathogens have set up shop there, I'd go with the fungicides. The trusty Zyban (1 TBS/gal) + Subdue (7-8 drops/gal.) might be a good experiment. You could do better by separating the chemicals, Subdue drench and Zyban "sprench". Follow that 24 hours later with a pure water flush. Test a couple spares/duplicates first. These will work best over 65 degrees F, 70-75 F would be perfect. If the plants improve, repeat a couple times (about every 4 weeks), carefully wash and repot on normal cycle, sanitize the area when you repot and introduce biologicals after repotting. At that point, the pathogen(s) may be gone or greatly diminished. Email me if that's a path you wish to explore.  
  
Sarracenia often have a startling quantity of endophytes, biologicals woven between intercellular tissues. Plus, their temperature tolerances, frozen to 120 F+ means some of their season is spent outside most pathogens' comfort zone. Full sun (UV) tends to waste a lot of spores, too. Might be interesting to see if the Sarracenia endophytes can cross over into Disas; you never know!  
  
Instead of bottom watering, maybe watering 2-3 corners, away from the crown, would be better. The thicker and longer-lasting a water film is, the easier it is for many pathogens to spread and establish. In our climate, for example, bottom watering often leads to mature Sarracenia death. As a crazy humid, historically agricultural region (since before Thomas Jefferson), humans built a nauseating assortment of pathogens into the environment. Fortunately, microbes that eat pathogens are also present. In the war of microbes, it's often small tweaks in culture that favor one or more of the combatants.
