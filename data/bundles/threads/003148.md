Much of the okee giants you see today in cultivation are from the Ware Co, GA population, which comes in all sorts of different shapes, colors, and sizes.  They'll consistently get large year after year (around 2.5'), but to get them giant (3+feet tall) in cultivation is a challenge.  There was one year where we had an Indian summer and every last clone of mine from Ware Co, GA got to about 3' tall.  Of course, there are exceptions to the rule, and some  individual clones from Ware Co, GA do get gigantic consistently (ie. Phil Faulisi's clone). There's also many European clones that may perform better than the ones we have here in the US.  
  
From what I've observed, it seems the biggest pitcher come from spring growth, at least when grown outdoors in full sun here in Northern California.  Of course, as mentioned earlier, when we had a warm summer and fall, they produced the biggest traps during that time.    
  
At this point in time, the Charlton Co, GA population seems to be putting on an amazing performance.  For one, I've found some individuals with not quite an inch long rhizome that produced over a foot tall pitcher last year!  This spring, almost every last individual plant has produced over a foot tall pitcher.  I've never seen this in the Ware Co, GA population, and only time will tell if these clones get gigantic year after year under optimal conditions.  I suspect they do!

Who knows what all the variables are: extra photosynthesis, nutrients, spring insect capture. We always cut ours to 3 inch stubs, once the subfreezing temperatures have mostly killed off the tops. Otherwise, we'd have a rotting, moldy mess and ideal vole habitat. Most everyone who knows me understands my dislike for voles; last thing I'd want to do is make a hideout of Sarracenia pitchers for them. They'd eat up all the plants, maybe some of the pressure-treated wood and in their ravenous rampage, move on to bigger things.  
  
I was in my Chevy '98, with the motor off, reading the owner's manual for oil specs (diesel). I kept hearing a weird scratching kinda noise and next thing I knew, the rear driver-side tire was deflating. I looked out the window and a herd of voles were chewing the tire up, so the vehicle would be low enough for them to hop in and attack!

Sorry, lost focus.. Perhaps one reason we don't see much health/size loss by cutting fall pitchers is the volume of insect capture during all seasons. Pitchers here routinely fill up with insects. Thanks for asking Jgreen1025!  I think the easiest way to describe it is, all else equal, the more room the roots have to grow, the bigger the plants can get!    Of course, there's way more factors to it than that: you can't expect all your plants to get gigantic just by giving them an enormous amount of soil to grow in, they need the right genetics and environmental conditions to do that.

[Sept 14, 2021 22:10:26 GMT -5](/post/47051/thread) [meizzwang](/user/11) said:

.....the more room the roots have to grow, the bigger the plants can get ......provided the right environmental conditions are met.     

  
  
I second this 100% having used this principle for 25 years. In my own collection all plants will get as large as they can when the roots are given room to run in adequate soil volume. If it is a single growth point, give it room. If it's a large genet, give it room. Especially the upright ones which naturally have more extensive root systems. More roots = more rhizome growth point development and larger leaves. The main roots from the rhizome will be thick and white with a vigorous downstream network. The individual plant will let you know with sulky size when it hasn't got enough room. Watch it boost away when it has.  
  
Seedlings are perfect example also. Keep them moving year to year into more space to develop roots. Mature flowering plants will be the result in short order.  
  
Combine this with lots of insects as Mike says for a winning situation. Insect volume is not a problem for outside grown collections and, in the feeding sense, a real advantage compared with glass houses.
