`post #4` · `id=31847` · `2017-03-30 22:18:55 UTC`

update on Darlingtonia cultivation: every last one of my plants died!!!  I have since started over and haven't had any issues (yet) because it's been very cold.     
  
One of the biggest challenges with growing Darlingtonia in climates where you have warm summers is maintaining the soil temperature below 80F. From my experience, It only takes one warm sunny day to kill all of your plants (which you'll find out about 3 weeks later).    On the other hand, Darlingtonia LOVE full sun, but only when you can keep the soil temperatures cool.   
  
 Others overseas have shown that these plants can be grown even when soil temps get relatively warm, but multiple years of testing suggests that doesn't work here outdoors in Northern California.  Growers overseas can't explain why these results can't be repeated.  
  
The late real master grower, Jeff Wong, had a mature Darlingtonia plant thriving on his front porch (with lots of direct sunlight) in San Francisco for perhaps decades.  San Francisco rarely gets above 80F during our hot summers, it's typically cloudy during the summer/sunny during the winter, and it's pretty much always cold there.  Where I am south of San Francisco, if you cloned jeff's front porch spot (same number of hours of direct sunlight) you'll end up with a dead plant.   
  
  I have  personally witnessed wild plants growing in 102F air temps, and the water felt warm, it was certainly higher than 80F.  However, Every last plant was stressed (roots appeared to have died on many plants that were still hanging on), quite a few large mother plants had died while stolon daughter plants were okay.  Tops of the traps had sunburn on many of the plants.    
  
I always hear people trying to study plants in the wild to figure out how to grow them, but I think doing this will be less fruitful than going home and finding out what kills them!  It's counter-intuitive, but the more you kill, the better you understand how to grow them.   
  
Anyways, during the hot, stressful late summer/early fall months, I place them in shade, but the morning sun (well, until about noon) is hot enough to get the temps above 80F, this was my fatal mistake.  To make things more complicated, plants became infested with powdery mildew from being in lower light levels/cool, humid climate!  
  
Here's the plan, and I'm hoping it'll work:  
1) Place Darlingtonias in full sun until about early afternoon, when they'll get shade as long as the air temperature doesn't go above 75F.  This basically during the spring and fall for us in Northern California.  
2)  During the summer when air temperatures go above 80F, not only will they be moved to morning sun/afternoon shade, but also I'm going to have them under shade cloth.  I think this might make it cool enough to survive the summer heat.  
3) to combat powdery mildew, they will frequently be sprayed with elemental dusting sulfur (technically, sulfur can be used for organic growing).    
4) Water-wise these will be grown like Lilium catesbaei and Disa orchids: no standing water, but kept moist at all times.  
5) gonna grow them in pure peat moss.  Others have had better success in sphagnum, but my moss stash is reserved for the disas, so the Darlings will just have to learn to put up with that.  
  
  
It's ironic that my Disas survived heat waves and extreme neglect, I always thought Darlingtonia were tougher, but even ones that were thought to be more heat tolerant croaked.  When you start seeing rot wipe out your plants, it's not because you exposed your plants to fungal pathogens (they're pretty much always present in our envirnment), you have an environmental problem that triggers the fungal infections to thrive!  However, once rot kicks in, you have to be very diligent to eradicate it because even when the environment turns for the better, a high population of "innoculant" will still wipe out your plants.  
  
More to come with photos, just thought I'd share these thoughts while I'm day-dreaming about transplanting my Darlingtonias!

#### **Mike Wang** (`@meizzwang`)
`post #7` · `id=32162` · `2017-04-03 20:34:59 UTC`

I suspect with peat moss, there's a lower tolerance for high soil temps, but with a looser soil mix (ie. pumice, lava rock, sphagnum moss, orchid bark, fern fiber, etc), it'll be interesting to see if they can tolerate warm temps better.  
  
I wouldn't discount having deeper soil as a possible solution.  the larger the volume of hydrated soil you have, the better the heat capacity (ie. it takes a lot more energy to heat up a larger volume of hydrated substrate than it does to heat up a smaller volume of hydrated substrate ).  
  
Nevertheless, I tried filling tubs up with peat moss (approx. 1.5 cu. ft of soil), but even with such a large volume, it still got too hot.  When we're talking about large volumes needed to keep things cool, we're talking about bog sized or koi pond volumes, at least here in Northern Cali climates.  Still, nothing beats finding a location that stays cool all year round: if you can find that spot, all of this nonsense is not needed.  This is why I've built a makeshift shade cloth shelter in my morning sun/afternoon shade spot!    
  
Dozens of growers by the coast where it rarely gets above 80F don't bother with any cooling shenanigans, large pots, or special substrates....

#### *dj57*
`post #9` · `id=32174` · `2017-04-04 03:33:30 UTC`

When I grew these I found that having a layer of live moss as a top dressing helped keep the soil from heating up too much for them. I also think an airy soil mix is beneficial to allow adequate aeration. The ones I had in the bog surrounded by moss did okay, but the ones I had in a pond setup did better with constant water flow keeping the roots aerated and no soil compacting around the roots. These were planted in pure sphagnum on top of pea gravel and grew in full sun.   
  
Mike is right in that, at least for some species, you have to use the "trial and error" method (kill stuff) to learn how best to grow them in your particular conditions. And when you think you have it all figured out, there will be those weird weather years that will come along and upset (maybe kill off) the whole lot. You don't necessarily need to start over from scratch, just make adjustments or tweak what you already know works. With Darlingtonia especially there is no one way that works for most, much is dependent on the particular conditions/climate you have to work with. I live in the PNW, vastly different conditions than central coastal and southern California.

I've grown Darlingtonia a couple ways, always with a shallow water layer near the root tips. One method was to reverse dormancy and refrigerate the plant in Live sphagnum fro about June–Oct. 1. Then when nights were cool and my basement stayed cool, the plants were grown under florescent light, lots of it. the plants routinely got 20 inches tall.  
  
When I moved into a rustic cabin, I had to give them away. Now, I'm working on a more heat tolerant strain. Only a couple plants survived out of hundreds of seedlings. A friend used a thermometer to collect seed, years ago, from plants growing in the warmest soil/water. So far, these have survived rank heat and humidity in Virginia, in a greenhouse! They grow super slowly and the plan is to treat them with preventative fungicide and move the two seedlings to a temperature controlled lab, where they'll be much cooler.  
  
Then, IF seed can be produced, it'll go through the same selective heat pressure as the parents.  
  
I always aerate the media and water when watering, but it's best to avoid wetting the crown. The petioles can collect water and provide a great spot for pathogens to get started.

Lots of inaccurate information is circulating the web, so here's some clarification on color forms.  One grower mentioned that they think there isn't any  genetic component to the plants coloring up, it's all environmental.  Experiments, observations in the wild,  and pictures below suggest otherwise!    
  
  Even in the wild, the most amazing red color forms, like those at the alpine farms site: [sarracenia.proboards.com/thread/940/red-darlingtonias-wild-del-norte](https://sarracenia.proboards.com/thread/940/red-darlingtonias-wild-del-norte)  don't typically color up all the way  until the fall.  If you clicked that link, check out the very first picture showing red variants growing side by side with regular green variants.  Assuming that all else is equal, the hypothesis is that the solid red ones colored up while the green ones didn't due to genetic differences.  
  
If you look at the same red color forms during the spring/summer in the wild, they're indistinguishable from the red color forms!    In cultivation, it's no different when these plants are grown outdoors: all of the plants look pretty much the same until the fall!   That said,  under LED lighting that has the light spectrum artificially maximized for anthocyanin production or under greenhouse conditions, it is possible to get these genetically red variants to color up all season long!  
  
  
So here's some evidence that suggests the red coloration is genetic.  In front, those red plants are D. californica 'red like alpine farms' Del norte Co, CA, while behind them, the rows of green plants are are regular form from Rattlesnake Creek, Josephine Co, OR!  Literally, everything is identical: soil, temperature, light, nutrients, water, airflow, etc:  
  

![](../../images/d1/d19bac43c1da197010b5a8d9542120a0080825309e509ebc2f66f26917aa6b94.jpg)

  
  

![](../../images/f6/f6e5f2d6ec760428e016877726982f566a2b36f9421f36b08cb60eae1eba7c36.jpg)

  
  

![](../../images/d7/d7563ef6126fa19c9db79b4a0a3b11c2ac49d60534e51ccbd4aa7202c2c139e5.jpg)

  
  
Sure, if you look carefully, some traps from rattlesnake creek have reddish heads, but none have consistently solid red bodies like the 'red like alpine farms' variant does!

As long as you can keep the soil cool at ALL times, these plants are relatively easy.  That pretty much means they're quite challenging for most, including me:
