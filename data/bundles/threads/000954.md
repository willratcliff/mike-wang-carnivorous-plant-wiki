Darlingtonias typically grow on hillside seeps high in the rocky, nutrient poor mountains.  If they're found in a forest, the trees are usually either stunted or dying due to the harsh environmental conditions.  However, one site has been discovered in the middle of the forest, which is a completely anomalous place to find cobra lilies.  For a cool video and even more photos of darlingtonias in the wild, check out Rob Co's famous The Pitcher Plant Project: [thepitcherplantproject.com/blog/2013/10/27/darlingtonia-trip-2013-day-1/](http://thepitcherplantproject.com/blog/2013/10/27/darlingtonia-trip-2013-day-1/)   
  
   
  
I visited this site in 2010, and according to Damon Collinsworth of California Carnivores and Harry Tryon, a local Darlingtonia expert, the site looked a lot worse than it did approx. 10 years ago.    Back then, they recalled the site was a lot more open, and is now getting shadier and shadier.  
  
Three years after visiting this site, my good friend Rob Co of the pitcher plant project and I revisited this site, and I was expecting "sarracenia doom and gloom."  Was this site completely shaded out now, and how are the plants doing?  
  
  
To my surprise, the site looked even better than it did in 2010!  I actually have some shots that I took in 2010, and you can compare them to 2013!

#### **Mike Wang** (`@meizzwang`)
`post #3` · `id=4200` · `2013-10-28 17:30:10 UTC`

Great question Calen.  This sphagnum site doesn't have any serpentine, which would kill the sphagnum immediately, even in small amounts.  The presence of serpentine is the reason why Sphagnum is so rare in in the wild at these sites.  Also, the forest doesn't ever grow so thick and tall when there's serpentine...it's too toxic for them to thrive.
