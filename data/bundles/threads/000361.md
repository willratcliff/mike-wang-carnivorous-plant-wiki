`post #5` · `id=1527` · `2012-08-23 22:23:30 UTC`

As the person who supplied the plant in the photos. I can add some information that may clarify and reinforce the things meizwang has said - particularly with respect to queries out there that exist as to the plants correct varietal name. This is indeed a *S.flava* var. *atropurpurea* and a genuine Carolina one at that.  
  
1) The plant in the photos is a asexual division of the plant below  
  

![](http://i128.photobucket.com/albums/p197/JayBeNineteen/CPs%20NOV%202010/IMG_4266LargeMedium.jpg) [broken]

  
  
2) The plant has been grown in New Zealand for over 30 years. It was imported initially by the Christchurch Botanic Gardens as a live specimen supplied from the USA. Subsequently, Christchurch Botanic Gardens ceased cultivating Sarracenia and their material, including this var. *atropurpurea*, was passed on to a private grower during the 1980s. After a short period that grower also wound up their Sarracenia cultivation and the atropurpurea was obtained by D. Gray in 1987. The clone has remained in the care of Gray and only two other people (one being me) and has been maintained as pure since it first arrived in the country all those years ago.  
  
3) As a feature of New Zealand's second largest city, Christchurch Botanic Gardens was founded in 1863 and throughout its history acquired many exotic species. While details are not able to be confirmed, it is known that the *atropurpurea* clone was imported from the USA with it almost certain to have been a specimen collected from the wild given both the status of the gardens and the the fact so many plants were collected that way during that period.  
  
4) The plant is a Carolina *S.flava* var. *atropurpurea* as distinct from the western Florida named "atropurpurea". The application of the varietal name to those significantly different plants, particularly var *rubricorpora* clones that eventually become fully infused, is the subject of conjecture. Verification that the clone pictured is a Carolina *atropurpurea* comes via the following:  
  
a. It matches Don Schnell's original description of Carolina *S.flava* var *atropurpurea* based on field observations perfectly []()[]()  
  
b. The clone is clearly of the same variety, both in colour and form, as wild *atropurpurea* native to Green Swamp, North Carolina featured in the invaluable photography of Jim Fowler (as referred to by meizwang above), they too matching Schnell's description. Particular note should be giving to the newly opened pitchers (as the ones posted here by meizwang and myself are) such as this one []()[]() , the form of the mature pitchers and, notably the veining on the column which is almost identical, a blueprint in effect (see meizwang's photos). Take the time to look through all Fowler's images of the variety.  
  
5) To address questions commonly asked elsewhere as to which variety this is, the clone is in no sense a *S.flava* var *rubricorpora* based on the on the description and observations of that variety in the field:  
  
a. Grown optimally, pitchers of this *atropurpurea* clone never have a yellow hood at any stage throughout their growth cycle. Prior to opening, that portion of the leaf that will become the hood is always red. This can be seen in both Fowler's Green Swamp field images and meizwang's here.  
  
b. The form of pitchers of the clone are representative of the *S.flava* varieties that exist in the Carolinas. The pitchers are quite different from the Florida *rubricorpora* throughout the colour variability of that variety, including fully infused all red individuals that begin life as any other *rubricorpora* but that are then accorded the distinction of being two things at once in being named "atropurpurea" as colour fills in.  
  
c. A feature of var *rubricorpora*, both in the field and in cultivation, is the low number of pitchers produced per growth point per season. This is well documented and a justifiably definable feature of *rubricorpora* that sets it apart from other *S.flava*. The *atropurpurea* clone discussed here exhibits the typically vigorous growth pattern of the related Carolina varieties as can be seen in the images of it.  
  
I believe the misidentification of var *rubricorpora* as "var atropurpurea" readily occurs and incorrect classification has become widely accepted as a result.  
  
I suggest that rare *S.flava* var *atropurpurea* as found in the Carolinas and represented by this particular clone is a distinct variety, as it always has been for a number of reasons - not least geographical isolation of ~700 miles from the highly variable var *rubricorpora* - and that the varietal name should apply to it alone. And further, that the name *S.flava* var *atropurpurea *should reflect this geographical isolation, as it does in reality for other populations within Sarracenia species, rather than reflecting a blanket reliance on colour alone as a determining factor.

good question.  I think the official description for atropurpurea is bogus: the variety doesn't breed true in general, and the color of certain clones can be drastically different depending on environmental conditions.  Officially giving a color variant a latin name isn't very helpful from a botanical perspective, but it's very valuable for growers.  
  
For horticultural purposes, I typically define any flava species, true to location, that can turn solid red from head to toe (excluding the interior of the mouth), an atropurpurea.  However, when you start breeding cupreas and getting atropurpureas, or rubricorporas with atropurpureas to make "atropurpueras," it's a grey area as to what you should call such hybrids.  I'm fine with them being called atros, so long as the breeder describes the parentage. That's critical to discern genuine atropurpureas true to location versus man made all red flavas. Taxonomically there should be a description for separate subspecies or varieties denoting the Atlantic coastal from the Gulf coastal flava populations due to the genetic and physical differences between them, and if described further (and would still only be useful horticulturally since they're too polymorphic to have an actual taxonomic basis) the various color forms on the spectrum should be unique to either location (atropurpurea was originally described as being a Carolina-only plant, and should remain such; dark plants from Florida etc. are just the extreme end of rubricorpora). "var. cuprea" has no identifiable shape to it as different variants of that color form occur in the spectrum between "var. flava" and "atropurpurea" in the Carolinas, and "var. rugelii" and "rubricorpora" in Florida, just as with "var. ornata" (and similarly "var. maxima" occurs in both regions, and has different shapes based on those regions). The initial yellow internal color of 'Waccamaw' was part of the original description of the "var. atropurpurea" variant, not a sign that it's not an atro. the dark rubricorpora that are now labeled as Florida "atropurpurea" also sometimes start out with yellow internally, but age out of that rapidly.

The subspecies should be the entire subpopulation of flava on either coastal region, which most certainly have distinct genetic and physical characteristics. Because they don't always (or even often) breed out reliably, naming of various color forms is not seen in basically anything other than plants; people track localities or give labels like "high red," "striped," "red flame" etc. but they're not named with any official label. The various color forms in flava really shouldn't be treated different, they only are because of the horticultural influence on botanical taxonomy.  
S. flava atropurpurea, rubricorpora, etc. do breed true, in the sense that they're only going to produce plants representative of whatever location they originated, signifying cohesive supopulation polymorphism, that they're just part of a coloration spectrum.

`post #41` · `id=44250` · `2020-08-25 02:26:02 UTC`

If "atropurpurea" is restricted to plants that have an interior coloration of "pale tan" and cannot include colors of yellow, green, etc. there might be, then, a dozen clones in existence altogether, if that, that match such a description; not only inaccurate (and the description for cuprea would not fit 'Waccamaw' anyway either as it's described as at best the upper quarter of the pitcher only can develop red color, while that plant certainly can and does turn red from top to bottom, including the interior at times), but also yet another example of why polymorphic color variation alone holds no bearing on reliable taxonomic naming. Geographic separation and thereby the genetic differentiation between the two populations are more valid for naming criteria alongside the morphologic differences those genes produce, and as has been discussed elsewhere in this forum if the red plants in the Atlantic plain vs. the Gulf plain had different origins (ex. purpurea venosa vs. rosea ancient outcrossing lending the high anthocyanin genes), they neither should be nor validly can be called the same variant even if at a glance they look similar. Red forms have not been recorded in populations between, nor any callback to them as far as I've yet seen (unless anyone's got deep red plants from the middle of Georgia somewhere?), so greater indication they didn't originate as one and spread out, so the northern version should be under one name, the southern under another, if any name at all, rather than picking different versions of red plants from the two and lumping them together under one name.   
Also the description for rubricorpora doesn't cover everything now called rubricorpora (plenty of plants with green/lemon yellow or solid red interiors with yellow lids, rather than "buff yellow"), cuprea can't cover anything with truly red lids, rugelii can't cover any plants that don't have notably oversized lids or big mouths, and all the plants that have only moderate veining but no red throat patch can't be designated under var. flava if we're going to nitpick the names in the same way that people have a problem with 'Waccamaw' being an atropurpurea here.

Personally, I share hcarlton's position in respect of recognition of the significant geographical distance demarcation between what I would call populations, even if they are the same species (which DNA ultimately resolves). As hcarlton says, this defines them as having a status of difference. Not least if they are to be defined by their appearance. Perhaps leopards are good example. Although both are *Panthera pardus*, the sub-Saharan African leopard, *P. pardus. pardus*, (including its melanistic form) and the ultra-rare Amur leopard, *P. pardus. orientalis*, of cold-climate northern China and eastern Russia are separated as sub-species due to geographical demarcation and morphological differences. Clearly, both are genetically leopards but their appearance - not least in terms of coat length - and habitat are discernibly different. Yes, the distances apart in the respective cases of the two leopards and S. flava of Florida and the Carolinas are vastly different. But both are cases of actual current demarcation (apart from historical connection) without overlap; fundamentally there is a similarity in circumstances. Yes, both Florida and Carolina plants in question are *S. flava*. But from the degree of longstanding and ongoing debate on what is or isn't an "atropurpurea" it appears there is in some in the Sarracenia fanciers' world a difficultly to allow the recognition of this sort of geographical and morphological difference in the case of *S. flava* when the plants in question present in red.  
  
In the end, I suspect there will be no resolution and commonality for a variety (no pun intended) of reasons. Not a problem. Atropurpurea, rubricorpora, cuprea etc are not taxonomical terms. People are free to use what they wish. Some adhere to the subjectively named variety descriptions published by others who share the CP interest. As ever though, descriptions are just that, not hard and fast and always open to conjecture. Whilst published books, for example, are very influential one must always hold open that published descriptions are fallible as time marches on and understanding of organisms develops. Natural history is replete with descriptions that no longer apply. In the case of our rightly revered champion of carnivorous plant observations, Don Schnell's descriptions were amended by him over time, notably during his collaboration with McPherson in the publication of that author's popular carnivorous plant volumes.  
  
One thing is certain: [Jim Folwer's 2008 photos of red S. flava in the Green Swamp of North Carolina](https://www.pbase.com/jimfowler/image/96987099) that he, as the man who stood before them, deemed were Bell/Schnell's "atropurpurea" are invaluable gold. The similarity to S. 'Waccamaw' is strikingly obvious. And, hey, (although clearly related to the proximal predominantly yellow form) not a "rugelii" or "rubricorpora" to be seen...
