While variegation is rather common in orchids and many other plants, it's pretty rare in Sarracenias.  Jerry Addington has a variegated judith hindle, which was created from a mutation invitro.  When you divide those cells so many times and produce so many plants from the same genetic material, it's just a matter of time until you get some mutation.

#### **Mike Wang** (`@meizzwang`)
`post #15` · `id=7732` · `2014-05-20 19:42:38 UTC`

sflguy, that photo was taken at Jerry Addington's place at Courting Frog's Nursery.    
  
EDIT: I think those "chimeras" shown in the picture above are from pesticide use.  In the first pictures in this thread, that variegation came about from sexual reproduction.     
  
Anyways, when I grew miniature roses commercially, we had a clone called 'Alto," which was solid peach. We continuously propagated them via cuttings.  After making literally millions of cuttings over the years, one branch mutated and had both peach and red colors (bicolor), so we called it 'alto amour.'  We cloned the heck out of that one branch, and some of them reverted back to the solid peach color.  We quickly flowered all of the cuttings and weeded out the ones that reverted back, and kept only the bicolor cuttings.  After probably 10 generations of selective "weeding" we finally got a stable mutation that consistently only produced the bicolor rose.
