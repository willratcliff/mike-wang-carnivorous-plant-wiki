One of the most exciting trips I've ever been on  was a trip to Cordova, Alaska in August 2009.  It was during the beginning of the recession, so very few people were up there and we had the entire bay to ourselves!  You have to fly everywhere in pontoon planes, and we rented cabins that were isolated and far from the city.    
  
Of the many cabins that we moved around to, the best one had a private beach front with a view of islands and the ocean.  Behind the cabin was an extensive bog which I spent a lot of time exploring.  There's a lot more to this alaska trip, but for now I'll focus on the bogs since that's what's most relevant to the fellow plant nerds here

`post #10` · `id=31438` · `2017-03-16 22:42:22 UTC`

for sure man, hit me up anytime!  Whatever you do, don't Ace yourself!  I acquired most of my mushroom hunting knowledge from spending the winters picking with a commercial picker when I was a kid and nerds at UC Davis.  
  
There's a story of a very experienced European mushroom hunter dude visiting North America and harvesting/cooking a mushroom that looked identical to the ones he picked at home his whole life (in the genus Russula).  He picked them and got poisoned, but I'm pretty sure it wasn't a fatal mistake.  This was a hardcore mushroom expert we're talking about, and it turns out he ID'ed them right, but the strain in Europe is edible, while the strain here is poisonous!  For this very reason, I can only advise pickers on the pacific northwest, since these are the genetic I really know and have consumed for a long time.  NEVER been poisoned, only got some serious gas from the oysters.  
  
  There are also some that are edible but only if cooked well; they'll poison you if you eat them raw and in large quantities (ie. morels, blewits).  There's even one (Coprinus comatus, the shaggy mane) that is totally edible and harmless, but if you drink alcohol the day of or next few days, or had alcohol in your system from a few days before, you can get really sick.  The compound, coprinin, mixes with ethanol and produces a compound toxic to humans.  Needless to say, I've never tried the shaggy mane (never know when a homie will show up with a brown bag and want to get their drank on) even though it fruits like crazy here all the time.   
  
As mentioned earlier, there's a very common edible mushroom that grows wild here called the oyster mushroom, and it's also commercially cultivated.  I have been eating them since I was 16 and there are poisonous look-alikes so you have to be careful.  One year, my entire family started getting stomach cramps from eating oysters, and I was 100% sure on the ID, so I went back to the bag to check out the mushrooms.  Turns out, if you harvest them when too over-grown, they become harder to digest because the flesh becomes more chitinous (same shizzle that insect exoskeletons and crab/lobster shells are made of).  You can only eat them up to a certain stage of development without giving your guts a roller coaster ride.  But get them at the right stage and HOLY LUIGI!  They're soooo damn flavorful.  The wild oysters have WAY more complex, intense flavors compared to cultivated ones.  
  
 I've seen yellow Chanterelles growing in Santa Rosa Co,  Florida (about 1/4 mile away from a leucophylla bog) and I'm 100% sure they're Chanterelles, but they were left alone cuz I thought of that European dude.  It was weird seeing them fruit in September when it was nearly 100F during the day and 76F at night:here on the west coast, it has to be cold and rainy for a while before they pop.  There are risky mushroom hunters, but not many old, risky mushroom hunters.   
  
There's one genus that I shy away from even though most of the times I'm 90% sure it's all good: Agaricus.  Crimini and the white button mushrooms are in the genus Agaricus.  There's just way too many poison look alikes, but there's one species, Agaricus augustus, that's so distinctive that I'll munch those.  Others like Agaricus albolutescens (which I did eat after having an expert ID it) I still don't eat when I find them even though I have in the past:it's just too sketchy, and the look-alikes are very hard to discern.  But damn man, they taste like toasted almonds, it's amazing!

From the wiki page: [en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morchella](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morchella)   
  
"Toxicity  
Morchella [Yee Home slice, these are also known as morels] species contain small amounts of hydrazine[73] toxins that are destroyed through cooking; because of this, morels should never be eaten raw.[74] It has been reported that even cooked morels can sometimes cause symptoms of upset stomach when consumed with alcohol.[75]  
  
When eating this fungus for the first time it is wise to consume a small amount to minimize any allergic reaction. As with all fungi, morels for consumption must be clean and free of decay. Morels growing in old apple orchards that had been treated with the insecticide lead arsenate may accumulate levels of toxic lead and arsenic that are unsuitable for human consumption.[76]"

finally got some more pictures uploaded!  I'd like to take you on a tour of what we saw in Alaska, there's a couple of videos of us "poppin caps" while intoxicated from consuming too much ETOH, but that has been omitted since I'm supposed to be a good role model here.  There's also a lot of inappropriate language, and now that Ellie is able to cruise through the internet (yes, they grow up fast!), it's prolly best to omit those videos.  Ahem, anyways, here we go!  
  
These are the pontoon planes that are commonly used to get to places that you can't drive to.  You first fly up there in a regular commercial airplane, but then you drive to another "airport" (aka lakes) and take off from the dock.  Much of alaska doesn't have roads, so you have to fly to your destination:  

![](../../images/81/8121be7d959fd570745a5f98e22d58204bf44d5e3252a3ad059d929e5af70f4b.jpg)

  
  
  
The engines are pretty loud, so they have you wear these earphones so you can talk to all tha homies and pilot while flying.  No, contrary to what many may think, it's not just a fashion statement like when you see those rappers and musicians glamorously wearing such attire:  

![](../../images/7b/7ba274670384cbc34e835069ecb4ea504f7a78b37791b78027c19031c90f181d.jpg)

  
  
A view of Cordova, Alaska from the air!    

![](../../images/2a/2adb67a3b4f8a8120f5d9430de10a22bd0a1c823c2925baf47379a3bd28446ad.jpg)

  
  
  
The scenery up there is absolutely breath-taking everywhere you go.  It's rather "pimp" if you want to take your significant other up there, but it's also mildly risky because it's one of the most wild places I've ever been to.   Literally, we were out there and didn't see any people for several days:  

![](../../images/bf/bfd74fe41f0e89d6f8403fb63ee0092f23e1a731d2d126331b7dde43f8be3fee.jpg)

  
  
  
We stayed in 2 cabins, and this is one of them-the floating cabin!  This spot was crazy fun: we had 2 boats, full kitchen, and several bunk beds.  It's like sleeping on a boat, except a bit more gentle since there's not much wake going on up there.  It normally rains so often that the roof collects enough rainwater that you can use for showers, washing dishes, etc.   

![](../../images/76/764572af86544292f0f763db73b8db56c56086b60b696e92fd7c46d21399fad8.jpg)

  
  
  
View from the front yard of the floating cabin, you could sit here for hours and not get bored!  It's just that beautiful up there.  The body of water is a bay connected to the ocean, so as the tide changes throughout the day, you can literally see tons of fish and other sea life swim by:  

![](../../images/91/913bc779ee8e90c6e9b6760b36ce44f6066946ed5b1e0cc081743624218f923c.jpg)

  
  
Again, the wildlife entertainment up there is relentless, we saw quite a few bears check us out.  Of course, you always have to be on your toes when up in the wilderness of Alaska, nobody will hear you and there isn't help for hundreds of miles:  

![](../../images/bf/bfe6f7d7ff9128c0f517c19507f0f8ccfc1b8c9fcee5203e4b0bd908b9ff4045.jpg)

  
  
A view of the bay upsteam.  A river formed by glacier melt feeds into the bay, and when the tide is in, you can take your boat up the river (it's like a calm lake).  Thing is, if the tide is out, it's a raging river, so you can't go upstream:  

![](../../images/4b/4beddf7a1e06f4b85d11680a0769d0e4fdbff8145a3cd13caa925704cfc258c8.jpg)

![](../../images/61/613558ecd14415567d28e76d065ec67d5abb46bc16172ec9990db07704bbd4bb.jpg)

![](../../images/c6/c6da51bf23b2adcf5f48aec8489e40dff21cb30fcae6cd5002a576e51c615b88.jpg)

![](../../images/49/49bc7cfca1229e82be9bd03a5a4e7eff8344d8fd79e6d3474b5d7d29b1a3dfee.jpg)

  
  
Oh, and the salmon fishing up there was crazy amazing!  You could literally be sitting on the porch of the floating cabin and then a giant school of them would cruise by.  However, they're way more difficult to catch than you think, it took us a while to figure out how to get them to bite:  

![](../../images/a0/a0c37439a0abae55c643d3da9c5586b036b5ba46bc09fb0d176d3f6e0816344a.jpg)

  
  
There were these really cute minks all over the place!  Most wildlife out there is timid and will run away from you, but not these little guys.  In fact, as I was taking this picture, the mink ran up all the way to my shoe and started stepping on me.  The second I moved, it made a shrieking noise and ran away.  This is when I learned the hard way that minks are in the skunk family: the little bugger sprayed me!  It wasn't as bad as a skunk, but it didn't smell like roses neither:

We made some amazing cioppino and had so much amazing fresh seafood every day, it was unbelievable how easy it was to forage for food up there.  The clams and mussels really have a lot of dirt in them, so what we did is place them in a bucket of ocean water and changed the water throughout the day.   There are also some that are edible but only if cooked well; they'll poison you if you eat them raw and in large quantities (ie. morels, blewits).  There's even one (Coprinus comatus, the shaggy mane) that is totally edible and harmless, but if you drink alcohol the day of or next few days, or had alcohol in your system from a few days before, you can get really sick.  The compound, coprinin, mixes with ethanol and produces a compound toxic to humans.  Needless to say, I've never tried the shaggy mane (never know when a homie will show up with a brown bag and want to get their drank on) even though it fruits like crazy here all the time.  
 

*Coprinus comatus* actually doesn't cause any negative interactions with alcohol.  It just happens to be slightly similar in appearance to another inky cap type (that is NOT shaggy) which does cause the negative interaction with alcohol.  I think the myth that this alcohol avoidance rule applies to *Coprinus comatus* may have been borne out of an abundance of caution, but realistically if you can't tell the difference between the one with a shaggy exterior and the one without you prooooooobably shouldn't be eating wild mushrooms.  lol.
