The original site where S. leucophylla hurricane creek white used to exist in the wild was plowed and turned into a pine tree plantation.  While I never saw the original site before it was destroyed, I had heard it was a huge field filled with plants!  The original site had many normal S. leucophyllas, but a few plants displayed the blinding white traps that we are fortunate enough to have preserved in cultivation prior to the site being destroyed.  
  
A lot of people probably are wondering, what does this site look like today, and is there anything left?  Surprisingly, there is still a tiny little patch remaining to this day, but it is slowly being overgrown by the surrounding shrubs.  Are there any super-white plants left?  Sort of, but nothing like what we have in cultivation.  
  
2 years ago when we first spotted this relic patch, there was a little opening in the  shrubs (which were much shorter at the time) and you could walk in there and see quite a few plants.  This little clearing had a decent amount of sunlight.  As of 2013 (2 years later), that open patch is now filled with thick shrubs, and there's no way you can even attempt to walk in there!   All of the plants that were once receiving decent light are now etiolated and shaded.  Many only produced phyllodia.    
  
Will this patch survive in the long run?  IF a fire comes through, or someone clears up the shrubs consistently, this relic site can potentially last a long time.  However, the landowners clearly aren't interested in preserving these plants, and as is, the site will likely persist for a few more years at best.   
  
 In the case of the S. rubra wherryi Chatom giant site, in 2004 (?), the population was in the same situation: they were heavily shaded by thick shrubs and struggling to survive.  As of 2013, we didn't find a single Sarracenia in the original Chatom giant site.  There were also gigantic S. psittacinas  that used to grow there..these were also all gone.   While I remain optimistic that the little hurricane creek patch  may persist for a few more years, I remain skeptical that it will last through time.

`post #23` · `id=39548` · `2019-01-15 20:53:08 UTC`

Things aren't looking very good at the relic hurricane creek white site but to be fair, things in the wild in general didn't look good overall.  I think Sarracenias in the area  had a rough year, but history has shown that poor grow years don't have a significant long term effect on the populations as a whole.     
  
When I last visited this site (2018), my first thought upon arrival and inspecting the plants was that it probably wasn't worth the drive. I did see a few seedlings, which was a good sign, but the L. catesbaeis and the S. rubra wherryis that were pretty obvious a few years back couldn't be located.  The brush has grown very thick and the plants are getting more shaded than I can remember.  There is open space for new plants to colonize, so if somehow those shaded plants can set seed, this site may persist for a bit longer.    
  
Here's one of the more open spots of the site:  

![](../../images/2b/2bba73a9b92a7fd9551e7764f6cb2580ab72212775ea4b5a5fa8d768eb1d55e0.jpg)

  
  
  
Plants look horrible, but that's not what worries me.  The shading is the main threat.  Overall, there's still a ton of plants at this site, but most of them are growing underneath thick brush.  This spot pictured below is relatively open:  

![](../../images/1f/1f0151a1d9276175496831ce83247f334e4ada5c2ec34970d595ec31a97b058a.jpg)

  
  
What I mean by shaded is the thick brush in the background.  If you look carefully, there are many plants growing underneath it!    The majority of the plants are being choked out.  On the other hand, right in front of this thick brush, the shrubs and grass are consistently "bushhogged" every year, so this is why I think the site still has a chance in the long run.   As long as some seeds sprinkle out into the open areas, the plants will survive.  Also, if the maintenance crew decides one year to clear up the thick brush, that'll be the ultimate revival of the site:  

![](../../images/9e/9ea466dc4fff10f7693e780021e797b61347d2232aea10828d8437eb6629e13e.jpg)

  
  
Trap that probably won't fully color up becuase it's in the thick brush:  

![](../../images/43/43ffc4c2bec375545d2d3668e77a81ab65dfd5258bd3436dacc29e8df506b063.jpg)

  
  
  

![](../../images/cb/cb4e687ffa2d9528f0adcdae7b15476a316ef10213cffd20bae0c4c02ec62b53.jpg)

  
  
Aside from the habitat shots, There wasn't much worth photographing this time around:  

![](../../images/67/6703fbea647d4db2f9f84b9ec603e2b58d85c75cb62583b68b04201dcc116c9d.jpg)

  
  
You can judge the health of a population by its production of youth and fecundity.  Seeing this seedling brings hope:  

![](../../images/a7/a785aee8d8452ec62d71e56e648fec7d4883a4d26bc80464eb5ac6d5f4da8280.jpg)

  
  
Overall,  it looks worse, but it doesn't look like S. leucophylla will disappear completely in the long run.  I think the genetics will get even more bottlenecked over time as the decades old plants eventually die from being shaded out.  I wouldn't be surprised if rubra wherryi and L. catesbaei go extinct at this site.
