for the most part, if you look at a S. flava flower, "they all look the same" overall: they have large, yellow "dangling" flowers that smell either like sweet lemons or cat pee, depending on your olfactory sensors and environmental conditions.    
  
However, after looking at so many different flava populations in cultivation, it seemed like it would be interesting to compare flower color and structure from various S. flava populations.  The most obvious differences are in color, which we surmise is from recent or distant hybridization with other species.  Other differences, such as stem height, how long the bloom lasts (partially environmental), date of flower opening (highly environmental), and overall flower shape may not be as obvious.    
  
A lot of what we can learn about these flowers is by comparing plants growing under similar environmental conditions.  However, we also have to be very careful about drawing conclusions, as a single pot or tray can have various microclimates.  
  
Here's a thread dedicated to showcasing the diversity of flowers in Sarracenia flava!  For the sake of keeping things as detailed as possible, location data (or the best data available) will be included for every photo.  Feel free to add pics to this post!

Ah, a great question regarding the origin of red pigments in flavas-this discussion will extend to red pigments in the body of the plants as well.   I'll preface this discussion by saying that while evolution can't be proven, all of the scientific evidence and observations point towards it being true.  
  
Similarly, we can't prove exactly how these red pigments came about in flavas, but the overwhelming evidence supports hybridization as being the fuel behind it.  The answer to this question is more like a book,  I don't know if I can accurately leave out details to give an overly simplified answer, but here goes nothing:  
  
1) the probability of a red pigment mutation occurring by itself (ie. asexual, which then translates to a sexual mutation in the next generation) is much lower compared to the probability of hybridization in Sarracenia.  I do not discount mutation as being a possible factor in red pigment production in S. flava; in fact, mutation via hybridization could very well be the driving force in color forms in Sarracenia (ie. red leucophyllas).  
2)While hybridization does seem to be the main driving force, it's still possible that some red flava populations evolved from point mutations (ie. asexual) mutations.  I believe there might be multiple different pathways to red pigments in flavas, but again, hybrization seems to be the main driving force.   
3)All of the red flava populations, at least in the panhandle, are within close enough geographical distance to other species for hybridization to occur.  In fact, I haven't ever seen a red flava population where hybridization hasn't occurred (again, it doesn't mean there isn't a population of red flavas out there that don't have signs of hybrization, but for sure, that would be an extremely rare occurrence).  
4)I believe there might be different hybridization "pathways" to red pigment production.  In other words, it may not just be one species hybridizing with S. flava to create these red forms.  This topic is a thread on it's own, so I'll leave it at that.  
5)In a single population of atropurpureas from Okaloosa Co, FL, I'm now highly suspect that S. rosea is involved in the formation of this color form.  Here's the reason why:  
  
5a)S. rosea Liberty Co, Fl to the left(pink flower), S. flava var. atropurpurea Okaloosa Co, FL to the right.  Notice the strong semblance in shape of the petals between the two.  In fact, the atropurpurea flower shape is more typical of rosea than my sample rosea LOL:  

![](../../images/3f/3fbf70f68a5879fd4015d77c12c6d002af4d7139582164d71a857c3e9502700e.jpg)

  
  
  
5b)Hybrids between rosea and flava are incredibly variable.  The flowers sometimes have blended colors between the two parents.  Here's S. naczii Okaloosa Co, FL:  

![](../../images/7e/7e8081cb07199bc8bdf2119eec975d246e0df5d1902922666cf1a0075b7d5c16.jpg)

  
  
5c)Here's a S. naczii from Liberty Co, FL:  

![](../../images/b1/b1ea296f355451afde24e2cd12a53e8c52b30cfa8963e9bdd0e735b12a7489e9.jpg)

  
  
5d)The further you get away from the original primary hybrid, it's not inconceivable that after some generations of back crossing or even selective inbreeding (due to timing of flowers opening), the red pigments in the flower can get "watered down."  In fact, with the right pressure, it seems understandable that the red pigments in the flower could virtually disappear for the most part.  Here's a S. flava var. atropurpurea Okaloosa Co, FL, notice the red blush is very faint:  

![](../../images/bf/bfa5f7afb8420ec90c3fd5941df978f09c350a7ffef9538c1d3efd70d9d521ee.jpg)

  
  
5e) Here's S. naczii 'red throat' Okaloosa Co, FL.  Interestingly enough, this is a solid green bodied plant, but take a look at the slight blush of red in the flower:  

![](../../images/95/95bf512558997a4f85ba21825d1fbb00118c12e22e2bd44eb7d579a2ea951507.jpg)

  
  
  
5f) S. naczii from Liberty Co, FL.  This just shows how diverse (and confusing!) the flower colors can be, but notice how the color and shape of petals in this example seems to resemble a blend of both parents.  In this case, this clone is a reddish bodied individual with nice veins in the lid:  

![](../../images/be/be75365c6029c30404c932cf6fc0f6238f0aee131bb18769fae86a0714bfb591.jpg)

  
  
6) Here's S. flava 'bronze' which is pretty clearly a hybrid.  Check out the flower on this beauty:  

![](../../images/50/50293bc1f65927828800598cd6b455aad3c6cf6d54af18ac00c4eb2a439a6bd4.jpg)

  
  

![](../../images/2f/2f4e32debc1eef5e8abdef3e6b637ea0306532c20d8d2fe2e789a2eaa196d6ad.jpg)

  
  

![](../../images/04/04d296608c09ce8abee07d00b0c5276fb5dd0bf616eb883db975ed438b384c55.jpg)

  
  
7)When you self the following atropurpureas (Waccamaw, all of the okaloosa Co, FL atropurpurea populations, and all the Santa Rosa Co, FL populations), none of them breed true, as the offspring are still segregating.    This suggests that hybridization is a somewhat recent event with these plants, perhaps only 4-5 generations ago if I had to guess, if that many.   Segregation in the offspring points towards a hybrid origin.    Same observation can be said about S. flava var. rubricorpora Liberty Co, FL, although I believe this population has inbred for a long enough time that within 2 to 3 generations of selective breeding, you can get "pure breeding" rubricorporas from them.  With selective breeding, I believe you can stabilize the red color trait within a few generations with any atropurpurea.  
  
8) S. flava var. rubricorpora Bay Co, FL is either older than the other red flava populations, or went through generations of inbreeding quicker than the other populations.  There seems to be a higher frequency of plants from this population that breed true and produce mostly red offspring.  You do see S. flava var. rugelii and some "less red" individuals show up in the offspring, as well as extreme red throats coming from solid red flavas from this population.    I suspect extreme red throats, which are found amongst these Bay Co rubricorporas, may have come from one or a few sexual mutations that are relatively very new to the world (maybe only a few hundred years old if I had to guess).  Instead of the mutation being in the production of red pigments, it may be that the mutation resulted in the absence of red pigments in the interior of the trap except on the "extreme red throat".  Either that, or combining the flava var. rugelii genes to the rubricorpora genes in the "perfect combination" of parent plant(s) and generation(s) led to this unique combination.  
  
  I've tried producing seeds from crossing many different extreme red throat clones with each other, and there is consistently a low seed set.  This suggests they may have arisen from a single line.  As an aside, the way around this "self incompatibility" issue is to breed an extreme red throat that came from a rubricorpora.     
  
 I think this over-simplified explanation strongly suggests hybridization as being the driving force for the formation of red pigments in S. flava.  In fact, I believe that many Sarracenia populations that appear to be "pure" species have in fact historically hybridized with other species in previous generations.  We haven't even talked about the patterns observed in S. alata, S. leucophylla, and even minor giant color forms, let alone the nominal color forms.    When you get even nerdier and start digging into other plant species that show parallel results, it further strengthens the hybrid theory.

Evolution is seldom linear, though the phenotypes we see often suggest this. By evolution, we're examining the change in allele frequency, in a** population, over time**. This flux was/is happening all over the ranges with differing results. A sport or mutation can die out completely, and it may pass on useful genes before disappearing itself; the pathway to a phenotype is thus improbable to reconstruct. We can only follow the clues we can find, an incomplete set of data.  
  
Given the original, vast populations of Sarracenia, along with time the unlikely becomes a potential. Sadly, we are today looking at fragments. Many of the clues have been buried under asphalt.
