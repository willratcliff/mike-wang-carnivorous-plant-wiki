Here are some seedlings from S. purpurea ssp. venosa that is supposedly from their southern most coastal plain range in Georgia.  What's peculiar about these seedlings is their very close resemblance to S. montana-notice how the hood is recurved in some of the individuals.  Could this be an intermediate between venosa and var. montana?  
   
  
Quite frankly, who knows, but once they get bigger, we'll probably be able to get a better guess at what's going on.   Unfortunately, these plants aren't very fast growing...in fact, they're painfully slow to grow, but I still have very high hopes for them!

Hey Phil,  
  
You are absolutely right to indicate that the two subspecies are not supported by field research/morphological data.   
Here is an article that analyzes the situation:  
[www.uvm.edu/~ngotelli/manuscriptpdfs/ellisonetal2004ajb.pdf](http://www.uvm.edu/~ngotelli/manuscriptpdfs/ellisonetal2004ajb.pdf)  
And again, you are correct to state that the division of S rosea from purpurea is supported.  
  
With that in mind, Mike's purpureas from various localities is a collection. I have 2 quarters from 1962 (silver), one has a D mint and the other is a P mint. both are worth the same, and are from the same year, but just having one doesn't complete the collection.  
  
Whether the populations are genetically different or not doesn't matter much in this situation. Mikes location data, in my mind, is just placed on the label to show where the plants came from.   
  
While the subspecies for purpurea aren't supported by much data, I use the designation to know if this is a northern purpurea or one from a southern state. It might not make a difference genetically, but if an actual county or state can't be given, it is just cool to know where the plant came from, in general.   
  
If anyone has not read Gotelli's article on morphological variation of S. purpurea, I highly recommend it. He is one of the best ecological researchers studying purpurea in the U.S. currently. He also figured out how nitrogen deposition can lead to purpurea producing more phylodes, and possible extinction:  
[www.uvm.edu/~ngotelli/manuscriptpdfs/Ecology83p2758.pdf](http://www.uvm.edu/~ngotelli/manuscriptpdfs/Ecology83p2758.pdf)

HI Sunbelle-our night temps. here are in the mid 50's.  It seems these purps. tend to color up for me during the fall, especially after the pitchers have aged a bit.  They're starting to show some interesting veination right now.    
  
This plant photographed below was given to my good friend, Rob Co, last winter.  He grew it in his greenhouse-same latitude, same night temperatures, but his grow season is about 2 weeks ahead of mine.  Notice his plant is also bigger than mine.  In a greenhouse, when the weather outside is cold, it's warm in there, whereas outdoors, the plants stay cold.  This delays a lot of growth ourdoors, and delays the "aging" of the traps. It seems when you have warm days every day in a greenhouse, you'll get faster growth and quicker maturity compared to outdoor growing.
