Technically speaking, all leucophyllas have white petioles, but I guess this one was named white petiole because the white extends way far down the trap, and is very bright white.  About 2 years ago this plant produced the most amazing traps and of course Mike Wang didn't get a pic.  Last autumn I was waiting patiently to get some nice monster sized traps only to find phyllodia after phyllodia...NOTHING!  This year, it sorta produced some traps, but they're not as white and jaw dropping as I was expecting.   Perhaps they're good enough to show for the time being, maybe one of you out there will get this thing to perform better.  Hopefully next year there will be something more impressive to display.  
  
Breeding-wise, this is one clone out of hundreds that I'm focused on to produce more white pigments that stretch farther down the petiole.  It seems now that we finally have the brightest of bright white  leucophyllas in cultivation, the next step is to "paint" the trap with more white.  Tons of plants have white pigments, but very few have the white stretch down the trap more than an inch or so.    
  
This clone doesn't seem to like my conditions that much, so it seems logical to breed it with a more vigorous plant that's more cultivation friendly.  Current pictures don't do this clone justice, the white pigments a few years ago stretched much farther down the trap.  Long story short, here's some photos, taken 9/21/16:

`post #5` · `id=29566` · `2016-09-26 17:53:05 UTC`

It wouldn't surprise me if the lid (and possibly other structures) on Sarracenia is a modified leaf blade and tube is a rolled up petiole...this would be consistent with Sarracenia's relatives, where the nectar spoon on Heliamphora would be the modified leaf blade and the pitcher tube is a rolled up petiole....and then you see Darligntonia:the tongue could be a modified leaf blade whereas the pitcher tube is a rolled up petiole.  
  
If you take a simpler example, like a piece of celery, the entire portion of the main stalk that you eat is considered the petiole, at the end of the petiole are leaflets that are attached to a petiolule (secondary branch of the main petiole).  The Sarracenia "pitcher tube" is very similar to the petiole of a piece of celery.  It wouldn't make sense to call only the end portion of the celery stalk that connects to the stem the petiole, so this is my reasoning for calling the entire pitcher tube minus the lid the petiole.
