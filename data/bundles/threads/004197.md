As many of you know, S. flava var. rubricorpora from Bay Co, FL tend to color up under optimal conditions as the traps age: this even happens in nature!  The traps start off greenish with a bit of red mixed in!    What's not well known is that there are a few clones that seem to color up right after the traps open, which leads us to this particular clone in this thread.    
  
For the most part, this ERT recessive rubricorpora is pretty darn red right when the traps open and it gets much more red as the traps age.  Interesting?  Well, there's more to the story: in case you were wondering what the heck does ERT recessive mean, it has great significance to this particular individual.  Why?    
  
Out of a batch of S. flava ('extreme red throat x rubricorpora), both parents from Bay Co, FL, a lot of the offspring turned out to be..hmmmm, how do I put this...crappy!  To be fair, they had nice shape, but there was neither the extreme red throat nor did the traps color up even after they aged a bit!  They could probably color up, but imagine a rubricorpora that's extremely difficult to coax out the red body-that's what these were, and they made up the majority of the offspring.  However, I did get one jaw dropping extreme red throat with a nice red body, remember S. flava var. rubricorpora 'super extreme red throat?  Well, in case you forgot or never saw it, here's a picture of super extreme red throat:

Is the body darker and does it color up BECAUSE of the suppression of the extreme red throat?  Not sure. Happy to see you using my photo [meizzwang](/user/11) - what a treat that this forum has people scoping these plants multiple times in a season to get the real deal low-down 411 on how they behave!  
   
Having visited this population twice, once in early May when the above pic was taken, and again in late June of the same season, and having seen your photojournal of your plants, and having grown divisions from your stock for four seasons, and having grown my own material from seed, I feel these plants fit best as var. ornata rather than var. rubricorpora as that variety is commonly understood. Speaking more broadly, I don't think that any Sarracenia that requires 4-6 months to transform into a red taxon should be considered as such. I have seen fields of S. alata whose early season traps turn bright red by September in the baking hot savannas of south Mississippi while fall traps remain lightly veined green only. It's a bit of a funny argument for me to make in any case since I am not the hugest fan of the infraspecific framework laid out by Don Schnell, may he rest in peace.   
  
There are individuals from this flava population that do redtube-up fully in a short time, but probably 39 out of 40 do not. My sense is that these plants are related to the Liberty Co red tubes, but have diverged somewhat due to the river drainage in between them. There have been countless intervening sites lost, so perhaps the intergradation was smoother in the past. A rose by any other name...what we call these plants doesn't detract from their awesomeness, and these genetics are some of my favorite.

I'm not even sure the timing of how long it takes to color up is relevant, given that a single clone can take a varying amount of time under different conditions to color up.  The Bay Co plants are very environmentally sensitive and color up inconsistently from one environment to the next: they're undoubtedly harder to color up than the Liberty Co, FL plants in general.       To make things even more complicated, a single clone can produce the yellow top, red body phenotype one season, while the same exact clone in another season, in the same exact environment can have a solid red body from head to toe!  
  
   
  
Taking the complexity of color morphs a step further, even if a single clone of rubricorpora Bay Co, FL doesn't look like a rubricorpora under one environment doesn't mean it can't express that phenotype in another!        
  
  
Sarracenia taxonomy is definitely messy and not crystal clear. Perspectives can also be skewed based on the genotypes and sample sizes observed.  For example, I've grown out a batch of several hundred  rubricorporas from Liberty Co, FL where the majority of individuals aren't red bodied, yellow topped but rather ornata-ish and watered down rubricorpora looking.    
I've also grown out batches where the majority of the individuals have been rubricorpora dominant.  I've had the exact same experience with the Bay Co, FL population: one batch had mostly "non-rubricorpora" looking indivduals, whereas the other batch had mostly rubricorpora looking individuals.           
  
Nevertheless, it seems the frequency of yellow topped, red bodies individuals in both the Bay and Liberty Co, FL  populations are high enough that for the sake of practicality (and for horticultural purposes), I'm comfortable with the rubricorpora designation for the time being. I completely agree with [awkwardsilence](/user/1248)     . If we are going to assign a variety to a relatively homogeneous population (more of if we should in fact do this below), then we should look at the population as a whole to judge which name best fits.   
  
[meizzwang](/user/11)     :  
I maintain that rubricorpora is not the best name for a population that is at minimum 95% plants that are green with heavy veining and minimal to partial infusion. I was shocked by how few "classic" (aka unambiguous) rubricorpora plants there were there in late June, after around 2 months pitcher curing time. I would generously estimate there were perhaps 10 to 15 in the entire site. Just because you visited in fall and found all red plants does not mean that they can reasonably be called that. To be frank, I am surprised at your reluctance to reassess your position.   
  
Your counterexamples do more to confuse the issue than illuminate it. To wit: Of course it's possible to create redder plants from this site by selectively crossing more rubricorpora-esque clones. I have done it. But those plants are horticultural artifacts divorced from the genetic dynamics in the natural population. Of course one can speculate that the plants could be different colors in different years, and invoke all kinds of potential environmental factors without evidence. And of course one can always appeal to the apparently unknowable complexity of Sarracenia genetics and trot out the exceptional 1 in 1000 clone that in fact turns solid red.  
  
Perhaps if we pray to the Sarracenia gods (Bog Jesus???) in just the right fashion, these plants will miraculously be turned red.  
  
Enough. None of that changes the photographically supported fact that 95% of this population best fits var. ornata. Rather than blanket-designating a whole population, rare individuals that fit the description of var. rubricorpora can be called that, and the others can be ornata or ERT, or var. gluteus maximus, or whatever arbitrary category we want to create.   
  
After all, plants from other sites are handled this way. I have not seen anyone insist that all Okaloosa Co. flavas displaying more than a throat blotch are var. atropurpurea simply because they grow mixed in with a few var. atropurpurea. It would be absurd to say that such a population, ornatas and all, IS ATROPURPUREA and then deploy all kinds of hair-splitting to justify that position.  
  
Lastly, a big fat caveat on this entire argument is that it is a horticultural one. It is my opinion that Schnell's varietal designations have no botanical or biological validity. There is no biological "there" there, and this is a point on which you and I agree Mike. When present, all the varieties interbreed freely and tend to create a smooth phenotypic spectrum between themselves. Just as a red tulip would not merit taxonomic distinction from a white tulip from the same seed pod, Schnell's infraspecific varieties carry no weight. To extend the example, it would be highly intellectually problematic to single out the red tulip and the white tulip for designation, while ignoring the 20 other siblings in shades of pink. Pink is the reality on the ground with Sarracenia.   
  
Conservation-wise, the important genetic differences are those that occur between populations of S. flava, not any color forms that may occur within these populations. That is, the Sandy Creek flavas that we are currently discussing are distinct from the Liberty Co. flavas to the east and from the Outlaw plants to the west. Conserving these strains on a population level is what matters, not focusing on Schnell's varieties.  
  
These are terms of horticultural convenience only, and the sole reason I even engage on this subject is that I am a horticulaturalist and this is a horticultural forum. For us, these terms are extremely useful and if we are going to use them they should be applied in a way that minimizes the misunderstanding Schnell's overly simplistic system creates.
