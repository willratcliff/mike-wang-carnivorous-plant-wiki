As all hardcore Sarr nerdz on this forum know, S. flava var. rubricorpora from Liberty Co, FL doesn't breed true, and even when you cross two different rubricorporas from this population, you can get ornatas and even rugelii's!  In one such event, I got a several rugelii's, but this one individual seemed a bit more yellow than all the others.  I compared it to other rugelii's from various populations and it indeed was "yellower" if that's even a word.  
  
Will this characteristic be consistent year after year or as it reaches vegetative maturity?  No idea, so far it's pretty darn yellow, but can I say it looks as yellow as it was last year?  Kinda, but not as much as last year.  we'll see if this holds true, it may also give us clues as to how we can breed for deeper yellow plants.   Photos taken 4/18/17:

Long time ago back when I was in middle school, I had this carnivorous plant book and there were pictures of  the most yellow looking flavas I've ever seen!  He had several pots of this amazingly bright yellow flava, which makes me think it might have been goldie before that plant was named.  I think the collection was in Le France.    
  
Goldie is likely a Carolina flava var. flava plant, you can tell because of the veins in the throat help define the variant.  However, it's origins may be similar: perhaps it's a recessive gene from a pigmented plant.  Another interesting fact is when you breed AF plants with regular, non-mutant plants, the pigments in the traps tend to be darker.  It makes me wonder if the same concept can be applied to recessive genes found in red plants breeding with each other.
