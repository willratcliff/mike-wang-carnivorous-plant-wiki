About 20 years ago, getting access to multiple clones of S. leucophylla from various locations (in the US) was incredibly difficult: they were very hard to come by, and chances are, if you did get lucky enough to find one, it probably came from the late Bob Hanrahan, who asexually propagated many plants from his bog and distributed them wholesale.  The only other commercially available S.leucophylla was Sarracenia Titan, created by Bogman (Botanique) and widely distributed via tissue culture.  Long story short, we have very limited clones to experiment with in cultivation. (edit: we also had S. leucophylla tarnok from tissue culture. This was in the late 90's or early 2000's, can't remember when it started going into circulation).  
  
Fast forward the clock 20 years later, there's HUNDREDS of different clones of S. leucophylla, many of which are still just starting to get into circulation.  Things changed drastically once we started getting hurricane creek white into circulation, that is probably the most popular cultivar.  With this relatively new influx of genetics comes challenges: how do you keep S. leucophylla alive in the long run?  
  
When I first started growing S. leucophylla hurricane creek white in 2003 or so, the plants would grow strong during the grow season and then produce amazing fall traps!  Then, the plants would CRASH big time: the main rhizomes would rot and I'd have to surgically remove the good parts and throw away the rest.  The good parts were planted into fresh medium and the rot stopped.  While this helped divide the plants, it was frustrating because some of those clumps would rot out before I had a chance to divide them.  
  
The following grow season, the salvaged rhizomes grew beautifully again all grow season long and then produced amazing fall traps AGAIN..... but then they would all ROT **AGAIN**.....GAHHHHHH!!!  Repeat this for a good 3 years and this is what I went through when I first started growing S. leucophylla Hurricane creek white.  Why were these plants rotting on me so often?!  
  
Enough was enough, started experimenting with adding more perlite to the medium-that sorta worked, but I still got rot.  I tried spraying fungicide.  That didn't do anything, the rot just kept on going.  I tried jumping over a fire 3 times and making sure I didn't walk under a ladder or cross a black cat.  that didn't work.  What was going on?!  
  
My plants always sat in about 2-3" of water, and when you smelled the bottom of the pot, it reeked of sulfur.  The roots at the bottom of the pot were always brown or black.  What if I didn't sit them in water, would this fix the problem?  
  
I cut a hole near the bottom of the kiddy pool so that only 1/4" of water could accumulate, so these plants never sat in more than 1/4" of water at a time.  I watered the plants every day because by the end of the day, that reservoir was completely dry.  Results?  The rot disappeared completely!  BINGO!    
  
How do you know if the plant is sitting in too much water?  Smell the bottom of the pot:if it smells earthy, you're all good, if it smells sulfury, you'll want to lower the water level and let the reservoir dry out completely before watering again.  However, don't ever let the soil in the pot completely dry out, it should always be kept moist all year round.  
  
Turns out, this "discovery" isn't unique to cultivation, it's also a common theme in the wild.  If you look at some fields of S. leucophylla in the wild, the surface looks almost dry, but maybe 2' (give or take) below the soil surface is permanent water.  In fact, What looks like a dry field actually has permanent water underneath: cray fish will dig mounds and come out at night!  Occasionally, during the rainy season, you'll find S. leucophylla plants in flooded areas, but those floods are either temporary or are highly oxygenated.  I have also seen S. leucophylla growing aquatically in the middle of a pond, but that pond was more like a slow moving river with highly oxygenated, moving water.  
  
In habitats where S. leucophylla and S. flava grow together, you'll almost never see S. leucophylla growing in the wettest, muckiest places unless that water is moving or slowly percolating.  S. flava is less picky and can grow in more stagnant, boggy conditions.  
  
There are exceptions to the rule: during the fall pitcher growth, the plants are using up incredible amounts of water, so I raise the reservoir level and allow the plants to sit in water for 1-2 days.  anytime you have very fast, active growth, the plants seem to be able to tolerate a little more water than normal.   
  
With my collection, I literally water every day.  The S. leucophylla beds get filled with water in the morning, and about an hour later, the reservoir is completely drained. The pots get fully hydrated, yet never sit in water for long periods of time.   
  
This concept of keeping the soil moist at all times but not water logged can be applied to S. rubra, S. alata, some S. minors, S. psittacina (although some grow these waterlogged without issues), and some purpureas.  
  
Others will challenge the claim of growing S. leucophylla on the drier side and say they've grown S. leucophylla for years without rot problems.    I'd say if that's true, it's certainly an exception to the rule, and I have no idea how that works.  Maybe they are using taller pots, or a medium that breaths very well even when submerged in water.  Maybe they have semi-sterile conditions and water.  For the rest of us, if you grow S. leucophylla, the take home message is to keep that soil moist, but don't let it sit in water for more than 2-3 days in a row.  If you do decide to sit it in water permanently, make sure the water level is very low, maybe 1/4" or lower...that might allow sufficient oxygen to enter the soil.  But be forewarned, this method may still technically lead to rhizome rot.     There are many other environmental factors that can lead to rhizome rot (which requires an equally lengthy post to describe), but lowering the amount of water the plants sit in will greatly reduce the probability of losing your plant.   Let that reservoir dry out between waterings and be sure to show off your beautiful plants here on the Sarracenia Forum!

horizon46: Top versus bottom water doesn't seem to matter, so long as you either do one or the other.  If you switch from top to bottom watering all of the sudden, if you have salts in the water or building up in the soil, it can move the salts into the root zone, which can burn roots.

I know that in low humidity areas, pots may dry out if not kept in a tray or reservoir, but we never use water stored under flats, pots, etc. An alternative is a larger pot, hence greater water holding capacity. We water every day, when required. It's vital that pots go from saturated to moist, not quite dry. This process pulls air into the media, giving the roots much needed oxygen. S. minor, rubra ssp., jonesii/S. jonesii seem to require this fluctuation more than most.  
  
Your findings are consistent with ours, Mike. The other rot inducer is decomposed, slimy peat, which can produce the classic, anaerobic muck. As you say, the black root tips is a sign something in culture isn't right.  
  
Don't want to be slave to the hose? Got a large collection? Setup a battery operated timer(s) to run soaker hoses, Dramm Dribble Tubes, Jain/Chapin tubes, etc. With about 80,000 plants here, getting irrigation setup is always a priority, once freezes have past!  
  
The only downside to not having trays of water: ants can set up home base through the drain holes in pots/flats… jerks!

Great thread! I have been blessed with relatively little rot both here in OR and back in Northern California, so I’m not up on the super-ninja chemical warfare techniques exactly. What I have noticed is that almost  every time I have found rot, there was a Cultivation Fundamental that was seriously lacking (the other times have involved the aftermath extreme weather events during dormancy). If you are having rot problems, the first thing to do after you put out the fire that’s right in front of you is to step back and assess how well you are providing the basics to the plants: full sun (including not being shaded by surrounding Sarracenias), very clean water, low mineral acidic substrate, ample food supply, adequate dormancy, at least somewhat aerated media (a lot about that already here), and freedom from undue physical stress (i.e. you divide way too often). Missing fundamentals weaken the plants, and weak plants rot. Simple as that. Of course shit happens and no collection will be entirely rot-free, and some environments are inherently more challenging than others, but chemicals etc. are no bueno and could possibly be causing one to miss an opportunity to be better at listening to the plants and giving them what they need.

At my old job where we grew Sarracenia commercially (yes, I once grew these plants on a very large scale), we trialed around 24 different soil and soiless media for potted Sarracenia plants in a 1/4 acre greenhouse (~20,000 plants total).  Everything was grown in the same greenhouse (same environmental conditions), but watering was calibrated based on the type of media used.  Results were measured in leaf mass produced within a 6 month period.      the types of soil and soil mixes included almost everything you can imagine, as we ordered everything that was available or seemed reasonable to grow carnivorous plants in.   
  
 In terms of plant performance, nothing beat plain old 100% peat moss.  I remember hearing parts of Europe banned the use of peat, which is probably why Mike King was forced to find an alternative or stop growing.  Peat consumption is certainly problematic long term as it's not a sustainable medium, and the biggest threat to peat bogs is for the production of energy, followed by horticultural use.    
  
In my opinion, The environmental cost of consuming a relatively small amount of peat to grow Sarracenia is exponentially outweighed by benefit of saving genotypes from long term extinction.    Our consumption of peat is extremely efficient: I have community tubs of Sarracenia that haven't been transplanted in 9 years!  Needless to say, many of us are getting as much "mileage" out of this precious, unrenewable resource as possible.    
  
The trouble is, peat is being used to grow almost everything, which isn't good because many other crops can be grown in a less expensive, equally well performing soil mix.  Fortunately, it seems Peat isn't being widely used as fuel here in the US, but if commercial growers continue to use peat over a renewable compost,   it's only a matter of time when peat will be banned here in the US.

#### **Mike Wang** (`@meizzwang`)
`post #28` · `id=37876` · `2018-07-05 20:15:13 UTC`

I think the challenge with pure sand or perlite+peat tea isn't maintaining the proper moisture content, but rather the lack of buffering capacity of the medium.  pure peat is pretty darn resilient to a change in pH over time, but fine sand/perlite have a very poor buffering capacity (ie. ability to maintain a low pH level in this case).  The tea alone will have a weak buffering capacity over time because there's relatively less organic material in it to buffer the pH.  At first, You'll have a lot of free floating hydronium ions in peat tea, but once they're neutralized, there's nothing to "replace" them and keep the pH low...unless you're constantly renewing the peat tea.  Root growth/water uptake from the plant can change the pH of a soiless medium.     Nevertheless, it still seems worth testing, as theoretical results don't always equate to actual results!  
  
The wild isn't as linear and crystal clear as it is in cultivation: it's likely in constant flux, so it's hard to say that what appears to be pure sand doesn't have other factors affecting it (ie. frequent rain, organic soil beneath or nearby, or a humic acid rich "tea" leaching into it slowly or frequently, micro-fauna activity, etc).

I certainly have felt your pain basedrifter, sounds like you're re-living the exact story outlined on the first page of this thread minus the mealy bugs!  I guess you got it a little bit worse.    
  
To get rid of mealy bugs, you can either use orthene (but beware, that's some nasty stuff!) or if you want to take an organic approach, you can soak the rhizomes in hot water during the winter dormancy period.  First, peel off all dead and brown material, especially the stuff "hugging" the rhizome.  power wash the rhizome.  
  
Once you have  semi-clean rhizome, you can dunk it in a hot water bath.    The temperature should be just hot enough that your hands can stand it when you dunk them into the bowl of water, but when you move your hands in the water, it's almost unbearable.  No idea what temp that is, I just get hot water directly from the sink.    But beware, you can cook the rhizomes and roots if you leave them in the hot water too long.  too short of a time and you won't kill the mealies.  My recommendation is to experiment with a less desirable plant first, and once you get the hang of it, then you can "clean" up your more prized plants.   
  
Keep in mind, healthy, thick rhizomes can withstand longer hot water dunks than weakened, struggling plants.  Oh, and fragile clones like anthocyanin free plants really hate the hot water dunk, so be careful.

I'm starting to hear a lot of reports of growers having trouble with their S. leucophyllas/leucophylla hybrids rotting.    If you grow S. leucophylla or even some mooreis, I highly recommend reading the first page of this thread if you haven't already: [sarracenia.proboards.com/thread/4178/prevent-rot-sarracenia-leucophylla](https://sarracenia.proboards.com/thread/4178/prevent-rot-sarracenia-leucophylla)   
  
Even if you know how to prevent rot, it still happens to EVERYONE(eventually), so don't feel bad about it or go APE S*** like I did LOL   Most important thing is to figure out what went wrong and with diseases, think about days, weeks, or even months BEFORE you see symptoms.  Most of the times, infections start a few days or weeks prior to symptoms showing.      
  
   Last fall, I lost my mother plant of S. leucophylla 'old pink' Baldwin Co, AL, maybe that will make you feel better cuz look at this thing, and it would have produced really big fall traps this year:[https://postimg.cc/75mwNyfL](https://postimg.cc/75mwNyfL)  
  
[https://postimg.cc/2qymcXZ7](https://postimg.cc/2qymcXZ7)   
  
Won't go into the details of my exact  reaction because it's too embarrassing (some of you have heard the story), but good news is a small side shoot survived!  Why did it rot?  In September of last year, we had a heat wave that lasted quite some time (over 100F ever day for a few days).  If the pot didn't sit in water, it would be bone dry by the end of the day, so I sat it in about 1" of water....for 4 days!  2 weeks later, the fall pitchers suddenly wilted and by the time I realized there was rot, it was too late to save anything except a small side shoot.    
  
Good solution to this challenge, if you ever have it, is to slightly over-pot your plant or grow them in a bog (large volume of soil), that way you don't have to sit it in water during those heat waves.

[meizzwang](/user/11) have you considered frequent division as a contributor to rot in your plants? Do you experience high rates of rot in all your leucophyllas, or disproportionately in the select clones that you whip hard for propagation? Of course, it always seems like it's the nicest clones that rot, but you know what I mean.   
  
My experience with leucos, and rot in general, is far different from yours Mike and I'm trying to zero in on why. I on average lose about 1 plant per year to rot (in the last 5 years I have lost 2 flavas, 1 leucophylla, 1 minor, and 1 rubra). Of hundreds of divisions I took this spring, I have lost one to rot so far. I think that the climate here in OR is a good one for the plants, but it's not radically different from northern California. We both have lots of sun, good water, and good genetics in what we are growing.   
  
For the other folks reading this: I leave my plants undivided for as long as I can, space allowing. Minimum 3 years between chops, but often longer and slow growing clones might go 5-6 years between splittings. I always leave a big rump plant - if it's a 20 GP plant I'll keep half, 10 GPs, intact as my mother plant. If it's not ready to divide, it doesn't get chopped up for someone. It's common sense that frequently breaking up / chopping bits off of plants would weaken them, but I find most growers don't appreciate just how detrimental it is. Put another way, many growers don't appreciate the huge benefits of leaving plants undisturbed, and leave a lot of potential unrealized while increasing their problems with pests and disease. They simply figure that significant rot rates are "normal."  
  
Now, some climates have a lot of rot. Fact. I would just hate to have people resorting to all kinds of delicate strategies like keeping the water no more the .349862" deep at all times and worrying about the pH of perlite, while losing sight of the big picture. If you are a grower who constantly divides his or her plants for sales or to trade for that next sexy clone, then you are going to have more rot and you may have to resort to said delicate strategies. If you are a new grower and all of your plants were acquired in the last year or two as fresh divisions, you should watch out too. But if you just wanna grow big nice Sarracenia, put them in wet but aerated, acidic, low-nutrient media, in full sun, with bugs to eat, let them go dormant in winter, and DON'T FRICKIN' DIVIDE THEM ALL THE TIME. You'll be very happy with how it turns out.
