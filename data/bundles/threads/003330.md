I would not say at fault, but definitely a mistake. The proof is in the pudding as they say...when the seedlings all look different from the supposed parents, it's quite obvious.  
  
  

  
  
With polyhybrid crosses, the seedlings sometimes do look way different, or don't even resemble the parents, but the point you make is crystal clear:  a hurricane creek white packet, for example,  should have hurricane creek white looking offspring.  When they're all red, there's something off.  Some crosses have have  predictable outcomes.    
  
However, it's not always easy to trace back where the error occurred: the grower could have mixed up their own seed batch and not properly kept track of which seeds came from their own stash versus seeds they bought. They could have purchased from multiple different vendors and lost track as to which vendor sold them a batch that was incorrectly labeled.  Some vendors may have unknowingly had their flowers pollinated by insects, which screwed up their cross.  
  
I guess my point is, a good way to choose who to buy your plant material from  is via reputation:  a vendor who goofs up here and there is human.  On the other hand, a vendor who has multiple complaints about not selling as advertised and hasn't taken measures to correct the issue is a different story.

Am I missing something? Why was this expected?  

This clone opens with a brilliant pink/red flush in the veins and down the body as exhibited in most of the photos in the thread. After several weeks, the color fades to the typical green coloration seen on the usual HCW. You can see this in my latest photo where the pitcher in the foreground is green, and the newest pitcher in the background is red!   

  

The pink/red coloration seems to be inherited from the clone F parent as that plant is known for blushing pink the same way in certain environments (typically cooler regions).

  

We had our fingers crossed that eventually one division will experience an asexual mutation that causes it to retain the red color throughout the season. Imagine a brilliantly white alba that's always bright red to boot!
