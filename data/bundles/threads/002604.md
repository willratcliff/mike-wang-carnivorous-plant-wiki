If you recall from a previous thread, it was mentioned that Bud Wilkerson's bog has some incredible genetics, and this clone helps strengthen that fact.  All of the leucophyllas from this bog have a certain "look" to them (maybe it's the shape?), but the various color patterns set one clone from another.  Perhaps this plant is a cousin or long lost relative of the famous Wilkerson's red rocket, who knows.  
  
What we do know is that this red lipped clone produces nice spring/early summer traps (which aren't nearly as colorful), and then goes almost completely dormant all summer long, only producing phyllodia.  During this stage, much like S. oreophila, it's important to keep the soil as moist as possible but not overly water-logged to make sure they don't rot.  In the wild, S. leucophylla tends to colonize either substrates that are on the drier side, or substrates that have constantly moving water.  S. flava on the other hand, will hang out in the more stagnant, boggy areas.  
  
Anyways, during August, S. leucophylla 'red lips' looked like a dense bunch of phyllodia that was so thick, you couldn't see the rhizome. Some of the leaves were thinned out so the rhizome was exposed to direct sunlight.  It always seems to take forever to get fall pitchers on S. leucophylla and some years we don't get many, but this year is looking very promising overall. Finally, after several years of babying this thing into a strong, healthy plant, it produced its first showy pitchers.
