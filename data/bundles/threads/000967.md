I wish we visited the south earlier in the year.  In June, everything, including the flavas, have traps in their prime, and it's not 102 F with close to 100% humidity out there!  If you've ever tried to stay outside under such conditions for more than 2 hours, you will know what we experienced...it's savage!  
  
Fortunately, some of the S. flava var. rugelii traps were in okay condition...enough to get a glimpse of what was going on out there in the wild.  In other years, the traps by now would have been completely brown by September, and at other sites, they were.    
  
The okaloosa County rugeliis were very interesting, and Damon Collingsworth mentioned these plants were much like the newly registered S. flava 'Bob Hanrahan'-scroll down the page a bit: [www.carnivorousplants.org/cpn/Species/v42n3p107_109.html](http://www.carnivorousplants.org/cpn/Species/v42n3p107_109.html)   Traps on these plants were beyond belief gigantic, and the lid on some of these were unreal!  It's always tough to capture the actual size of these beast with photos, and I don't think I did a good job this time-you'll just have to take my word for it

KE-you're very welcome!  I still think Sarracenias could have colonized the "drier" areas had there not been years of drought.  The surrounding drier parts outside of these seeps supported droseras, lilium catesbaei and pinguiculas.  
  
Think of these seeps as creeks with maybe 3-4" of water (7-9cm) on the top.  Underneath, there's a meter or more of sludgy peat, and there's no knowing when you're stepping through if it's solid, or if it's soft.  If it's soft, you can get stuck, and you have to be very careful as it's tough to judge where these deep peat holes are.  I don't think we walked through any of the deep holes that could suck you in alive, but I suspect they are there.  At one spot, I sunk in past my knees, but eventually got out...it was pretty frightening.  You can yell off the top of your lungs and nobody can hear you, even if they are relatively close by.  The hills absorb a lot of the sounds here.  I remember yelling as loud as I could for Damon and Axel when I got out of the seep, but they couldn't hear me. Even if you don't drown in these bogs, if you can't get out, you can easily get heat exhaustion and dehydrated.  This isn't the kind of water you can drink!  
  
 These are no different than many of the peat bogs all throughout the world, where people have mistakenly stepped in there, got stuck, and were sucked in completely.  There was a story about 2 guys hunting in Argentina, and one tried to save the other, who was breast-deep stuck in a peat bog.  They both eventually drowned-this stuff is no joke!

About the strange 'dried up nectar" looking substance shown in pictures 4 and 5, I have the same "problem" in cultivation. every year, just starting in the mid of the summer, and becoming more and more present in autumn, I have this effect on my sarracenia pitcers. Not only flava, but also leucos and many hybrids. onlu purpureas and minors  don't produce it!  
I think it is a normal condition due to the fact that the place where I have my greenhouse is really humid and so my plants produce a big amount of nectar... then, when the climate become drier, generally from mid august to september, the nectar dry out  and rest on the pitchers. It is really unaesthetic. But I don't know how to avoid it...
