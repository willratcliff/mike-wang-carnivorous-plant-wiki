While S. rosea var. luteola and S. purpurea ssp. purpurea f. heterophylla are well known amongst collectors, S. purpurea ssp. venosa 'Antho-free' is almost unheard of.  Very few people in the world have this plant.  
  
    Interestingly enough, this is a man-made plant, and my understanding is there has never been an AF S. purpurea venosa discovered in the wild to this day.  Another peculiar aspect of these all green venosas is that they sometimes have folded hoods just like montanas.  Under optimal conditions, the plant turns bright yellow, and sometimes the traps get bleached out from the sun and have almost a slight white tinge to them.    There has been some speculation as to whether these are montanas, but I don't have enough info to support or deny such a hypothesis.  What I do know for sure is that these plants are down right amazing!!!

I don't know who in the US still has these original AF venosa clones, but admittedly, I lost them ALL over time!  So, a little background and recap on this strain: AF venosas are reportedly man made, but given that the seeds I acquired a very long time ago produced 100% AF plants, this suggests they were from a selfed mother plant that was the original AF  mutant.  Did someone really get lucky and get an AF plant from seed, or did they find a wild AF plant, dig it up and self it, but made up the story that the variant is man made?  I can tell you that I have sown 100's of 1000's of seeds in my life....never spontaneously landed an AF mutant.  In the wild, they're extremely rare, and if you find more than one AF individual at a site, chances are, they all originate from a single original mutant.  I know many of you fellow plant nerds with science backgrounds follow what I'm saying (so many details and explanations are omitted so this post doesn't get too cumbersome) but if you don't, it's all good, this stuff is complicated.   
  
  
Anyways, back to simple talk: The strain is just so dang rot prone!  IT's probably due to the fact that all plants originated from selfed seeds.  I do know the original clones are still going in Europe.  I had great success with them for several years but eventually every last one of them succumbed to rot.  Knowing that these plants are so dang hard to keep alive, more than a decade I decided to do a multi-generational breeding project with the hopes of creating a more rot resistant AF venosa by crossing the original AF plant with the most proven, rot resistant venosa that I have in the collection.  Several generations later, it appears there has been some success in recovering the AF venosa...finally!  Sure, I could have selfed the original plant and probably gotten 100% AF offspring, but that would perpetuate the poor horticultural experience that most face with this variant.  If the AF venosa plant has never rotted out on you and you're wondering what the fuss is all about, please message me so I can meet you in person and give you a pat on the back, you're special.   
  
It's hard to say if this experiment worked, as I don't have any clue about the genetics of disease resistance in Sarracenia, but the good news is if it didn't work, that's okay because I am still in the process of mixing in even more disease resistance into this line, so eventually we'll probably have a resistant strain even if this generation doesn't pan out.    
  
This breeding project isn't easy and takes commitment: in the photo below, notice how few all green plants there are, and of those, maybe 10 are anthocyanin free!  The other green ones have some red pigments, which are easier to see in person than in the photo.  Long story short, very few turned out AF, and of those individuals, will they be easier to keep alive long term?

They are mutants, it's a one-locus mutation different from the standard expression and isn't common (same-locus mutations can occur multiple times across populations if that locus happens to have a tendency toward being easily altered, but doesn't mean it's readily retained), and there is a detriment to the mutation in the wild that keeps them from becoming more frequent (anthocyanin is an antioxidant, it prevents damage from UV exposure and free radicals which harm DNA directly as well as can increase pathogen resistance, hence why it's so broadly produced in plants).
