Franklin County, FL is the eastern most location for S. leucophylla in the wild.  I believe they naturally occur here (can anyone confirm this?)  
  
Sadly, it seems like S. leucophylla from Franklin County, FL is doing better in cultivation than in the wild.  Granted, there are several populations we didn't see that are hopefully doing well, but of the ones we saw, they were in decline and had very few individuals.   There were no signs of seedlings.  The few individuals that we did see are in great health and had multiple growth points, but the low numbers of individuals compared to historic numbers indicates these sites are in danger.  
  
Much effort is being undertaken to restore some of these sites.   Even though they may be in bad shape today, there's great hope in the future that they will be back to where they should be.  Both habitats we saw were completely altered by humans, and one site was flooded earlier this year so the only plants that survived were the ones on the edge of the waterline or on islands that weren't fully submerged in water.  For those of you who have grown S. leucophylla, many of the genotypes out there aren't very forgiving when it comes to flooding the roots: they tend to rot very easily. Running water isn't a problem nor are large pools of water, but when you have a stagnant drainage ditch with low oxygen levels, that's when rot kicks in.

KE: Rob Co of the pitcher plant project showed me a huge S. leucophylla clone filled with impressive, large spring/summer pitchers.  He got the clone from me and it originated from Southern Baldwin Co, AL.  I've had this clone  for more than a decade and it NEVER produced large spring pitchers for me when grown outdoors.  Instead, it consistently produces dinky spring/summer pitchers and then outstanding, sometimes gigantic fall pitchers (of course, this happens especially after warm summer/fall temperatures).   
  
 The big difference between outdoor growing here in California and Rob's greenhouse  is that Rob's temperatures are around 10-20 degrees (F) higher than mine during the late winter/early spring, and all of his plants consistently start  about a month ahead of mine.  My cool outdoor temperatures early in the grow season might not cut it for great spring pitchers.  
  
On the other hand, I've grown some S. leucophylla plants from more northern locations, and these tend to produce beautiful spring pitchers despite our cool spring outdoor temperatures.  Maybe these plants are more cold tolerant?  
  
Sunbelle: glad you're enjoying the reports, and thanks for your insight!  Based on both your and Rob's results, I think it's safe to guess that the production of nice spring traps might be correlated with warmer day temperatures.  IF my leucos flower, they will sometimes not produce any spring pitchers whatsoever.
