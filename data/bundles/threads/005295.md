hcarlton is trying to orient your sense of direction. The layout in North Carolina is the opposite of that in California: ocean to the east, mountains to the west. You have to go a couple counties to southeast of Montgomery to find the flatter, sandy ground that is more typical venosa habitat (the pine savannas). The var. montana plants grow further west, in the foothills and low mountains of the Appalachians.  
  
A large portion of the Uwharrie Mountains is found in Montgomery County. It's a very old range in the middle of the state. The tallest peak is 1,188 feet / 362 meters, but most of them are just very large hills. I honestly didn't think that any carnivorous plants grew in that area.  
  
I recently bought a couple small divisions of Mike's Montgomery County plants. They have settled in nicely here in eastern North Carolina (close to the I-95 corridor). I'm curious to see how they develop. I currently have 8 Tattnall County plants, including a couple very nice seed-grown ones from Calen Hall. I also have one nice var. montana from Jeremiah Harris (keeping my fingers crossed that this one survives; my two previous montana's didn't enjoy the hot, muggy summers here). It wouldn't surprise me if the Montgomery County plants are an ancestral population of the montana's.

If this is the site I'm thinking of, it is protected as one of the few remaining Piedmont bog sites. It also contains *S. flava *and *S. x catesbaei*, so neat that you notice it in some traits of these plants! This preserve has some of the few remaining longleaf stands in the piedmont.

  

There are a few other inland bogs in the Carolinas. Many of these are closer to the sandhills (the hilly region between the low coastal plain and the more elevated piedmont). Some sites contain Atlantic whitecedar streams and look very similar to the Fall Line Sandhill *rubra* habitat in GA or the *gulfensis* habitat in FL. The inland Carolina bogs have *ssp. rubra*, but I think it was Schnell who noted some look similar to *jonesii*. These whitecedar stream bogs sometimes include *purpurea* and *flava. *

  

This hilly region is incredibly diverse, but unfortunately almost all destroyed. The *Sarracenia* we see today is just a limited sample of what used to grow in the region. The reason for the scarcity is due to the relatively rich soil of the piedmont while the fast flowing streams in the Fall Line Sandhills made water mills possible for an early industrializing South. Most of the large cities in the Southeast: Montgomery, Columbus, Macon, Columbia, and Raleigh straddle the fall line. GA has virtually no intact piedmont ecosystems.

  

It's great that at least couple bogs are preserved and some unique genetics are represented in cultivation.
