Several populations of "red flavas" exist in northwestern Florida: Liberty Co, Bay Co, Walton Co, Okaloosa Co, and Santa Rosa Co, FL (they probably are found in other counties as well).  Flavas with red bodies and green lids are considered S. flava var. rubricorpora, whereas flavas with solid red bodies are considered S. flava var. atropurpurea.     
  
  
In Liberty Co and Bay Co, there are large populations of S. flava var. rubricorpora, but arguably, some consider individuals in these populations S. flava var. atropurpurea because at the time they were seen, the plant was solid red.  Trouble is, in cultivation, amongst many different growers, it has been shown that many plants labeled "atropurpurea" start off with a green lid, and as the pitcher ages, the entire trap turns solid red.  Are these rubricorporas, or are these atropurpureas?  
  
Interestingly enough, in Okaloosa Co, FL and Santa Rosa Co, FL, there are both plants that resemble S. flava var. rubricorpora and S. flava var. atropurpurea.  The S. flava var. atropurpureas from these sites start off solid red.  However, plants from these sites strongly resemble the rare S. flava var. atropurpurea found in the Carolinas.  Do these "atropurpureas" from Okaloosa Co and Santa Rosa Co, FL occur naturally, or did someone plant them at these sites?  
  
In Santa Rosa Co, FL, Damon, Axel and I visited 2 different sites that were roughly 20 miles away from each other (ie.not within a reasonable distance to hybridize with each other) and we found red plants at both of these sites!

Amazing place Mike. Must've been brilliant to visit and thanks for the posting the terrific photos and a matter worthy of debate, as you say!  
  
As for the question of "atropurpurea" in these infamous sites and perhaps lone individuals within var. *rubricorpora* stands, I still don't buy it. My views have been well laid out but put basically I don't accept the variety name based on the following while fully recognising you have visited the plants in person:  
  
1) *S.flava* var. *atropurpurea* is a name assigned to a distinct population of a red form of plants in the Carolinas they being isolated from the ones in your photos both genetically and geographically.  
  
2) Those Carolina var. *atropurpurea* share an intimate relationship with the region's *S.flava* var. *flava* as evidenced in this and other photos of wild plants (photo Don Schnell, CPN Volume 27, Number 4, December 1998, pages 116-120 )  
  
[http://s128.photobucket.com/user/JayBeNineteen/media/v27n4p119f1_zps559fcba2.jpg.html](http://s128.photobucket.com/user/JayBeNineteen/media/v27n4p119f1_zps559fcba2.jpg.html)  
**A.** var *maxima*, **B.** var *atropurpurea*, **C.** var *flava*, **D.** var *ornata*, **E.** var *cuprea*  
  
3) The "atropurpurea" in your and other's photos are seen in an intimate relationship with *S.flava* var. *rugelii*. Clearly, there exists a foundational relationship between the red plants and var *rugelii*.  
  
4) This relationship between red *S.flava* and var. *rugelii* occurs in a number of locations in the Florida Panhandle as you note. In the locations you highlight, the red plants are intermingled with or alongside var. *rugelii*. Indeed, looking at your photos I believe the hood form of the "atropurpurea" is distinctly Florida Panhandle - particularly representative of the var. *rubricorpora* broad, floppy morphology. By that proposition they are quite distinct from *S.flava* hood form in plants native to the Atlantic coastal plain.   
  
5) The genetics in these Panhandle *S*.*flava* populations allow for the expression of the cut throat var. *rugelii*; the yellow lid red tube var. *rubricorpora*; and the all red plants wherever they are found. My position is that all three of these are related and their expression is a broad one. The expression of var.*rugelii* in these locations is, in fact, no less notable than that of var. *rubricorpora* or the all red plants other than being deemed the so by human beings.  
  
6) *S.flava* var. *rubricorpora* has, since Schnell's 1998 paper naming the variety and beyond, been acknowledged as being highly variable with a wide spectrum. In his book, *Sarraceniaceae of North America*, McPherson notes this as "The overall colouration of *Sarracenia flava* var. *rubricorpora* is extremely variable" (p 249). In his earlier volume, *Pitcher Plants of the Americas*, McPherson highlights this theretofore accepted variability in the following photo line up of var. *rubricorpora* specimens. Great point KE, and thanks!  I think we can all agree that in light of such controversies, it's important to maintain county and state location data to distinguish one red population from another.  
  
I think you're spot on!  To reinforce some of what you're saying, here's a post on creating atropurpureas from rubricorporas: [sarracenia.proboards.com/thread/273/flava-var-atropurpurea](http://sarracenia.proboards.com/thread/273/flava-var-atropurpurea)   
  
from my understanding, Here's the difference between the populations in Florida/the carolinas:  
1) In the Liberty Co and Bay Co populations, the majority of the "red" plants have green tops, red bodies.  However, many individuals turned solid red after the trap aged.  Some have solid red bodies, even on newly opened pitchers.  From horticultural observations,  two Liberty Co, FL "rubricorporas"  crossed with each other can result in solid red offspring.  
  
  
2) In the Okaloosa Co and Santa Rosa Co, FL populations, the majority of the "red" plants have solid red bodies, even on newly opened pitchers.  
3) Carolina atropurpureas have completely different genetics from the red florida populations, but these start with solid red traps, even on newly developed pitchers.  
  
  
  
It's my belief that most, if not all of these flava variants are of hybrid origin, and we are witnessing them either early on (ie. you only see a red plant here and there) or later on (after that red plant has been inbred for many generations and is starting to stabilize).

A plant at any location, or in cultivation for that matter (a real can of worms wriggling in the mud), *can* fit a variety name, as you say. It must be remembered, however, that these variety names are nominated as such and are discounted or accepted as time moves ahead. Indeed, other persons will promote their proposal that a new variety exists by their reckoning. McPherson's recent Sarraceniaceae work is an example.  
  
The real issue, explicitly in the matter of "all red" *S. flava*, is what names are proposed. Whilst the name var. *atropurpurea* exists, rather than the overly simplistic, "Oh, look, a red plant: that's a var. *atropurpurea*, done deal", my assertion is that, to avoid inappropriate designation, those NW Florida all red "atropurpurea" have a variety name assigned them in recognition of all factors that differentiate them from Carolina var. *atropurpurea*. Such recognition would include the obvious that they, in Florida, represent an expression of red in the *S. flava* genotype that is *concurrent* with that expression in the Carolinas but not the same given the relationship with other *S. flava* varieties around them respectively.   
  
I am loathe to use this crude example, but human skin colouration within what is a single species, is a reasonable example with degrees of very similar melanin expression (or apparent lack of it) found in otherwise diverse populations geographically. It is incorrect to lump all such superficially similar groups under one name based solely on colour. And, in fact, despite the ease with which the person on the street will do so, academically it doesn't happen for good reasons.  
  
In the case of *S. flava*, whilst some are content to do just that, lumping all 'all red' plants into var. *atropurpurea* without being cognisant of the differences beyond colour at a stage within seasonal development alone is flawed in my view. Surely the *Sarracenia* world is more interesting than that? In fact, agree with them or not, it *is* as evidenced by McPherson's submission of a wide ledger of variety names in *S. alata*.  
  
I submit it is more than valid to recognise geographic regionalism and  distinctive growth patterns as they present themselves consistently (such as the case with var. rubricorpora) when assigning variety names in Sarracenia.  
  
The other issue is that of hybrid origins of *S. flava* varieties. Obviously, as the plants present today the historical sequence of genetic influence in any species has a broad foundation. The only way to know for certain is through the challenging task of establishing a DNA based phylogeny. We stand in a snapshot of time and the eccentricities of speciation are occurring all around us. I realise it is hard to not be impassionate when viewing red *S. flava*. But in the field they stand right along side other varieties that are just as notable academically as they are. The plants in Mike's terrific photos simply are the expression of *S. flava* in the locations. It is truly a chicken/egg situation as to which is the 'standard' *S. flava* genetically versus which human beings may emotionally decide is the base type.   
  
In NW Florida var. *rugelii* is commonly referred to as the baseline and var. *rubricorpora* (and others) as the extension of that. This is based on what is the observed degree of occurrence of the two. In actuality, the intimacy of the occurrence as an overall population of *both* underscores that they are derived from a genetic base, rather than they simply growing together. The same intimacy applies to those *S. flava* varieties in in the Atlantic coastal plain: vars. *flava*, *maxima*, *atropurpurea*, *ornata* and *cuprea*. Perhaps a little more commonly found, var. *flava* (though as pointed out the distinction between var. *flava* and var. *ornata* can be a grey area and literally in the eye of the beholder) is colloquially referred to as the type specimen essentially. In reality it is one expression of the *S. flava* genotype in the region. No one variety or individual is a "failed" anything. They simply are what they are.

The discussion in this thread on S. flava ornata highlights two problems with reading written descriptions, focus and interpretation. By focusing on Schnell's comment regarding throat color, the historic feature of this taxon ie. the venation throughout the tube and lid is ignored or relegated to secondary importance. Don was not describing a new variety but recognizing the validity of prior publications of the taxon, " three are matched with previous formal descriptions and varietal names". He was not writing a description. The name and descriptions of the variety date from 1881. In their over 100 year history they are consistent in recognizing the venation as the distinguishing characteristic. As with atropurpurea, I believe the taxon was published concerning the plants in the Carolinas. These plants rarely have extensive coloration in the throat and when they do it is as heavy vertical venation rather than horizontal suffusions or bands and halos of color like the gulf coast veined plants. As with atropurpurea Schnell notes "It is frequent in the Atlantic North Carolina and South Carolina coastal plain, far less easily found in northwestern Florida". To suggest the taxon does not apply to the plants it was originally designed for is quite a reach. In fact, I suggest that it is the plant in the second picture that best fits the historic notion of the taxon and Dr. Schnell’s comment. Here is the issue of interpretation, as I see no OBLITERATION of throat color but very prominent and enhanced color in the first plant photo, not reduced at all by the veins that overlay it. Indeed it is the throat patch intensity that nearly obscures the venation. Conversely in the second plant's photo the color is concentrated in the prominent veins and nowhere else in the throat. Finally, when we examine the photo Don chose to illustrate the taxon we do not see a plant with the intense suffusion of color proposed in the post as a defining trait. While the underside of the lid is hidden, the throat is still visible and is distinctly veined, with the central vein most prominent, less than the type flava to the left and about the same as the cuprea to the right and with none of the suffusions of color present in the proposed "proper" ornata in the first photo .  The type of plant in the first photo is popularly referred to as super-ornata to distinguish its atypically abundant venation and color suffusions. That would mean that the second photo illustrates a more typical version.  
  
Sarracenia flava ornata is a plant with prominent vertically striped veins on the exterior of the tube and on the lid. The presence and amount of lateral secondary venation is variable as is any other suffusions and patches of color. While those traits may be more desirable to many horticulturists they are not necessary to the identity of the variant.

  

Your last photo, Mike, has a single heavy vertical stripe of color in the center of the throat flanked by veins. I submit that it is indeed the remnant pigmentation and has been nearly wiped-out.

boggrower asked “Or is the stabilzation the result of the atro genetics being swamped out by the rest of the genes in the population?  Does anyone know of any studies on this?”  
  
Not too long ago I came across the following doctoral dissertation. While it doesn't deal with atropurps or any other subspecies or varieties specifically it does look at the species and the role of hybridization among them generally. If you care to wade through it (lots of required technical information - cut to the results and discussions - see the contents - p.8 by the PDF page counter) there are three studies or chapters in the document  It seems to address some of the issues/questions/hypothesies posted by members in this thread. I found it fascinating and informative:  
  
The role of hybridization in generating species- and population-level variation in Sarracenia (Sarraceniaceae)  
Marvin Steven Furches  
[trace.tennessee.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=2675&context=utk_graddiss](http://trace.tennessee.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=2675&context=utk_graddiss)  
  
Some interesting statements/conclusions based on data obtained are:  
  
“Populations of different species at the same field site are more similar than populations of the same species at different field sites”  
  
“The entire genus can not be generalized as “freely hybridizing,” and allele-sharing among species can not be entirely explained by ancestral polymorphisms. This is not only an oversimplification, it is, as our data show, also incorrect. Interspecific gene flow in Sarracenia may be common between some species, rare or absent in others, and uneven or unidirectional in others.”  
  
“Our data show greater genetic differentiation at both microsatellite loci and non-coding chloroplast regions between S. jonesii and S. alabamensis than between S. alabamensis and the morphologically and ecologically distinct S. oreophila. Our study further supports the consideration of these taxa as separate species.”  
  
“The extent to which hybridization occurs across the range of these and other species in Sarracenia is not entirely known. However, our previous work in the genus (Furches and Small, in review) showed that most species of Sarracenia – particularly those whose center of diversity lay on the Atlantic coastal plain – formed relatively cohesive clusters when examined with six microsatellite loci.”   
  
“Sarracenia sites along the Gulf Coast tend to have multiple species – up to five in some sites in southern Alabama (personal observation; Schnell, 2002). While Atlantic Coast sites may also have at up four species in one location, most have fewer. There may also be additional factors that limit hybridization in this region.”  
  
He also documents that some species in some populations have significant admixtures of genes from other species while retaining most of their own species genetics and physical characteristics, thus documenting the significance of interspecific gene-flow in some populations.  
  
Also pertinent is:  The pollination biology of Sarracenia alata Wood (Sarraceniaceae) in Louisiana, Michael S. Bodri and Alana M. Gaspard in BARTONIA, journal of the Philadelphia Botanical Club  
[ia600700.us.archive.org/3/items/mobot31753003728992/mobot31753003728992.pdf](https://ia600700.us.archive.org/3/items/mobot31753003728992/mobot31753003728992.pdf)  
  
“The unique structure of the flower suggests adaptation for pollination when the bee exits the flower rather than enters, arguing for self-pollination rather than xenogamy.” and “Self-pollination could  
help explain the development and persistence of morphotypes ... even though Sheridan and Karowe (2000) showed, at least in S. flava, that the largest seed sets and the most viable seeds are obtained by cross-pollination.”   
  
Also this study notes the “ "Flower constancy" (Kearns and Thomson 2001) of Bombus tends to keep them in the vicinity of the ramets, as observed by us, so that if self-pollination does not occur, geitonogamy within the genet is the next most likely alternative. This could explain the occurrence of distinct populations identified in this species, for example, in bogs where plants exhibit areoles in the trap leaves, and could possibly have contributed to speciation within the genus.”  
  
Their observations for pollination of S. alata are consistent with observations of pollination by Bombus reported by Schnell (1978a,1983) studying S. flava in North Carolina, and Burr (1979) and Mandossian (1965), studying different northern populations of S. purpurea and may well apply across the genus.  
  
I think the results of these studies again support the importance of maintaining "location data" on plants and the necessity of preserving and maintaining plants from each site as representative of unique and valuable genetic groups. While the taxonomists may not be able to, willing to or are resistant to splitting them into official designations these groups are at least analogous to the races, nationalities, and ethnic subdivisions of the human species. Two leucophyllas may be less alike genetically than a Swede and a Philippino. The Sarracenia species of Florida and the species of the Carolinas are similarly so.   
Beyond the debate on atropurpureas, I believe evidence emerging from genetics studies and hybridizers efforts shows that any lopsidedness or omission of these various population-forms in the attempted conservation of species is a failure at preservation and in effect botanical genocide.  In the part of his study dealing with the diversity of the three endangered species of Sarracenia, Furches makes note of certain population locations of oreophila that are “unique and an important part of any conservation plan for this species.” The evidence suggests this is likely no less true for some populations of more common species.

Boggrower,       I was preparing a more thorough reply to your last post with some posts to the science section of the forum but it is taking some time to organize and document and I don't want too much time to pass without a reply.  
So here it is in short form without citations.  
  
The red pigmentation in flava atropurpurea (both gulf and east coast forms) has been published as coming from purpurea and rosea by Phil Sheridan of Meadowview. Therefor, not a mutation but an expression of introgression and gene-flow.  
  
Recent gene analyses for establishing phylogenetic relationships have strongly suggested alata and minor are sister species and put them into a clade together. That could explain the areoles. Perhaps a pre-divergence relict artifact. It might even explain the okefenokeensis populations.  
   
The alata pollination study makes the point that the preferred or most common pollination strategies are 1) self- pollination (selfing), 2)pollination within the same species or variant, 3) pollination with different species,  in that order. It puzzled me for some time why, in a genus that so easily hybridizes in cultivation, there were not more, obviously-hybrid plants in populations and how in these areas the entire field site did not blend into one big hybrid swarm but maintained separate species identities. Despite research data that shows outcrossing produces healthier more abundant seed and seedlings, and where there is oft repeated "common knowledge" that inbreeding(selfing) produces inferior, unfit plants, maintaining species identity by selfing may just be a more important advantage. Of course there may be other advantages to preferred selfing that we haven't figured out. The Ahh-Haa moment hasn't happened yet.   
Frankly, while writing this, I've just come up with an aha of my own. Maintaining variability(separateness) in the face of quick and easy homogeneity would be a tremendous advantage.  
  
As far as prey specificity, I wonder if it exists. Sarracenia seem to be opportunistic feeders, the kinds and numbers of captured prey varying with the prey populations themselves. They seem to attract all sorts of animals not just prey, from ants, flies, bugs, and wasps to spiders, moths, and hummingbirds. My personal experience adds blackbirds and our urban raccoons to the list with disasterous consequences (I now keep my plants entirely surrounded by netting).  
  
While pigment is a distinct trait (it doesn't encode for other traits) it absolutely acts in communion with other distinct traits like nectar production and composition, combination with other chemical compounds, and even odor. Besides the odor of rotting flesh that these plants can certainly manifest, there have been various other odors that have been reported which may play a role in attraction (or even possibly repulsion) of certain potential prey. Except for possible chromosomal linkage, these traits are largely independent of each other genetically. When do they each become critical for the plants' well being or survival (known as a limiting factor) has not been studied in any depth, to my knowledge.   
Pigmentation is often a protective mechanism, chlorophyll will burn out at high light levels or temperatures. Red pigment ebbs and flows in response to light levels that are not optimal but the chlorophyll is there, masked by the red, so photosynthesis is largely unaffected. Slow growth is a tendency in many clones/populations of Gulf coast flavas and doesn't seem to be related to degree of pigmentation. Large areas of unpigmented white however, definitely impact photosynthesis in a permanent way. They rarely become green once they have formed. There are other mechanisms the plants have that deal with low light.  
  
Enjoying the interesting and thought-provoking comments and ideas here. Hope more get started like it. Thanks to all.

MW, for me this reinforces my position in a major way: if there is to be any credibility to variety names being applied within Sarracenia species, then something *can't be two things at once*. This goes double for red flava specimens and big time for the intensely variable colour within "rubricorpora".  
  
While I'm aware others have a differing perspective, to my mind the elemental questions that arise and need addressing in respect of the variety names "atropurpurea' and "rubricorpora" beyond the simple installation of those names by their original proposer, and beyond simply saying they are all red or not, remain:  
  
  
1) Is there a significant and thereby isolating geographical demarcation between the red flava populations in NW Florida and the Carolina coastal plain?  
  
2) What is the nominal growth pattern of the distinct populations (clearly they are different i.e, the infamously slow division and low pitcher production of "rubricorpora") and do they set them apart (as readily recognised between concurrent "rubricorpora" and var. rugelii)?  
  
3) Is it fair to say that Liberty Co etc "rubricorpora" colouration can range markedly from yellow hoods with some red veining to hoods fully infused with red? Should that circumstance be recognised simply as the variability of the genetics that present as "rubricorpora" of NW Florida; a distinct reflection of those genetics?  
  
4) Is it the case that this colour range can be seen within stands of "rubricorpora" where those populations are clearly exist on the same genetic foundation?  
  
5) At what point of the pitcher development (pre-opening, just opened, two months down the track etc) do you describe the variety based solely on colour if that is felt necessary? To reiterate, can something be two varieties within the same season or is it in fact a presentation of the population genetics within which it exists?  
  
6) Is it realistic to describe one individual in the centre of otherwise related specimens within the population not so much as an expression of that population's colour range and therefore something notable therein but as something otherwise acknowledged to be native to a location 700 miles away?   
  
7) Is it fair to consider the question of why, within the otherwise accepted geographical stands of "rubricorpora" plants will be described as "atropurpurea" yet no plant in the eastern "atropurpurea" population range is described as "rubricorpora" and no such suggestion has ever existed?  
  
8) Referring to the question of *necessity* in 5), what lies behind that necessity? Is it the case that we seek to divide the visibly superficial differences to too large a degree without due consideration to other elements?  
  
  
My responses remain that the reliance on colour alone when it comes to the questions above is unrealistic in considering the distinction between the diverse populations in question and too simplistic in addressing those distinctions. I believe it is well demonstrated that some "rubricorpora" will become fully red having started the growth season as an obvious member of such a population on a number of grounds and, thereby, remain so.  
  
Mike, I firmly believe your Liberty Co. plant is representative of this (as are those I and others have) and goes no way to establishing a relationship between the genetics it derives from and those of "atropurpurea" as found on the Atlantic coast.

In cultivation and as a generalization, if you cut off all the dead winter traps from S. flava var. rubricorpora Liberty Co, FL (we won't talk about the mysterious bay co plants for now, LOL), the new traps tend to come out greenish (although some clones are so damn red that they'll still come up red).  Keep everything on from the previous year until you get new traps in the spring, and they'll start off as red as can be.

Since [meizzwang](/user/11) brought up the moorei question, let me drop some rank speculation! Based on what we saw, my guess would be that *originally* the red flavas begot the red mooreis, and not the other way around. Seemed like there generally had to be red flavas nearby to find red mooreis - bogs with leucos and green flavas had green mooreis. Just a hunch but I'd put money on the red color coming from rosea via x naczii, stabilizing in the flava population, and then being passed on to moorei clones. Of course, once formed the red mooreis could re-inject red genes into green flavas, possibly forming red flavas that do have a moorei as their most recent red ancestor. I suppose it's also possible that through some unknown mechanism a green flava crossing to a leuco could create red mooreis de-novo. For example the throat blotch on mooreis is often bigger than on the rugelii parent - the throat blotch gene was "amplified" somehow when the plants were crossed. And many of [philcula](/user/116)'s best red mooreis come from his (leuco Purple Lips x flava ornata) crosses, and neither of those plants is particularly "red." So truth be told there might be more than one path to a red flava, but if I had to theorize on what mostly/probably happens / the primary mechanism, rubles to ribeyes it was the rosea genes, not the leuco genes.
