Here's a population of S. oreophila from Cherokee Co, AL.  These have been stuck in the "seedling stage" for many years and didn't look all that interesting until now.  The pitchers this year probably tripled in size compared to last year, and surprisingly, these were one of the very first pitchers to open for the season.    
  
There's some variation in these plants, but for the most part, they all look similar.  What's noteworthy are the individuals that have veins and color this early on in the season-typically, they're the "dullest" looking in the spring, but can become quite colorful in the late summer to early fall.  Some of my purple throat clones have no color on them whatsoever right now, but they are tried and true.  
  
It'll be interesting to see if any of these color up later on in the year like the purple throated clones or the oreophila ornatas.  So far, there are a couple that have external veination, as well as veins in the throat, which gives them a good chance.  There's also one or two that have some slight bronzy color to the pitchers, but it's very slight.

`post #17` · `id=36615` · `2018-03-19 23:31:07 UTC`

gotsarrs: That's not the correct circumstance which led to the differences, but your reasoning is correct!  I had some tall minor giants in front of this tub, and it was shading the front section of the oreophilas, so the ones in the front stayed very cold and were practically shaded compared to the plants in the back.  I just trimmed those minor giants maybe 2 weeks ago, so now the whole tub gets full sun, but you can see the effects of late trimming.    
  
What do you win?  Aside from a high 5 and a generous pat on the back (electronically of course),  you just won an incredible prize that normally costs 29.99 plus tax, but I'm giving it to you for free: it's the gift of knowledge LOL  
  
    If you ever had multiple copies of a clone (ie. a tray of Judith Hindles, for example) and wondered why one plant is emerging from dormancy quicker than the other, it's typically due to a slight variance in environmental conditions.  Even if they are growing side by side, one pot may be experiencing slightly different conditions than another.  
  
In the oreophila example above, there's more than just shading that's causing the front plants to emerge slower than the back plants.   The plants in the back are also higher above the "water table" than the ones in the front (ie. plants in the back have deeper soil, so they're higher above the waterline when the tray gets flooded from rain).  This is because I had to lift up the tub a little bit to drain out the water after a storm, which shifted some of the soil towards the back.  Plants in the front have less deep soil as a result, so when it rains, the rhizomes are submerged under cold water whereas the ones in the back never get submerged in water.     The plants in the back heat up quicker on a sunny day because there's less water in their substrate, and of course, they get more sun than the ones in the front, which only get afternoon sun...  Anyways, Less water in the soil means the soil will heat up quicker.
