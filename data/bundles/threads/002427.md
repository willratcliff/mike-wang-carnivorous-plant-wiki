`post #7` · `id=19616` · `2015-07-10 16:26:14 UTC`

selfing can actually bring out certain characteristics in Sarracenia.  For example, the only reason we have a black veined flava in existence today is because a somewhat mediocre, mildly-veined ornata was selfed, and most of the offspring were heavily black veined.  But the downside?  Every last plant is extremely slow growing and difficult to keep alive in the long run (hence the improved black vein project).  How does selfing bring out even more veins and darker colors?  Maybe there's some better stuff hidden in the genes from the great-great grand parents of the black veined flava.  
  
As for S. leucophylla, can selfing a select hurricane creek white, for example, accentuate the white color in the offspring?  Maybe...On the other hand, I've seen hurricane creek white crossed with "regular" leucophylla and not a single plant turned out as a var. alba.  I've also crossed HCW clone F x leuco var. alba (MKL042) and got 100% var. albas, majority are vigorous and shapely-they're outstanding in general.  This result suggests both parents are pure lines (slightly inbred) and breed true.  Lastly, I've also crossed 2 var. alba clones and got 0% var. alba offspring, so the results all depend on the genetics of the parent plants.

#### **Mike Wang** (`@meizzwang`)
`post #9` · `id=19620` · `2015-07-10 18:07:12 UTC`

When it comes to probabilities, selfing gives less favorable results, and even if you end up with a selfed gem (ie. black veined flava), it'll likely take some continued breeding to get it grower friendly.  Selfing in the wild is more of a last resort to prevent extinction....outcrossing is favored and tends to increase long term survival in the long run.   
  
Of course, cultivation is a lot more friendly than the wild, and even weak clones will live if they look great (ie. Mike King's weak but amazingly white alba clone).  On the other hand, Sarracenias have a good reputation as being relatively easy probably because we select for vigor and ease of growth, and my aim is to get more of the general public involved as part of their assured survival for the future.

Also, a large percentage of flava outcrosses can tolerate very high salt concentrations.  Very fast growing flavas have a relatively high salt tolerance, but the same can't be said for very fast growing leucophyllas, which have finer, more sensitive roots.
