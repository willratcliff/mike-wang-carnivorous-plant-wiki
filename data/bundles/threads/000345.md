If you read the description of S. flava var. cuprea and you want to stick by the book, then you can say this Bill Hoyer clone isn't S. flava var. cuprea.    However, be careful with believing the current naming scheme, as it doesn't account for phenotypic variation in cultivation.   Growing this clone under various environmental conditions shows you can bring out phenotypes that are otherwise not noticed in both the wild and under normal growing conditions.   All of  the darkest, most prominent coppery topped "cupreas" I've ever grown at some point produced reddish bodies, and for the sake of simplicity, I consider them all to be genuine cupreas.     
  
Some claim that if the body of a S. flava does  color up, then it's an atropurpurea, even if it looks like a cuprea under most normal growing conditions.  After growing out many cuprea clones, I'm starting to find that very few cuprea clones DO NOT color up slightly on the body of the trap under certain environmental conditions.  
  
In the case of S. flava var. cuprea 'Bill Hoyer,' the reddish coloration might be due to excessive direct sunlight and very cold days/nights.  For the past several years, this clone has never produced a reddish body to the extent that it is now, but we haven't had cold spring weather like this in many years.  Under normal years, the reddish pigments do show up just a little bit on the body, perhaps not noticeably enough to get people's attention.  This year is different.    
  
  Just as an aside, Sarracenia flava var. atropurpurea 'Waccamaw' was selfed, and one of the offspring was a cuprea!  Anyhow, here's S. flava var. cuprea 'Bill Hoyer' Photos taken 4/2/18:
