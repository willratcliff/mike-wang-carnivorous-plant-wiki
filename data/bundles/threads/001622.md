It doesn't take a rocket scientist to know that one of the best S. flava var. rubricorpora populations in the world still exists today in Bay County, FL.  Apparently, it does take a genius to explain to the State of Florida that destroying this remaining habitat and not having regulations to protect the remaining sites is beyond ridiculous, seeing how they've already demolished literally everything they possibly could.    
  
Bay County encompasses 1,033 sq miles (2,675 km²), and out of all of that land, maybe 10-20 acres of ***pristine*** habitat is left.    Most of the remaining sites are relic patches of once vast fields of dreams.  One can only imagine how many endless acres of fields filled with red flavas once existed.  
  
Everything that is left in the wild in Bay County is surrounded by altered terrain or is altered terrain.  I visited a site in 2011 that was reported to have huge fields of Sarracenias and natural ponds containing giant floating S. psittacinas!  Can you guess what this site looks like today?  It looks like what 90% of Bay County looks like: endless rows of non-native pines grown for pulp production!  No ponds, no savannas, no rare native plants, no sunshine, just thick, dark forests of planted trees as far as the eye can see.  Unlike us CP enthusiasts, the majority of the general public sees nothing wrong with this: after all, they've "reforested America"!  
  
To add insult to injury, one of the last remaining sites is literally on it's last legs: the parcel right next to the red flava site, which is slightly uphill  and is the water source for the delicate savanna, was recently harvested and cleared but fortunately, much of the soil was  left undisturbed.   Will they come in with tractors and  till the soil to plant more trees?  Will they dig ditches and destroy the water table?  Keep in mind, many of these springs are extremely shallow.   Even without laying a finger on the savanna, this site can be destroyed by altering the neighboring land.   
  
Well, the rest of the story has yet to be written, but there is hope that fields like these will keep going past our time.  After all, this site still does exist as of 2014, and it's THRIVING!  I'm grateful we live in a time when there's still pitcher plants in the wild, and it's a pleasure to share this with you all.  
  
  
Try to imagine the most amazing, giant, diverse red tubed plants you've ever seen, and that's what's here, in relatively large numbers.  Also try to imagine 99F (37C) weather that felt like 110F (43C) and mosquitoes and other insects constantly biting you while you're drenched in sweat. Finally managing to get some posts together from this season's two awesome bog trips. These photos were taken May 10, 2015 on [DirtyDivisions](/user/168) and my "early season" trip. On this day we were very fortunate to visit one of the last remaining populations of S. flava in Bay Co - I think the number of viable populations as of 2015 doesn't take up all the fingers on one hand, sad to say. That said, this bog appears to be thriving. It's water supply is abundant (to say the least!), supporting a diverse population of flavas and some of the biggest S. psittacina EVAR, and making it virtually impossible to plant the spot with pine trees as the entire surrounding area already has been. Interestingly, the pine plantation on one side of the bog was harvested recently and some expressed concern that this disturbance might alter the hydrology of the bog for the worse. I am happy to report that this has not occurred and that the bog is as boot-swallowingly mucky as ever. In fact, clearing all those thirsty trees from part of the water recharge zone probably leaves more water for the bog!  
  
On to the plants. [meizzwang](/user/11) 's excellent posts on this site made us very excited to visit. His photos taken in the late season clearly show that by September or so most plants at this site have infused solid red and look like "atropurpureas." Since this also occurs at the famous Liberty Co. red tube site, Trey and I expected to find a population of plants looking broadly similar to the Liberty Co. flavas: red or mostly red bodies with green lids, and some ornata and "atropurpurea" outliers. Well folks we were in for a surprise! The plants at this site are mostly green in the early season! Here is an overview of a representative sample:

As an aside: before Mike coined the ERT name, these plants were casually called "ornata" - a good example of how "var. ornata" has become a catch-all category for anything odd that displays the inadequacy and incompleteness of the other existing varieties. There are many such plants in wild populations: just go to Okaloosa Co! I think it's funny that we are back to the pre-1998 situation of creating informal names to attempt to describe the diversity in wild S. flava. Don S. better catch up! Perhaps he is working on descriptions of several new varieties now...

Yet another interesting thing about this site and the ERT genetics is the conspicuous absence of var. rugelii. I think we spotted ONE small rugelii plant in the whole place and this was the only site we saw in the whole Panhandle where this color form was so rare. Most of the ornatas here lacked rugelii-style throat blotches as well, with the ERT form being the dominant manifestation of throat blotching. A Gulf Coast flava population with minimal rugelii influence?? These genetics are SO WEIRD.

I have no doubt that most of the veined plants will color quite strongly after several months of exposure, but we were pretty flabbergasted at the contrast with the Liberty Co. plants. The latter definitely darken considerably after opening, but the "typical" individual has a red tube before or at the time the traps open. The color behavior of the Bay Co. plants reminds me of S. alata in which even plants that age solid red open with lightly veined green tubes. Early season photos I have seen from the huge Stone Co. MS site famous for its red alatas contain not a single visible red plant! By mid-summer however the field has countless red plants. This behavior definitely throws a wrench in the color variety scheme as it is currently accepted. As others have asked, how can a plant be variety A in May, variety B in July, and then variety C in September? Do our concepts of color variation in S. flava match reality on the ground in wild populations? Does a sensible way to divide the color diversity in S. flava into separate categories even exist? And if it does, is it important to do so? Look for more fuel on this fire from what Trey and I saw in Okaloosa Co. this season...

You guys and gals in the US are so spoiled for incredible CP sites, thanks for sharing and documenting those scenes of dwindling uniqueness Mike!    Hope you can muster up some big time Gov't conservation interest before these remaining stands are gone.    The categorizing all the variants and at all the different stages of color development is anything but simple and there is no label you can attach to each plant for the progression of change.    Personally a seedling of a plant collected from this site, like any other in my collection, would read S. flava the location and as fitting a description of the clone as I could fit on a label.        Unfortunately as collectors, part of our plant selection process is to grab the most defined by color and sometimes by shape in a population as it is in our nature to do so.   Let's face it if you were offered a few plants, you would choose the reddest, the one with the most defined blotch and the one with the best example of venation, hence Don and the like put a name to these uniquely different clones.   These highly defined forms are supposed to be a typical cross section of the extreme variants within a population but as you can see from Mikes pic's the variation goes way beyond simple categories.    Unfortunately the variants tend to be considered unfairly, perhaps even only subconsciously, as "mongrels" and therefore less desirable.   
Look forward to more amazing wild photo's Mike and everyone!

This is too simplistic and, in fact, highlights how fragile the designation of variety status based on colour has been and continues to be. Mike and Calen's images of their respective field observations of the same population bear this out explicitly. You speak of the "final color" being the determining factor. That begs the question: just when is the final colour to be observed? If you are referring to the end of the pitcher season, colour can be striking as a result of the leaves progressing towards dessication as dormancy begins to set in. If field observations are undertaken at that time of the season they record a colour which, while compelling in the moment, is not necessarily representative of the, say, middle or any earlier part of the season. Indeed, a lot of visits to red tube sites for example are made to coincide with that period of compelling colour. And no foul there, why wouldn't people do that? Totally understandable, but not necessarily telling the full story.   
  
I have made the point before with respect to the debate over the designation of the variety *atropurpurea* being found within a population of plants otherwise and predominately designated var. *rubricorpora* (both variety name usages founded on previously published descriptions) based on individuals within that population eventually (the key word) becoming fully red. However, the plants in question had yellow lids like the genetic kin that surround them for a good period before becoming red, unlike the lids of var. *atropurpurea* over on the Atlantic coast which start out red at pitcher opening.   
  
To put it very, very simply, if the application of a variety designation is made based on observations made when the lids were yellow and the tubes were red the name var. *rubricorpora* (if one accepts that name as valid) would be applied. If observations hadn't been made at that stage but at a later date when the lids were red the application of var. *atropurpurea* is readily made - over enthusiastically with a motivation to see something noteworthy (as Mr Ed alludes to) would be my submission. My position before has been and remains, as Calen also asserts in his post above, if the colour based variety system is to be considered legitimate how can it be that a single plant or population of plants can exist as two varieties in one season? Or more accurately, be two varieties at all?   
  
Also, its worth repeating that the varieties we are referring to are not handed down by the Gods but those subjectively applied by individuals who, in all the best spirit, have considered the colour variability in a species and published conclusions that thinking has led them to in an attempt to create some form of order within the range of colour presentations. It is for the reader of their work to assess and accept or not those variety assertions. They are not inherently correct. Indeed, like anything they are open to question and there are many that do not accept them. Thing is though, books such as *Sarraceniaceae of North America* are very compelling and many simply accept the contents as "official", so to speak. It is my position that there remain flaws in the variety designations based on colour alone in that work. There is just too much fluidity in the expression of colour in an inherently widely variable base. You could, and my suggestion is that McPherson slips toward this in his work, create a variety list that went on forever dependent on erroneous stimulation.
