The Great Move: sounds like a title to a movie that's Rated R, doesn't it?  Well, with all the cursing, it's a good thing that this wasn't filmed, haha!    Anyhow, as many of you know, my entire collection had to be moved from the old site where it had been for more than a decade!  I've moved 7 times in my life, and this was the most painful one EVER!  Some of you are probably wondering why Mike Wang wasn't himself for the past few weeks....well, now you'll see why!    
  
I work 6.5 days of the week, have a new baby, and then had to deal with this!  Needless to say, I had to hire a crew to do most of this (and I'm thankful that we were able to save enough in advance for the move!), and it was a Nightmare on Elm Street (rated R for sure)!  There won't be any venting here, just imagine a very stressful situation, and then the movers trying to double the estimated price on you the very last minute without any warning or notice.  It was a great learning experience, that's for sure.

Thanks Rudeko!  
  
It has been cold/cloudy recently,  and even in full sun, it hasn't been  too hot these days here in Northern California, which has been a godsend for the new beds.  Every last one of these beds started off unlevel, so some plants are drowning in water, while others are so dry they're wilting.  To make things worse, it's heavily water intensive and requires irrigation  1-2 times per day, even during cold weather.  Light wind in the forecast today?  Yup, everyday!   That means spending 20-30 minutes every day picking up plants that fell over.  
  
Underneath the bed are soft woodchips.  All of the plants were falling over before because these were loosely laid out.  You have to literally compact them into the ground to help level and straighten the bed out.  A more compact bottom makes it less likely for the plants to fall over.  Organizing the plants and making these sections level by hand is incredibly labor intensive:it took me 2 days to complete only 3 beds!  It's also physically intensive because of all the heavy lifting (plants are placed in flats in order to move them from one bed to another).  
  
  
Thankfully, the nightmare is finally over!  Here's what I figured out: the beds are all at a slight angle downwards, so what I did was lined the middle of the bed with a drainage canal.  I then made several sections and leveled them by hand, so now each section holds about an inch of water.  If it rains, water from each section will overflow into the canal in the middle and drain out of the bed (the canal goes underneath the 2x4 board).  Before, there was no drainage!    

![](../../images/30/304a7d9f6b83a094190e5e84647e5b5a984749f7f313f4a4c3bc53261d1d2ada.jpg)

  
  
If you could only imagine, I went to several people for advice on how to fix this issue and one suggested installing a hydraulic device for drainage.  The people who installed the beds suggested that they build a pumping system with a float, and they'd engineer it to turn on automatically once water got to a certain level.  The best and less cumbersome solution is to put a little sweat into it and do it yourself!  For all you kids at home, the moral of the story is it pays to get a college education! What I figured out isn't rocket science, and it certainly doesn't involve bringing in engineers!  Okay, okay....no school needed to figure this out, but school certainly teaches you how to problem solve!  
  
  
  
I wasn't sure if this was going to work out at first, but I can't tell you how much of a relief it was to find out that it does!  In the same bed, you can customize each section so that one corner can hold only a little bit of water (ie. for rubra jonesii's) and another section can hold more water (ie. flavas).  None of the sections share the same water, so if there's a disease outbreak, it won't spread to every last plant.
