Normally, when it's freezing cold outside, I'm not as worried about pruning, pests, rot, and diseases.  However, with the warm temperatures and extreme drought we've been having here in California, we have new challenges that are othewise normally "dormant".  First off, there's potentially less time to prune and transplant because we are in danger of having the plants break out of dormancy earlier than normal.  Another challenge are pests, like thrips and mealy bugs.  With warmer temperatures comes bigger winter pest populations, and controlling them early in the season is crucial to prevent outbreaks and crop damage.  
  
  
  
Sanitation and prevention are the best ways to keep your plants healthy.  Clear up all the weeds in the surrounding area to prevent overwintering pests from having a "hide out" during the dormancy period.  Cut away all dead pitchers and get those rhizomes exposed to light-this will help ward off fungi and bacterial issues.

Hi Jeff,  
  
Not at all-never had any rot problems caused by dividing early in the dormancy period-in fact, it's been a good preventative measure here, as I'm able to cut out the old, dead material early on before it gets infected and eats up the healthy tissue.    
  
However, you bring up a good point: you and I have completely different winter temperatures.  Here, our night temperatures don't usually go below 25F, and are typically around 40-50F during the day, with bright sunshine.  Despite being cold, the cumulative exposure to full sun every day probably helps them callus a lot faster too, although light isn't needed for this.  There's also very good air circulation.    If you have extreme freezing temperatures, it'll theoretically take the rhizomes a lot longer to callus, which could account for lower resistance to mold.  You may also cover your plants with black plastic or remay during the extreme cold temperatures, which lowers air circulation and increases relative humidity.   On the other hand, freezing cold isn't good for fungal growth either.    
  
That is certainly a great experiment to run, but I'm guessing not every winter will be the same temperature-wise, so it's hard to prove one way or another. I bet a lot of it has to do with the local weather, the botrytis spore "bank" in the soil, and the native concentration of botrytis/pathogen spores in the air at the time of dividing.  If you take divisions when the spore population is very high and the temperatures are conducive to pathogens, I would expect a lot of rot issues.

I would tend to agree that the topical fungicides usually work fine for Sarracenia rhizomes.  We use sulfur fungicides such as those found in "Safer" brand products.  Sulfur has a residual effect, so it works well as a preventative.  I know it can cause problems in high temperatures, but like any of this stuff, you have to follow the label.  
  
I have used Tebuconazole products many times since it's found in Bayer disease control concentrate that you have to mix, and in their 3 in 1 pre-mixed combination product. It's been mostly in our greenhouse for powdery mildew on Cephalotus.  The concentrations in Bayer products are probably less than an agricultural product such as Merit.  A couple cautions, however.  The first is in using Ag chemicals unless you are really sure of your end product concentration.  We've had some mishaps in the nursery with that.  Also, you're dealing with a much more hazardous concentrate that most products made for home use.  The other caution is on systemic fungicides themselves.  We don't use in on Cephalotus anymore, and not because it harmed them, but it doesn't work anymore.  Fungi build resistance to systemics relatively quickly.  Topicals like Captan and Sulfur work more by making the environment inhospitable to the fungi.  
  
One product that may be of consideration for battling fungal problems is Trichoderma.  It's a predatory fungal spore product applied as a root drench.  Here's an example of the product:  [www.territorialseed.com/product/130](http://www.territorialseed.com/product/130)  Like anything it's probably best to try it on a few plants first to make sure it won't give any adverse results.  We haven't used in on Sarracenia simply because we haven't needed to.  I have used it on Cephalotus with good results.  
  
Jeff

`post #24` · `id=5994` · `2014-03-12 04:25:16 UTC`

joanne-totally missed your question, but fortunately, Calen dug up this thread 

![:)](//storage.proboards.com/forum/images/smiley/smiley.png) [not mirrored]

  The leaves furthest away from the dormant growth tip are completely taken off, even if they're green.  Typically, this is 3-4 leaves maximum-I snap them off like you would snap off a petiole of celery (although it's 10X harder to do with Sarracenia).  This exposes dormant "eyes" on the rhizome, and allows side shoots to form.    
  
The rest of the traps that are green are left on-these are typically traps that were formed in late summer/early fall, so they're a lot "fresher" and can last a while.  When you have 2-3 new pitchers in late spring/early summer, then those old leaves from the previous year can snapped off, although I leave them there throughout the season for the most part (too time consuming to go through the collection for a second trim).  However, if you want to grow Sarracenias for Jesus, it's best to do the second round of trimming.  So much easier to show with pictures-if you don't get it, let me know and I'll take some before and after pics to demonstrate.  
  
Calen: green phyllodia and  traps are equally valuable to the plant, even if there's new growth just starting.  In fact, if you have an old trap that's still green and has old insects in it from last year, leave those on until around May (for us in California).   
  
 How do you know when to chop them off completely?  When you have new pitchers catching a lot of insects, the plant no longer needs the old stuff. Keeping the old traps on is one of the "secrets" to producing gigantic traps on large rhizomes.     
  
I say good move on the purps.-they don't have any space to grow, so they benefit from "thinning" this time of year if they were very dense the previous year.    
  
Keep in mind these methods are over the top: you can technically trim everything off to the ground in december or during the dormancy period and still produce beautiful plants.

That's definitely a part of it, but I think there's more to it.  After much thought, I think it's also the environment (ie. high humidity, temperatures, etc).  S. flava var. rugelii clone A, for example, over the past decade gets giant for me outdoors here in California, but when I sent a piece over to California Carnivores during the winter dormancy, two months later, they had enormous spring traps in their greenhouse...bigger than I've ever seen for this clone!       
  
Christianjames: First, uproot the plant and clear away all the dead tissue from the rhizome.  Second, with a high pressure hose, wash all nooks and crannies thoroughly like you were trying to tissue culture this stuff!  If you miss a spot, your efforts will be wasted.  If you have a huge clump, divide it, and separate the rhizomes so they're not all tangled together and so that you can reach every little small hiding spot.  Be sure to wash the rhizome far away from your collection so the root mealy bugs don't splash right back onto your collection.  
  
NEVER recycle old soil.  If you mix old infected soil with new soil and plant your entire collection with it, BAM: you infected your entire collection!     
  
Lastly, keep ALL plants with suspected root mealy bug isolated from your other plants.  if you have "tubs" or trays that are separated, these mealy bugs won't spread, they'll only stay within the tub or tray.  These animals tend to stay put and not spread too fast, but if you help them, they'll spread everywhere!
