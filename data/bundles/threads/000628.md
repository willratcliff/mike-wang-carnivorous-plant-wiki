Two of the best clones from the same population (originally from Liberty Co, FL) were crossed together with the goal of creating fast growing, dark red plants that produced copious pitchers.  As many of you may know, S. flava var. rubricorpora is notorious for being a slow grower and only producing one or two pitchers per plant.  
  
I took clone L (the darkest rubricorpora clone in the collection) and crossed it with my best clone, which is the fastest growing clone that maintains a solid red body under optimal conditions.  The seedlings from this resulting cross are producing their first significant pitchers, and a few have opened.  Many are so vigorous that they have tons of side shoots, even on small plants, which means each clump will be able to produce many pitchers instead of your typical one or two per plant.  So far, they're turning out to be really nice!

`post #7` · `id=2657` · `2013-05-07 16:03:49 UTC`

Thanks Paul!  A lot of people are cautious about acquiring S. flava var. rubricorpora because a lot of the times, the plants they get are very green and barely resemble a rubricorpora.  People tend to think the plant they got isn't the real deal (and unfortunately, a lot of the times, it's not the real deal!).    Nevertheless, it's important to understand the environment plays a huge role in the production of red pigments.  If you look carefully, there are quite a few plants in the photos above that are only just starting to turn red.  Many of them were very green with a little bit of red a few weeks ago, and they looked dubious... but now as the pitcher is aging, the red is filling in. There were a few green ones here and there, but the majority were solid red bodied individuals.  BTW, the genetics of your mother plants play a huge role in the percentage of offspring that breed true to type.  
  
  Every last one of these plants photographed above were recently transplanted this year, but not divided.  Typically, people report their plants will turn green when repotting, but I've found if you follow some strict rules, you can still transplant them without having the plant turn green on you.  When you cut up the rhizome, or damage the roots, they tend to turn green, but if you don't damage the roots and don't cut up the plant, they can stay red.  Another thing to mention is light intensity and purity of water-you need very bright light to get these colors, and your water needs to be very pure.  Last year, I had water that was over 110ppm for several months of the grow season (here in the bay area, city water fluctuates in quality during the winter), and this (I suspect) caused them to all green up.  I had to get a DI filter this past winter in order to get them to color up like this!  To give you perspective, not a single one of these plants were solid red last year!

#### *kiwiearl*
`post #10` · `id=2923` · `2013-06-02 22:16:40 UTC`

Lovely presentation of rubricorpora. This last photo goes to show how variable the variety is. What you created in your cross pollination of clones grown from wild parents (a replication of a natural circumstance) delivered up the results seen in the wild - many different expressions of the alleles within the parents. In the field one has to be very careful when considering what is and what isn't a rubricorpora (that goes triple for cultivated plants!). Careful in the sense that in a stand they may well all be (aside from those that may clearly be intergrades with adjacent rugelii) just expressing their heritage differently as in the case of the batch shown here.   
  
Growth pattern (as you've pointed out previously Mike) ie, very few pitchers from very few rhizome points, is a major signature of the variety that almost transcends colour in defining rubricorpora while not being so obvious at first glance compared to that highly engaging flashy colour. Certainly the growth pattern is very distinct by definition from the other flava varieties, particularly var. atropurpurea. In the case of the later, in my view not enough consideration of that factor is given when rubricorpora presenting from one end of the variety's colour expression spectrum are enthusiastically misidentified as atropurpurea based solely on the fact they present as full infused with red (more often than not tending to purple).
