In many ways, this site reminds me of the S. leucophylla habitat in Franklin Co, FL in the sense that the habitat has been drastically altered, yet it's still habitable for carnivorous plants.  It's also very similar in the sense that these are just relic plants that escaped a once larger field that is now a pine plantation.  These populations are significant because there are very few populations left in the area, and they may also be useful for understanding the genetic makeup of the populations in Liberty Co, FL.   
  
Can the genetics of the BAy Co, FL leucophyllas help us determine the origin of the eastern most locations of S. leucophylla?  Many state with certainty that the S. leucophyllas in Liberty Co, FL were introduced, much like the venus fly traps, but it seems like these leucophyllas are genetically unique.  That doesn't mean they weren't introduced, but it does raise questions in my mind.  I also wonder if the Franklin co, FL leucophyllas were introduced, or are they native?  How did they get there?    
  
It's also very intriguing that these Bay Co, FL leucophyllas are strikingly similar in appearance to the franklin co FL plants!  Red variants are less common in nature, but perhaps they were more common in these sites prior to being destroyed.

Mike:  I am not sure where your Franklin Co, S leucophylla site is located.  Many years ago (2001/2) I located a population that is still in existence in Tate's Hell Swamp (now State Forest) in Franklin County that still exists and is in process of full environmental restoration.  Or, at least the genetics of these plants are benefitting from the hydrology restoration of the swamp and the elimination (finally) of irresponsible timber management practices that have plaqued the eastern U.S. for the past 100+ years.  
  
This population is found absolutely associated with a riparian system (historically mapped and currently mapped) that makes me confident that this population is 100% undeniably native in the region.  My experience with S leucophylla (as I believe is yours) is that this species throughout its range, just as S rubra, is obligately riparian in all of it's known native occurrences.  Many appear to be drainage specific sometimes occurring in one drainage of a large creek or river and completely absent from an adjacent drainage within distances of less than a mile.  this appears to be particularly true in the easternmost boundaries of it's range (eastern FL panhandle and Georgia).  
  
I also know of one poulation in eastern Liberty County (not Hosford bog) that I suspect is native based on association with a riparian system -  **but** - it is at a location that human seed dispersal **could** have happened.  More exploration of this water system will likely prove or refute this observation in time.  
  
So, my thoughts of the criteria for determining if a population of S leucophylla is native with today's heavily fragmented ecology is to ask the question of whether the plants appear to be distributed such that they are or once were attached to a closeby native stream or river.  If so, i think it is likely the plants are native.   In the case of Hosford Bog, casual observation would indicate that these plants do not have and may never have had such association in their current location.  
  
Just some thoughts.

Thank you for sharing your experiences and insight!  It's definitely great to know that there are sites in the area that are moving in the right direction!  your conjectures are solid  and without a doubt, I think you're right.  Seeds float on  water, so perhaps one of the main driving mechanisms of seed dispersal is when there are heavy floods and lots of water moves from one spot to another.  Seeds likely get distributed to all sorts of habitat,  but the nutrient deficient riparian areas are the only places they succeed.  The chances of germination are low, but if you think of all the seeds produced every year, year after year, the chances of dispersal are actually quite high over long periods of time.  
  
What baffles me is if water is the main source of seed dispersal, how do these plants end uphill?  Do the seeds' hydrophobic properties somehow repel them like a magnet, thereby allowing them to travel upstream?  I wonder how much of an effect wind, tropical storms, hurricanes, and tornadoes had on the historic distribution of Sarracenia?  Also, it seems equally possible that birds and other animals may have had some seeds stuck to them, and as they traveled from one riparan zone to the next, so did the seeds.    Animal dispersal of Sarracenia seeds seems less likely compared to animal dispersal of darlingtonia seeds, which are designed like velcro to stick onto animals.  However, I don't discount animal dispersal of Sarracenia seeds, especially in light of the 100's of thousands of years these plants have been been around.  Probability over time makes it almost certain yet likely infrequent.  
  
Per Sunbelle's experience and knowledge, it's safe to say the Hosford site has been around for a long time and was recently altered within the past century or so. Botanical distribution: a really interesting subject and discussion.  
  
Certainly waterfowl are a more than significant vector for new establishment and range increase of wetland plant species. Not too hard to imagine many thousands of Sarracenia seed have been and continue to be spread amongst material transported by fowl moving from one site that suits both them and the material caught up in their feet, feathers, bills, etc to another. Not sure if Sarracenia seed survives a journey through the gut of waterfowl but potentially this is another possibility.  
  
Although another genus altogether, I have seen a outcome where just this has happened. A man-made pond on a farm I frequent became inundated with *Utricularia gibba* (listed as an unwanted exotic organism here in NZ) in short order. The pond is well up the side of a hill with no flowing/flooding streams anywhere around. Under gravity water fills it simply from flowing from the contours of the landscape. One year (first photo) there was no sign whatsoever of it. The next year, well, see for yourself. There was no known population of *U. gibba* anywhere local to the pond. Same applies to the water lily in the photo that became established too in the same way as did the vigorous species of floating water weed that can be seen.  
  
It was one of the most explicit examples I had seen of what could only have been due to waterfowl (most likely Mallards in this case). Fascinatingly, the Utric only survived for the one summer and then disappeared altogether.

Earl The New Jersey Pinelands National Reserve contains approximately 1,100,000 acres (4,500 km2) of land, and occupies 22% of New Jersey's land area, including territory of much of seven counties.  This is just the reserve, the whole ecological zone (including surrounding towns and developed and undeveloped land) is very roughly 5000 square miles, 125 miles N/S by 40 miles E/W, about half the state. The maximum elevation is about 100 feet above sea level. A large part of the pinelands is protected by state and federal legislation. The New Jersey Pinelands Commission is an organization that oversees the management of the outer, Protection Area of the pinelands, and the inner, Preservation Area. [map 1](http://economyleague.org/files/pinebarrens_lg.jpg)  and [map 2](http://www.usgennet.org/usa/nj/county/atlantic/Pinelands/images/pinelands.gif)
