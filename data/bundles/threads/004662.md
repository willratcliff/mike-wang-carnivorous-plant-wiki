Some of you were probably thinking "ya right, I don't buy your chameleon clone theory mike wang!" Okay, well now I have a clone to present that hasn't EVER colored up for me until this year!  I've had this plant for many, many years, and it looked like a normal leucophylla year after year.  What was different this year?  The level of neglect to this plant was a bit more severe: early in the season, this pot dried up and several pots next to it died from dehydration, but for whatever reason, this plant survived.  There's maybe 40% of the soil remaining from degradation and lack of repotting for a good 9 years or so!  about 2 months ago, the plant looked horrible: there were tiny summer traps and very yellow phyllodia.  Fast forward until now, it shot out quiet a few strange looking traps, one of which was this jaw dropping white trap!

Very interesting, meizz; that's about as extreme as I've seen in a leuco, but it makes sense. Lets look at some variables.  
  
1. How did the sunlight and heat levels this year compare with most? E.g.: Can very high light be a factor?  
  
2. This could be partly due to nutrient deficiency and timing. Roots die back; soil is depleted, lack of water exacerbates inability to uptake nutrients.  
  
3. Both of the above, combined, create an extreme need for the plant to regain nutrients, especially nitrogen, which is critical not only for growth, but to dispose of photosynthetic wastes, byproducts. Perhaps the plants react by activating a dormant gene(s) in order to attract more insects in a very competitive (natural) environment, needing to stand out amongst what used to be thousands or millions of "neighbors". The extreme white glows more both at day and night, especially to an insect's vision, producing a competitive advantage.  
  
The combination of 1 and 2 above might trigger a growth change which would otherwise be detrimental-extreme white means less photosynthesis, but this deficit is offset by the change of replenishing low tissue nitrogen, which can be worse.  
  
If this theory has merit, it may be possible to induce what is a natural response and get leuco's. their whitest. We see it in the field and we see changes in what we grow. I just got some HCW from you and Calen. Potted in generously-sized pots and capturing their fill of insects this spring, they are not nearly as white as I've seen, for the exact clones. I have a HCW from John Hummer, but it has never been super white. It's a clone which, I believe, is not in circulation; I may be the only grower. It's got the fat mouth and look of HCWs, and has decent white, but nothing special. It may be time to experiment!  
  
Now, how to set this sort of test up: First, dehydration shouldn't be necessary if the water is relatively nutrient-free. Secondly, the soil/media should be leached, older and nutrient-poor. Third, some plants should be put in a higher light position. In this group, some should either have spring pitchers cut off or have them plugged as soon as they open to prevent insect capture. I'd vote, expect the latter to be better as it lowers photosynthetic loss while not satisfying a need for nitrogen. Differences in morphology and color may give credence or debunk this theory. However, results may take years to appear. Those rhizomes can store all kinds of things, including endosymbionts!  
  
Bill Scholl has noticed very long "windows" areoles in some S. minor plants after burns in the field. While liberating potassium and other nutrients, one would expect a nitrogen loss. These "areolata elongata" clones revert to normal-looking S. minor when put into cultivation. There may be a parallel.  
  
The trick, if this sort of approach works, is to get the results, but not kill the plant in the process!

Temperature may be a factor, especially when the pitcher is forming. This brings up another issue: the ultimate pitcher characteristics largely depend on what's going on as the pitcher is growing or perhaps right before pitcher emergence. Once the pitcher has formed, the photosynthetic layout probably doesn't change much; it's set at the cellular level. Pigments, like red, can change, being a mask for photosynthetic cells; I'm not sure you'd see the dramatic whitening after the pitcher has matured fully and hardened up.  
  
From a logical perspective, which may be wrong, more white should correlate with the need for more nitrogen. Your observation about field variations, from year to year, may support this. Rainwater, especially during thunderstorms, contains nitrogen. Some years, a lot of rain and/or spring,early-season insect capture might cause a morphological shift. In dry years, or when insect capture is poor, the plant's need to amp up fall nitrogen intake so they'll have plenty in storage for flowering (genetic distribution) the next season.  
  
In the southeastern USA, it's often the late-season sun which is the hottest, from a PAR perspective. Humidity is lower and the sun's zenith is lower, striking the pitchers on their sides, rather than from above. Maybe there's a relationship with this and pitcher emergence timing.  
  
Meizzwang, it'd be good to mark the super white plants and date this phenomenon. It'd be wild if there's a biological "clock" and these plants do this on some sort of cycle! Meanwhile, I have some leuco's. which might be in for some nitrogen-based experiments.   
Something is flipping a genetic switch and inquiring minds want to know!
