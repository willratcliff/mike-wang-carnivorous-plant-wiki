`post #5` · `id=39060` · `2018-11-07 13:40:15 UTC`

Well Meizzwang, again, our interests cross! Tea, the best tea, has its roots in China. Yeah, there are Indian teas and teas from other countries, but the Chinese are, and have been historically, the tea masters of the world. When the Brits. got kicked out of China, they tried to plant the same varieties in India, but they failed to adapt to that climate and soil. So, a different subspecies/species was utilized.  
  
Chinese teas are usually from C. sinensis (makes sense), whereas Indian teas are usually C. assamica (Assam). C. assamica has broader leaves. C. assamica, to me, has a harsher, more tannic flavor, even when cured, than the smoother tasting C. sinensis. I'm kind of a tea freak and get lots of different teas from Upton Tea Imports. Hands down, I prefer Chinese teas, although Javanese and Indian teas do make a good, classic iced tea.   
  
Now, if you examine the main tea growing regions of China, Guanxi, Guandong, Fujian, you'll see they're pretty warm, somewhere between subtropical and temperate. Humidity is pretty high, soil is rich and sloped land is often used to make sure the soil is draining well.  
  
Harvest seasons are generally divided into 1st pick/1st flush, 2nd, and other date and/or sequence-related descriptors. A lot of variation exists as to when and what gets picked, all effects flavor. Typically, the tip bud and lower leaf or two gets plucked. After that, the treatment varies wildly.  
  
Green and white teas (mostly buds) are not fermented or cured, pretty mild and straightforward. Some are pan heated, before packaging or brewing. India and Japan produce some excellent white teas. Methods of fermenting, slow drying/curing for Black or red teas, are often a trade secrets. These processes make, in my mind, some of the finest teas in the world. Tie Guan Yin and Zao Bei Jian Congou being good examples of tea with an incredibly smooth mouth feel, i.e. not too tannic, with very complex flavors, often with caramel in the flavor profile. You need leaf piles to make these!  
  
Lapsang Souchong, especially if you can get the "Imperial grade", is smoked and has a rich, unique "bacon"-like flavor. Good for cold weather enjoyment, on its own. It's worth trying, if you've never had it. Too strong to have with most foods. Although, my dad put some in mediocre Scotch to add an expensive tasting smokiness to it!  
  
The most heavily fermented, aged tea is not to everyone's liking: Pu-Erh. Here the tea gets packed slightly damp, usually into disks, to ferment slowly and pick up a mushroomy flavor that suggests fungi are involved. Surprisingly, it retains a decent caffeine kick. I like it, but not every day.  
  
I try samples of Indian teas and teas from other areas, always coming back to Chinese teas for my main purchases. Americans are not usually familiar with Chinese teas, since Lipton mass marketed Indian teas and saturation-bombed the US and much of Europe, with these types. Until fairly recently, many Chinese teas, except "finings"=dusty bags of old Oolong, were the only teas available here.  
  
I'd encourage anyone into drinking tea to check out [Upton Tea Importers](http://uptontea.com). They even have "sample sizes", so you can try a bit for cheap. Don't get tea bags! That usually has finings, the lowest grade of tea. Get a cheap, reusable tea ball and whole leaf teas. Pay close attention to brew times! Usually 2-4 minutes.   
  
Also, let the tea cool a bit before you drink it. Years ago, increased risk of esophageal cancer was attributed to folks who drank a lot of tea without milk. At first, it was thought that the milk bound tannins and made tea safer. Then, the same throat cancers were noticed in folks who routinely drank any very hot beverages, like coffee. Later, it was determined that the overly hot fluids were damaging the throat and contributing to the risk. The milk, associated in the first study, was merely cooling the beverage.  
  
You could experiment with slow curing if you set up a way for fresh tea to dry slowly, without rotting. It'll probably involve a lot of turning, mixing. Or, just make green tea and buy the fancier types, if you want. It's a bit too cold here, or I'd be trying to grow a bit. Don't think -5 degrees is tea friendly.

To echo what bogman said, C. sinensis sinensis is what the Chinese grow. It's one of those plant names that's sometimes elevated to full species, like Acer drummondii (Acer rubrum var. drummondii), Sarcococca humilis (S. hookeriana var. humilis), Juniperus silicola (J. virginiana var. silicola), Drosera multifida (D. binata var. multifida) etc. So C. sinensis var. assamica and C. assamica are both correct depending on the taxonomy you recognize.

alexintx, yes, those regions are on the SE part of China, with a climate buffered in large part by the South China Sea. It is milder than the SE USA, not usually as hot (90F is high) and seldom, if ever, freezing (40s-50s in winter). There are colder growing areas, especially as altitude goes up, but figure it's mostly tropical to cool subtropical.  Coupled with decent humidity, which hovers around 50% rH, higher in many areas, it's not the easiest climate to duplicate, here in the US.  
  
In China, most is harvested during rather cool weather. The "Pre Qing Ming", which is harvested as dormancy breaks in early April, are considered the highest quality. Three or four major harvests occur, until about mid May. Rains during this period are not that heavy, compared to the July-Sept. period. Of course, picking can occur anytime until November, but these later flushes are generally lower quality unless they have been specially handled.  
  
I've only sampled tea, grown in the US, from a Carolina plantation, and it was pretty bad, harsh and tannic. I would guess that's more a reflection of how the harvest & post harvest was handled.  
  
The Chinese have thousands of years' experience with post-harvest treatments. Small details, like rolling the leaves into "needles" or twists, makes a difference in curing and final flavor. In large part, oxidation is being controlled. Cultivars have been bred and chosen for centuries. Empires, Dynasties and governments have all changed, but tea growing has remained a constant. With each sip of tea, you're taking in a bit of history.

There be a lot of curiosity around this place, leads to learning.  Oh yeah, back to tea...  
  
The Chinese do utilize C. assamica, which could be C. sinensis var. assamica, C.s. ssp. assamica, C.s. cv. assamica, C.s. forma assamica-you get the picture! The Pu-Erh (Puer, et. al.) and some other types are made from assamica ("big leaf"). A lot of the assamica is grown in Yunnan, west of the other southern growing areas. Being closer to India, these cultivars may explain why assamica performs better in India.  
  
For those of you wanting to make your own from scratch, the primary steps include:  
•Plucking, spring buds and one to 2-3 leaves down.  
•Wilting or "withering", which happens soon after picking. Leaves are allowed to lose some excess moisture.  
•Oxidizing (not so much for green and white teas). This is where tearing, twisting, tumbling, etc. bruises the cells and they start to  change chemically, creating new flavors. Some tea is aged and/or "fermented" (really fungus culture). Can include smoking.  
•Fixing, basically slowing down or stopping the above oxidation. If I recall, it's heating various ways to about 150 degrees F.  
•Drying, to preserve the tea, most will drop the moisture level to 3% or less. Smoked teas are sometimes staged at different levels  in a chimney-like structure, with drying closest to the fire. Ovens, sunlight and dehydrators are also used.  
•Grading, sorting particle sizes and shapes, "fannings" being lowest grade and large or broken leaf a higher grade.  
•Storage-keep out of sun, cool-cold and in containers with little air space (less oxidation). Vacuum-sealing and oxygen absorbers are  good for long-term storage. I like to get a quantity/bulk, vacuum and O2 absorb most of it for later, and use from a smaller jar.

More interesting and/or useful info for you tea growers:  
  
According to Tony Gebely, a well respected tea guru, tea grows best near 65-77 degrees F and 75%-85% humidity. Soil should be sandy loam, high in organic matter and the pH in the range of 5-5.8. This last data point is surprising, as it's about where Nepenthes like pH. Those of you having chlorosis issues should take note, and add some peat moss to the soil, if your pH is too high. I'm not sure if sulfur would be toxic or affect flavor! mmm... "Rotten Egg Style"!  
  
Shading tea plants for several weeks increases the amount of amino acids. This raises the umami and "brothiness". If you want to use this shading method, plan on starting it 3-4 weeks before plucking, in the early spring.  
  
Gebely's book is pricey, unless you find it used or discounted. But, "if knowledge had a price, no one could afford it". In the book, you'll find lots of cool info: cultivars to seek out, processing, chemistry and more. If you want to make Tibetan Yak butter Tea, you'll need Kang Zhuan tea. Got some Ya Shi Xiang plants? That's the "Duck Shit Fragrance" cultivar of Dan Cong Woolong (Oolong), a very famous cultivar! grim… (Personally, I'd opt for the cultivar: Xing Ren Xiang- "Almond Fragrance", if I were planting stock.) Anyway, the book is titled: "Tea A User's Guide"  
  
Enjoy your Cha (tea)!
