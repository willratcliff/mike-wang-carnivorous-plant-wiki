Interestingly enough, this site experienced a drastic flood earlier this year, but the plants overall seemed to have been unaffected.  One minute, it'll be dry, 30 minutes to an hour later, the roads are half a foot under water!  These floods are likely one means by which seeds can be dispersed from one site to another, since the seeds float on the top of water.  In today's world, there are very limited number of new sites that Sarracenia can colonize since everything is pretty much developed. A drainage ditch is a Sarrcenia's best bet at starting a new population.  
  
 The deep south has some extreme weather: in the winter, I've seen reports of the temperatures dropping to 5 degrees F at night, and then a few days later, it'll be in the low 70's during the day!  The temperatures will swing drastically like this during the winter and early spring, and then as soon as early summer hits, it's like you're in the tropics!    
  
With all that in mind, it's hard to understand how these plants below seemed unscathed from the harsh and unpredictable weather they've experienced.  There's a lot of genetic diversity in this field, and something that caught my eye was this dark red S. leucophylla:
