Some of you may wonder, what do the parents  of all those amazing wilkerson mooreis  look like?  Well, I don't really know, but for sure, this clone below is a cousin or at least relative of all those great cultivars!  Hint-hint: this clone and all leucophyllas from the wilkerson bogs have incredible potential for breeding mooreis.    
  
Spring pitchers are surprisingly large on this leucophylla...normally, S. leucophylla in general produces weak, tiny spring pitchers outdoors in Northern California.  However, after growing so many more S. lecuophylla clones from various locations, I'm starting to see that genetics likely plays a big role in that as well.  Some populations produce nice spring pitchers even if we have a cold spring (ie. the wilkerson leucophyllas, washington Co leucos, and leucophyllas from northern baldwin Co, AL).  I'm sure there's other location specific leucos. that produce great spring pitchers even with colder weather.  Such characteristics are a huge benefit when producing quality hybrids, and I highly encourage those who want to produce leucophylla hybrids to use "cold tolerant" clones.  
  
  
S. leucophylla Bud Wilkerson's bog, N. Walton Co, FL is a strong, vigorous grower and plants from this population have a somewhat unique "look" to them.  They closely resemble some plants seen in Okaloosa Co, FL, but again, I would generalize that all leucos from okaloosa co, FL look similar because there's a lot of diversity in that area.  
  
  
Anyhow, if the pitchers look this nice during the spring, I can't wait to see what they look like during the fall!  Here's S. lecuophylla Bud Wilkerson's Bog, N. Walton Co, AL clone A, photos taken 4/1/15:

Can't wait to see the fall pitchers - keep us posted Mike. Very interesting how much variation there is in the propensity for strong spring pitcher production in leucos in cultivation. To back Mike up on this, Wilkerson's Red Rocket, obviously a plant from the same area, is known for it's exceptional spring pitchers. Mine has a pair of traps right now that stand just shy of 33 inches. In contrast my leuco "Alabama" looks like it might produce no spring traps whatsoever! Seeing leucos in the wild last June I was astonished at how large and strong the traps were. My overall impression was that wild spring pitchers were as good as most cultivated fall pitchers. But if you look at temperature averages for leuco country you'll see that days in the 80s and nights in the high 60s are typical for the time when spring pitchers are growing - far warmer than those we experience here in Cali and most other regions where people grow leucos. [DirtyDivisions](/user/168) might have it made, but I guess I'll have to count on the "cold tolerant" clones
