Tomato flavor has a lot to do with: Water supply, Calcium, variety, day temperature, exactly when it's picked, whether or not you use diluted sea water to irrigate(?!=true), solar flux, night temperature, amount of leaf cover, soil type and a gazillion variables. hcarlton: solanum chilense, Solanum lycopersicoides and Solanum peruvianum all have some populations with black fruit, but not all individuals from the aforementioned species are black fruited.  I'll find out in a few months, but many times, the individuals that produce anthocyanins on their stems during cold weather tend to have anthocyanin production in their fruit as well.    
  
A scientist at Oregon State University produced the very first black tomato ever, but the claim is that they used Solanum chilense and crossed it to a Solanum cheesmaniae (galapagos wild tomato) that carries the genes to express anytocyanin in the fruit.  There's lots of claims that S. chilense won't easily hybridize with the cultivated tomato and a "bridge" hybrid is needed in order to get those chilense genes into cultivated tomatoes.  They have all kinds of technical genetic explanations and tests to "prove" why things don't work directly, but after reading a technical paper on leucophylla population genetics, it's clear to me that plant genetics aren't always so black and white.  In other words, what they conclude in a scientific paper isn't always universal: there's almost always exceptions to the rule, and our job as enthusiasts is to figure that out!    Well, I'm doing a bunch of crosses and will find out what the real deal is.  The biggest barrier is probably the amount of time it takes to do the crosses, and how difficult it is to work with such tiny flowers (ie. even if you have perfect vision, it's hard to tell if the pollen in fact covered the stigma...everything is so tiny!).    Fortunately, I'm driven by fascination and passion, not by deadlines and pressure to get published in scientific journals.

Tomatophile Mike, Amazing how many side projects and hobbies you're juggling! Bet you dream of Bananas, with Wasabi-flavored leaves, or tomato-bearing Wasabia. 

![8-)](//storage.proboards.com/forum/images/smiley/cool.png) [not mirrored]

   
  
I've spent a lot of time around Italians, who have been passionate about tomatoes ever since they were brought to Europe. When everyone in the US thought tomatoes were toxic, the Italians were breeding. Now, Americans, Italians and Russians seem to be churning out the best flavored types in quantity. In Italy, brackish water has been used to irrigate tomatoes. A halophyte, Agretti (Salsola soda), has been used to remove salt from sea water. this provides an edible green and lowers the sodium levels enough for tomatoes. The region in Italy using lower sodium sea water has the reputation for producing the best tasting tomatoes in Italy.  
  
Taste, of course, depends on what a person likes. Many like sweet tomatoes. I like the more acidic, full-flavored types a tad more than the sweet ones (my Italian genes). Many of the Russian types have insane production, with complex, almost smoky flavors. The L. cheesemaniae I grew a couple years back defied blight. Branches would die with blight and new, productive ones would sprout, bear fruit, get blight, and new growth popped out again! Wild seedlings kept coming up for years.  
  
A good friend of mine, the late Alberto Vazquez, helped introduce L. pimpinellifolium, the Currant Tomato, to the US. He had a cultivar "Alberto Shatters" named after him. It's from Alberto that I got "Bilera", one of the best cooking tomatoes ever, and a very good fresh eating variety.  
  
Many of the new varieties with low acid and lots of seeds are less desirable for processing. Acidic tomatoes barely fall into the "safe" category for water bath canning. Many types now need a citric acid boost to be safe. Long, pressure-cooked tomatoes are not appealing! Seeds, if cooked for long periods, can ruin a sauce; I've done it. So, the seedy ones are problematic due to the volume of seeds when straining. I use a Squeezo strainer, after a brief cook to kill enzymes, to separate seeds and skin from pulp. Crank the handle and it's like magic! You can plow through 5 gallons of tomatoes in very little time, no peeling, no seeds; the puree is ready for gazpacho, Bloody Marys, or sauce-making.  
  
Thanks for recommending Candy Sweet Icicle; gotta try that one.  
  
The WORST tasting tomato relative I tried was the Litchi Tomato (S. sisymbriifolium); what a spiny, poor tasting, seedy pos! Plus, it's apt to be invasive, as the seeds kept coming up for years, in a climate with subfreezing, near zero degree winters. Evil kitty.  
  
Criminy, my Italian tomatoes are already 6 feet high Those old varieties, along with a lot of Russian ones, get HUGE.

Dark Galaxy is beauteous. If seed can be found, and it's open pollenated,(OP), I'd love to try it. When mixed with other foods, like a burger, BLT, etc. the acidity can be a plus and is diminished by the combos. In a salad, with oil and vinegar, might be too much.  
  
Here's the Bilera, all but extinct:  

![Bilera Tomato](../../images/b3/b39dce6ec19907e54f084bb18388057640ed5a7f1bd8825f343af226b3e394f0.jpg)

  
  
The fruit has very few seeds, rich flavor and qualities favorable for processing and large-scale growing. For canning, it's easy to slide the skins off after cutting the top off. The fruits are picked when half to 3/4 red, usually, and after-ripened in dark boxes to achieve full flavor. This allows you to transport crates from the field without bruising. Round tomatoes don't stack, in crates/boxes very well. The round shape makes limited pressure contact points and they bruise. The long shape is ideal for canning, packing into jars. The juice and sauce quality are outstanding. It has a lot of pulp, with relatively little gel-associated with seeds. Yet, it's not bland and mealy like the majority of "paste" tomatoes. A local grower supplied a "foodie" store in Charlottesville, VA, Alberto had a hand in that. Despite a large selection of other, local heirlooms, this one sold out after the shop offered taste samples. It's odd shape caused a certain prejudice, which vanished upon tasting.  
  
There are quite a few similar looking varieties: Jersey Devil, Opalka and more. So far, none have compared to Bilera, although Opalka had a terrible year (unfair to judge) and I'm growing it again.  
  
Like many OP varieties, Bilera has variation. I try to keep the cultivar a bit plastic by growing about 50 per grow-out season, with no other tomato in the area. I usually mix seed crop years, too. Small flower bees mix up the genes within the variety and I select a bunch of fruits from the best tasting, healthiest plants, which often get 7 feet tall, or more.  
  
The quest for flavor never ends.

If there's one thing i'm just as obsessed with as red flavas, it's gotta be tomatoes!  While the pacific northwest has a wonderful climate that make my sarracenia more than happy, its not exactly known as a prime location for growing tomatoes. And since i'm a sucker for tomato sandwiches and BLT's like nobody else, and someone who really likes a challenge, I decided to figure out how I could consistently grow big heirlooms out here with great flavor.  
  
Last year, I grew 12 different varieties all hydroponically in the greenhouse. They grew good, real good and HUGE - hitting the top of the greenhouse (11 feet!) before I trimmed them back a bit. They were so thick you couldn't see a person standing across from the row! The flavor was pretty good, better than anything I could buy locally, but I knew I could do better. So I decided to try growing the same varieties multiple ways to validate the flavors I thought I was tasting. In the ground, in grow bags with my own soil mix, and again hydroponically. The hope is that i'll be able to enjoy the same variety of tomato grown in very similar weather conditions, but different soil conditions. My real goal being dialing in exactly what I need to do to make my hydro tomatoes taste as good or better than traditional soil grown tomatoes. Hydro has so many advantages, but one really needs to dial in the flavor.  
  
  
This year i'm growing 49 different varieties of tomatoes spread across close to 300 plants. All full sized heirlooms from white, to blue to black and everything in between. Aside from canning, thought I'd mention what I'm doing with the harvest. First up, salsa! The last salsa blend had the high honor of my wife saying it was the best she ever had, anywhere. She's a foodie, so I took notice  

![8-|](//storage.proboards.com/forum/images/smiley/nerd.png) [not mirrored]

  Everything was chopped using a razor-sharp knife, less juice and more consistent chunk sizes than a processor, 'bout 1/8-1/4 inch chunks. Chopped tomatoes were put in a sieve to drain while the other ingredients are all added to taste: Jalapeños-cored, seeded, Vidalia onion, Yellow/ Orange/Red, whatever Bell pepper, Finely ground greek oregano-(careful, flavor creeps out), finely ground basil, pressed garlic, lime juice, apple cider vinegar, ground cumin, black pepper, salt, cilantro. It's important to drain the tomatoes because of the added lime juice and vinegar; you don't want it too fluid.  
  
Drying tomatoes has to be monitored so they don't oxidize and turn black. Keep the heat low, but most important is to remove them from the dehydrator as soon as they're dry and vacuum-seal them.  
  
I'll be freeze drying some, for the first time. Freeze dried tomatoes can be crushed into tomato powder. Then, seeds can be sifted out. The vacuum sealed powder, along with an oxygen absorbing packet, can allow storage for up to 25 years! Plus, it's a fast way to make sauce (no long boil time), add to chili, gazpacho, etc.. Boiling as soon as rehydrated is important; tomatoes have enzymes which cause the juice and solids to separate. Boiling denatures the enzymes.  
  
But, the bulk of them get processed by a Squeezo food mill. Made in the USA, this is the bomb for turning gallons of tomatoes into skin and seed-free juice/puree. Core tomatoes, chop into big chunks, e.g. quarters or halves and cook them briefly to denature the enzymes. When cool enough to handle. You put them in the Squeezo's funnel-shaped hopper and crank the handle. Seeds and skins come out one side, puree, the other. You can re-squeeze the seeds and skin to get the most pulp out possible. I can this juice by bringing to a boil, loading the jars and doing the boiling bath.  There are cheaper models of Squeezo imitators, for those on a budget. Even a Foley food mill will help remove seeds and skins.  
  
During the winter, when we need the humidity, the juice gets turned into a reduction sauce on the wood stove, or added to chili. In the summer, it's the base for gazpacho and Bloody Marys.  
  
Cautionary notes: I once made about 30 quarts of inedible tomato sauce by cooking them down with the seeds. After the long boil and canning, the seeds gave the sauce a weird, bitter flavor. Smaller tomatoes, with their higher seed count, are notorious for this.  
  
Many tomatoes these days are low acid and require a bit of citric acid when canning their products. Follow package directions. If this step is ignored, it's possible to get botulism from canned tomato products.

Solanum pimpinellifolium, the real deal (see pics below)!  These had location data and were offspring from wild seeds collected many decades ago (before Countries in South America made it near impossible for the United States to acquire new material to preserve long term).   
  
What's so special about this wild tomato relative?  Among the random accessions I acquired, it's Disease resistance, but there's a lot more valuable traits in the species.   If you go through enough wild genotypes, you can probably find unique flavors, but since the original specimens came from only 1-2 wild collected fruit, the odds are against that happening.  It's pretty sad, many of the collections were done so long ago, the idea of collecting seeds from a large number of individuals from each population didn't seem to be the main goal at the time (considering many accessions came from so few wild plants).  I think they were more interested in finding that one plant in the population that has "potential," whether that be a unique shape, color, size, production, growth habit, disease resistance, etc. However, they did collect seeds from countless populations, but one or a few individuals captures only a portion of the diversity.    
Over time, you can see scientists started acquiring more diversity from the populations until the early 1990's, when the South American Governments put a stop to these collection missions.  Well, they didn't stop people from collecting, but they pretty much said if the US collects any material and makes money out of it, they must share the profits with the South American Government.   It could take decades of work to turn a wild tomato relative into something profitable, and this pretty much wiped out private companies from wanting to get novel, wild genetics.  They figured they'd use the genetic material the US government has already acquired, and this is the story of many modern day tomato cultivars came to be!     
  
To give  tomato scientists credit, back in the 1950's, they were a million miles ahead of everyone at the time.  They had the vision to see that wild populations were being destroyed, and they saw the need to improve the diversity of tomato genetics in cultivation.  Much of what is preserved by the US government are relic genes that no longer exist in the wild!    These guys are the reason we have disease resistant tomato plants and tomatoes that taste better than cardboard!
